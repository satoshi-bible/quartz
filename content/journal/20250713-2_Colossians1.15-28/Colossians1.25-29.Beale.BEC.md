## C. Christ’s Supremacy over the First Creation Is a Pattern for the New Creation (1:15–23)

|  | ➤ C. | Christ’s Supremacy over the First Creation Is a Pattern for the New Creation (1:15–23) |

Here is a fuller statement of the main idea of this unit: Christ’s supremacy over the first creation is a pattern recapitulated for the new creation to bring about reconciliation of all creation, especially in order to make believers acceptable before God.

The famous so-called poem of Col. 1:15–20 has been written on by commentators more than many other texts in the NT and certainly more than any other particular passage elsewhere in this epistle. Here we are able to give only a brief analysis of the recent interpretative history of this passage and some of the main lines of past interpretation. Many believe that Paul has adopted a preexisting hymn (so, e.g., Schweizer 1982: 55–56 and literature cited there). This is typically based on the following observations about 1:15–20: (1) that verses 15–20 contain balanced rhythmic units; (2) the section could appear to stand alone and make sense apart from its context; (3) frequently repeated words, (4) non-Pauline phraseology and hapax legomena; (5) the use of literary devices such as chiasm (or inclusio or parallel or antithetically parallel expressions);[1](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (6) the unusually numerous relative pronouns; (7) the shift in pronouns: verses 13–14 are in the first person, and verses 21–23 are in the second and third person, while verses 15–20 are consistently in the third person (see Mitchell 2000: 65; Garland 1998: 82, summarizing most of these observations by others). More recently Gordley (2007: 32–39) defines Col. 1:15–20 as “an example of a hybrid of Jewish and Greco-Roman expressions of praise that can be considered a philosophical prose hymn” (2007: 39), which has been adopted by the author of Colossians (2007: 170–230).

Others are doubtful that such a hymn can be detected. If the conclusion of most scholars that Paul has alluded to such a preexisting hymn is correct, then Paul has adapted it to fit into the context of what he is writing.[2](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Since we do not have the context of the preexisting hymn against which to interpret Paul’s use, we must remain doubtful about whether it really existed as a hymn and concentrate only on how Paul is using the wording in its context.[3](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) For example, Caird (1976: 174) says that a poetic form in Col. 1:15–20 can be discerned “only by the excision of words which do not fit, on the grounds that they are a gloss by the final editor.” Though a minority viewpoint, it is possible and even probable that Paul himself created the section perhaps as a hymn or poem, since parts of his other epistles reveal that he was skilled in liturgical style.[4](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Yet whether it is a hymn or a poem or some creedal confession is difficult to know. As we investigate the purported hymn, we will see how its themes are developments of the preceding context. The so-called hymn probably includes verses 12–14 (though most doubt this)[5](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) and fits into the overall context well as part of the thanksgiving given earlier, in verse 3 (cf. also v. 12). Thus, while there is a massive amount of literature written on this supposed hymn, much of it will not be summarized here. The reason for not doing so is because, even if verses 15–20 were based on an earlier preformed hymn, no significant light is shed on the meaning of verses 15–20, since we do not know the original context and historical setting of that hymn. Thus this commentary has nothing new to offer on this debated issue, though we agree with those who are doubtful about a preformed hymn being the basis for this passage. Even if there were a preformed hymn, it would not affect our exegesis of the Colossians text.

Most directly, verses 15–20 (together with vv. 21–23) are an explanation of the directly preceding verses, that is, who Christ is as “the son of his love, in whom we have the redemption, the forgiveness of sins”* (vv. 13b–14). Recall that verses 13b–14 concerning Christ are part of verses 12b–13a concerning the Father’s work in salvation, so that all of verses 12b–14 provide the basis for “giving thanks to the Father” in verse 12a. Recall also that all of verses 12–14 are ultimately supportive of the main point in verses 3–14 of Paul’s prayer that believers “walk in a manner worthy of the Lord to please him” (v. 10a), pleasing the Lord being the climactic goal of verses 3–14. Thus, verses 15–20 (along with vv. 21–23) are also part of the ground, together with verses 12b–14, for the thanksgiving (v. 12a) and are also ultimately supportive of walking “in a manner worthy of the Lord to please him” (v. 10a). Accordingly, verses 15–23 are vital in understanding how believers are to “walk worthily to please him.” They need to know who Christ really is and what he has done for them to know who they really are and to act out of that knowledge. Verses 15–20 are also an amplification of “the kingdom of the . . . Son” (v. 13), highlighting Christ’s sovereign rule over not only the church but also all of creation (so likewise Melick 1991: 210). Verses 15–20 are treated as a new paragraph not only for literary reasons[6](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) but also because of the new subject matter. Whereas Christ was only introduced in verses 13b–14, now there is an expanded description of who he is, especially as the sovereign king over all things (picking up “the kingdom of the Son” in v. 13). We include verses 21–23 as part of a larger segment together with verses 15–20 because of its close logical connection with verses 15–20 (see on v. 21). Nevertheless, verses 21–23 are a subdivision within verses 15–23 and literarily distinct from verses 15–20.

The verses in this segment can be broadly divided into three thematic sections: (1) Christ’s supremacy over the first creation (vv. 15–17), which is the basis for (2) Christ’s supremacy over the new creation (vv. 18–20), all of which results in (3) Christ’s supremacy in reconciling sinful creation (vv. 21–23), which is the main point of verses 15–23. The emphasis on Christ’s supremacy over the cosmos is to strike an initial blow to those among the Colossians who think there are other cosmic spirit authorities who should also be appealed to in order to find spiritual “fullness.”[7](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Furthermore, it underscores that the “rescue” from the “authority of darkness” (v. 13) and the “redemption, the forgiveness of sins” (v. 14), has certainly taken place because it was accomplished by none other than Christ, who is the sovereign deity over the entire cosmos.[8](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Thus, while verses 13–14 conclude Paul’s prayer begun in 1:9, they are also transitional to the segment of 1:15–20 (so also Sumney 2008: 44, 56, 58).

Exegesis and Exposition

15He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation. 16For by Him all things were created, both in the heavens and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities—all things have been created through Him and for Him. 17He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together. 18He is also head of the body, the church; and He is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead, so that He Himself should come to have first place in everything. 19For it was the Father’s good pleasure for all the fullness to dwell in Him, 20and ⌜through Him⌝ to reconcile all things to Himself, having made peace through the blood of His cross; through Him, I say, whether things on earth or things in heaven.

21And although you were formerly alienated and hostile in mind, engaged in evil deeds, 22yet He has now ⌜reconciled⌝ you in His fleshly body through death, in order to present you before Him holy and blameless and beyond reproach—23if indeed you continue in the faith firmly established and steadfast, and not moved away from the hope of the gospel that you have heard, which was proclaimed in all creation under heaven, and of which I, Paul, [became] a minister.

1. Christ Is Supreme over the First Creation as the Divine Preexisting Adam-Like Being (1:15–17)

[1:15]

Paul begins with an interpretive elaboration upon the initial description of Christ in verses 13b–14 that continues all the way through verse 23. The initial ὅς (hos) has its antecedent in ᾧ (hō) in verse 14 (“in whom we have the redemption”), which refers to Christ’s redemption of his people. Christ, God’s “beloved Son” (v. 13b) and the redeemer (v. 14), is now called “the image of the invisible God” and “the firstborn of all creation.”

An Adamic background for the portrayal of Christ as the preexisting “image of God.” A number of commentators rightly understand that the reference to Christ as the “image of the invisible God” is, at least in part, an allusion to Gen. 1:27: “God created man in His own image, in the image of God He created him” (LXX: “God made man according to the image of God”; see additional notes below). Paul’s language here is virtually identical with his reference elsewhere to “man” being in “the image of God” (1 Cor. 11:7, where clear allusion is made to Gen. 1:27; e.g., on 1 Cor. 11:7, see Fee 1987: 515). A clear link to Gen. 1:26–27 in 1:15a (ὅς ἐστιν εἰκὼν τοῦ θεοῦ, hos estin eikōn tou theou) is also indicated by recognizing that precisely the same phrase “who is the image of God” (ὅς ἐστιν εἰκὼν τοῦ θεοῦ) occurs in 2 Cor. 4:4, where it focuses on Christ in his incarnate and especially glorified state as the pristine image in which the first Adam should have been.[9](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) In 2 Cor. 4:4 the “is” (ἐστίν, estin) refers to the ongoing truth of Christ in his exalted state as “the image of God,” which likely has some overlap with the idea in Col. 1:15.[10](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Paul’s thought may have been led to this Gen. 1 background, not only because of the repeated allusion to Gen. 1:28 in the preceding context (Col. 1:6, 10) but also because of the mention of “son” in verse 13.[11](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) “Sonship,” as we saw earlier, is inextricably linked to the Gen. 1 notion of God’s “image.” For example, Gen. 5:1–4 implies that Adam’s being in God’s “image” means that Adam was God’s “son”: when Adam’s son was born, Adam was said to be the “father of [a son] in his own likeness, according to his image” (so also Luke 3:38 speaks of “Adam, the son of God”). Thus image language is sonship language.

Colossians 3:10 also points to a Gen. 1:26–28 background, where the Christian is said to be “renewed . . . according to the image of the One who created him.” Here “image” is most likely connected with “image” in Col. 1:15, since it is also an allusion to Gen. 1:26–27 and the two passages are so close together. Obviously Col. 3:10 refers to something in this age and not in eternity past.[12](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Also strikingly similar to Col. 1:15 is Rom. 8:29, which echoes Gen. 1:27 (note the allusion to Gen. 3:17–19 in Rom. 8:20): Christ is both “the image of His Son” (to which believers become “conformed”) and the “firstborn.” This passage also refers to the historical Christ as being in God’s image. But does “image” in 1:15 refer to the incarnate and exalted Christ?[13](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

In contrast to Rom. 8:29; 2 Cor. 4:4; and Col. 3:10, here Col. 1:15a refers not to Christ as the “image” in his human incarnational and exalted form. Rather, as we will see in verses 15b–17, Christ’s preexistence as God’s divine “image” is the focus, though his status as the divine image still applies to his incarnational state and eternal exalted status. The reason for this widely temporal understanding is found in ἐστίν (estin, is) in verse 15a, which is a timeless or gnomic[14](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) present in that Christ is eternally and originally the “image of the invisible God” (cf. Tipton 2008: 187). This eternal status in God’s image refers to eternity past (the focus here), the incarnation and exaltation and Christ’s exalted status into eternity future. In support of this ongoing sense of Christ as the “image,” as we will see, the perfect verb in the clause “all things have been created” in 1:16b highlights a stative idea, referring to the very beginning of creation and to its continuing existence on into the new cosmos. The genitive “image of God” should be taken as an objective genitive (Jesus “imaging God”) instead of a genitive of relationship or a possessive genitive (cf. Harris 2010: 39), since the tone in the context is on Jesus reflecting God both before and in his incarnation and exaltation and into eternity future. In this respect, in Col. 1:15 Christ as the “image” includes notions of “representation of,” “similarity to,” and “likeness to” God (so Callow 2002: 43).

Early Judaism also closely associated the notions of Adam’s sonship and his being in God’s image, sometimes even referring to his being the image of the “invisible” God (see the additional notes below). Paul either independently interprets the OT notion of the Adamic image along the same lines as does Judaism or he follows the interpretative trajectory begun in earlier Judaism.[15](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Either way, he sees that Christ was in the divine image of God before creation and that he is (ἐστίν, estin, referring to a timeless truth) still in the divine image throughout redemptive history and on into eternity: the eschatological image of Christ in his resurrected estate is the manifestation of his eternal ontic status as the divine archetypal image in its eschatological significance in light of Christ’s incarnation, death, and resurrection. Christ has come in human form and accomplished that which the first Adam did not (as apparent from Col. 1:18). The incarnate Christ is the perfect ectypal image reflecting his preincarnate archetypal image.[16](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The incarnation brings something truly new in terms of Christ’s eschatological image bearing: it is not something contradictory to his divine preincarnate existence as the archetypal Adam-like image but is a manifestation of it in a mode that is conditioned by Christ’s work in redemptive history. Consequently, as the preexisting and divine image, Christ reflects in eternity past, present, and future the archetypal image that Adam and others should have reflected in an ectypal manner but did not.[17](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Accordingly, Ridderbos (1975: 73) rightly affirms that “Christ’s power and glory, already in his preexistence, are defined in categories that have been derived from his significance as the second Adam.”[18](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

In contrast, a number of commentators have concluded that Christ as the visible incarnate revelation of the invisible God appears to be the main focus of verse 15 (see also Calvin 1972: 308; Mitchell [2000: 58] cites John 1:18; 12:45; 14:9 as parallels to this idea in Col. 1:15). The incarnational revelatory emphasis is also pointed to by Col. 1:12–14, where the exodus-redemption background has been applied to God’s redemption of people through his Son; indeed, verse 15 continues to describe the “Son,” who accomplished redemption and forgiveness for his people (vv. 13–14; so also N. Wright 1992a: 109). Likewise, 1 Cor. 15:45–49 portrays Christ as possessing the heavenly image of the last Adam, which Christians will fully reflect at his final parousia. Thus, to see Col. 1:15 referring to Christ in his redemptive-historical role and primarily against the background of Genesis is a line of interpretation followed by many commentators, though a few resist it (especially the Adam background).[19](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Many might say that not to see “image” in Col. 1:15 as having the same background and redemptive-historical meaning of “image” in Rom. 8:29; 2 Cor. 4:4; and 1 Cor. 15:45–49 would seem unlikely.

But in light of the observation that verses 16–17 affirm Christ as the preexisting Creator and sustainer as the ground for verse 15, it is clear that the main thought in verse 15 is of Christ being in God’s image before creation began. This “image” is certainly described with Adamic contours from Gen. 1, but the image refers to Christ in his preexistence. Paul is speaking of the eternal relationship of the Son to the Father.[20](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Recall that ἐστίν (estin) refers to a timeless truth of Christ being in the divine image not only before creation but also in that archetypal image in his incarnation and exaltation. Christ being in the perfect Adamic image in his incarnation is but the expression of his eternal existence as the divine archetypal Adam-like image in his preexistence.

This implicit pattern of preexistent archetypal image and incarnational ectypal image appears to be present in Phil. 2:6–7, where Christ is said to have “existed in the form of God” before his incarnation, and then he “came to be in the likeness of men,”* both phrases which many commentators now view rightly to have an Adamic identification (e.g., cf. N. Wright 1992a: 56–98, esp. 57–62, 90–97). Wright declares it appropriate that Christ in his preincarnate divine state could be said to be in God’s image, since the mission of the perfect man who would represent Israel and save the world is a task that the OT also attributes to God; indeed, the glorious authority of the ideal or perfect man (in fulfillment of Gen. 1:26–28; Ps. 8; and Dan. 7:13–14) is (according to Isa. 45) thoroughly suitable for God himself (N. Wright 1992a: 95). It may well be that describing Christ’s preincarnate condition with the word “image” is a way of portraying him “as being, so to speak, a potential man” (N. Wright 1992a: 95, a conclusion Wright makes about Christ being in “the form of God” in Phil. 2:6). However, “potential man” is language that in this context is ambiguous and could be misleading, since one must ask, In what sense was Christ a “potential man” in his divine preexistence?

But if by “potential man” Wright means that Christ preexisted as the divine archetypal image that the first Adam was to reflect ectypally, then the notion is consistent with Paul’s thought. In a similar vein, Wright also mentions that just as 2 Cor. 8:9 can refer to the preexistent Messiah by his subsequent incarnate name of “Lord Jesus Christ,” so the same is the case with calling the preexistent Christ the human “image” of God, “much as we might say ‘the Queen was born in 1926’” (N. Wright 1992a: 116) or that “‘the Prime Minister studied economics at Oxford’” (N. Wright 1992a: 96). I was initially attracted to the helpfulness of these examples, though after further reflection they express a view in which Christ as the human image of God is cast back upon the preincarnate image and could potentially compromise that preexistent divine ontic existence. Before the incarnation Christ was not identified as a human but existed only as the Second Person of the Trinity, even if his divine image reflected features of the first and last Adam.

The preexistent Christ is being described in the language portraying his historical revelation; “the author is merely ascribing to Christ in protology what is true of him in eschatology” (Dunne 2011: 6–9), though this must be understood respectively as Christ preexisting in the archetypal image and coming eschatologically in the perfect incarnate ectypal image. The one who brought redemption (Col. 1:14) and reconciliation (1:20, 22) to the world is the same one who existed as a ruler in God’s divine image before the creation of the world.[21](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Paul can conceive of the manifestation of Christ’s ontic image in his economic image-bearing as incarnate Son without confusing the two.

With regard to the ontological aspect of the image, Jesus is an exact representation of God, especially in the light of Col. 1:19 and 2:9; the latter reads, “In him [Christ] dwells all the fullness of deity in bodily form” (so Harris 2010: 39). Some also think that Christ as the image of God in his incarnational form is the focus of 1:15a, since Christ is the expression of the “invisible God” (so, e.g., O’Brien 1982: 43), and his existence as God’s image before his incarnation was in an invisible sphere. But, on the other hand, Christ’s imaging of “the invisible God” does not have to be and probably is not a visible imaging of the invisible deity. Rather, as we have seen above, in the light of “glory” and “image” being synonymous in the OT and Paul, one can easily conceive that Christ as God’s image reflects the glory of God, which before creation presumably would have been a nonphysical reflection of the glory. This is similar to Heb. 1:3: Christ “is the radiance of His glory and the exact representation of His nature,” which focuses on Christ’s existence before creation (see Heb. 1:2),[22](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) though this is set in the context of the redemptive-historical revelation of Christ (see Heb. 1:1–4).

Philo’s repeated references to a nonphysical and eternal Adam-like figure and a physical Adam created after the pattern of the incorporeal Adamic-figure are a striking parallel to Col. 1:15, where Christ is portrayed as the true Adamic archetype who existed before creation. It is similarly implied in Col. 1:15 that the earthly Adam was created after the image of this preexisting invisible Adam, since the Gen. 1:27 allusion to Adamic “image” language is applied to the preexisting Christ. This is a precedent indicating that a being could be thought to be in the image of God and not have a physical, visible existence before the creation of the corporeal Adam in Gen. 1–2.

Thus, in summary, the concept of “image” here does not originate ultimately from Paul’s view of Christ as the second or last Adam in Rom. 5 or 1 Cor. 15, where Christ follows after the historical first Adam. Rather, Christ as “image” is “antecedent to the first [Adam], and in this respect the first Adam cannot be regarded as his ‘type,’ as is the case in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15.”[23](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) We think it best that Christ “in the image of God” is the original and perfect archetypal image, likeness, representation, and revelation of God, after which the human Adam was to be patterned (“in” or “according to the image,” Gen. 1:26) as the ectypal image.[24](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The first “Adam was a creaturely replica of the eternal Son of God” (Tipton 2008: 193), thus “an earthly sketch of the eternal, preexistent Son of God” (Tipton 2016). As such, “Adam manifests something of the glory of the eternal Son” (Tipton 2008: 193). Adamic “image” language from Gen. 1:27 is applied to Christ even as the preexisting invisible image, since this was the image after which the human Adam was to be created and was to reflect. In his nature, from eternity past, Jesus was “the image of God,” perfectly mirroring the life and character of his Father. Therefore, it was suitable for him later to be “the image of God” as man, since from eternity he had maintained the same relation to his Father that humanity, at the time of creation, had been designed ectypally to bear (following N. Wright 1986: 71), though no human would ever do it perfectly, as the incarnate God-man Jesus has done. Being in the essential image of the Father in his divine person was a presupposition for him becoming the ectypal representative image in his incarnate person unto the world (Eadie 1856: 45, citing Owen 1867: 78). Thus the preexistent Christ being in the perfect ontological image enabled him in his incarnate form to function perfectly in the Adamic image of God.[25](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The following chart expresses this relationship of Christ and the first Adam as both being in God’s image (this chart is from Tipton 2016):

Just as the creation of the human Adam as the son of God temporarily manifested the eternal glory of the uncreated Son of God, so also the historical resurrection of the last Adam reveals the eternal glory of the incarnate Son of God (following Tipton 2016). Christ as the resurrected Adamic image is the typological fulfillment of the first Adam’s failure to reflect the image (the B-C coordinate). Christ began to become the incarnate last Adam even in his earthly ministry (see Beale 2011: 385–429), though this is not depicted as clearly in the chart.

An Adamic background for the portrayal of Christ as “the firstborn.” In the second line of Col. 1:15, the reference to Christ as the “firstborn [πρωτότοκος, prōtotokos] of all creation” highlights further the idea that he is an Adamic figure and “Son” of God, the latter of which expands further on what it means that Christ is God’s “beloved Son” (v. 13b). Is the “firstborn of all creation [πάσης κτίσεως, pasēs ktiseōs]” to be translated “all creation” or “every creature” (KJV)? Either rendering is viable lexically, but the first is probable for three reasons: (1) The parallel “in all creation” (ἐν πάσῃ κτίσει, en pasē ktisei) in 1:23 does not mean “among every creature” but “in all creation,” because of its clear parallel with “in all the world” in 1:6 (ἐν παντὶ τῷ κόσμῳ, en panti tō kosmō; see additional notes on 1:23b). (2) Paul clearly has in mind the entire animate and inanimate creation in 1:16a (“in him was created all things in the heaven and on the earth”*), 1:16b (“all things have been created through Him and for Him”), and in 1:17 (“in Him all things hold together”). (3) Paul’s typical use of κτίσις (ktisis) refers to the animate and inanimate created “world” as a complete creation (Rom. 1:20; 8:19, 20, 21, 22, 39 [?]; 2 Cor. 5:17 [?]; Gal. 6:15 [?]), though it can sometimes mean “creature” (Rom. 1:25; 2 Cor. 5:17 [?]; Gal. 6:15 [?]).[26](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Nevertheless, the focus is probably more on animate beings, as Col. 1:16 indicates (“whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities”), though they are singled out as a part of the larger creation, since Paul wants to focus on them more later,[27](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) just as he focuses on the humans within the sphere of the whole creation in verses 6 and 23.

What precisely does “firstborn” mean? The meaning cannot be that Christ was the first of other beings that were created, since verse 16 says that the entire creation came into being by him (Bruce 1984: 59). The OT repeatedly asserts that the “firstborn” of every Israelite family gains authority by virtue of being given a double share of inheritance rights (e.g., Deut. 21:17). This notion was projected back upon the first Adam by early Judaism, since Adam was the “firstborn” of all human creation.[28](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) By a similar application, this feature of Adam as “firstborn” is projected back upon the preexistent Son, who in reality is the prototype of the first historical Adam. Christ is the “firstborn,” not only of all humanity in the new creation (see on 1:18 below) but also of “all [things in the old] creation” (1:15b).[29](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Even Israel was called God’s “firstborn” (πρωτόγονος, prōtogonos, Sir. 36:17 [36:11 LXX]) and his “very first [πρώτιστος, prōtistos] offspring” (e.g., Philo, Post. 63). Both the law of primogeniture and Israel as “firstborn” are part of the background for Paul’s expression (so Wall 1993: 67; see additional notes on Col. 1:15b for further references to Israel as “firstborn” in Judaism). The reason Israel was assigned this name was probably because the nation was given the same mandate to “rule and subdue, multiply and fill the earth” (cf. Gen. 1:28) as Adam and Noah, the latter a second Adam figure (see comments on Col. 1:6, 10 above). Thus Israel was a corporate Adam figure and thus was to accomplish the same purposes of supremacy over creation as Adam, and Col. 1:15a–b show Christ in his preincarnate state having this feature of supremacy over the first creation.

In the light of the above, it is understandable that Ps. 89 (88 LXX) refers to David and (typologically) to the coming eschatological messianic king as having God as his “Father,” being “firstborn” (πρωτότοκος, prōtotokos), and inheriting a position “higher than the kings of the earth,” with a “throne” that lasts “forever” (Ps. 89:27–29, 37 [88:28–30, 38]).[30](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The emphasis on ruling instead of on temporal priority in Psalm 89 is apparent from recalling that David was the youngest among his brothers (1 Sam. 16:11), yet in this psalm he is called “firstborn” (see Pao 2012: 95). “Firstborn” in Col. 1:15 probably includes allusion to this psalm passage.[31](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Allusion to the psalm develops the notion in the immediate context of Colossians that Christ as the preexistent Adam is the archetypal king and ruler over the creation (Gen. 1:26–28; see further Beale 2011: 30–33). There will come a time when the archetypal king will become incarnate and fulfill in history the psalm’s typological prophecy (with David pointing to the Messiah within the psalm itself).[32](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) What points further to a Ps. 89 allusion is the use of χριστός (christos) twice in the psalm (89:38, 51 [88:39, 52 LXX]) to refer to Israel and the use of the verbal form χρίω (chriō) to refer to David being “anointed” and, implicitly, to the coming Davidic seed who will be the eschatological representative king of the nation (see 89:19–28, 34–36 [88:20–29, 35–37]). Though “Christ” as a name does not occur in the poem of Col. 1:15–20, it does appear repeatedly in the nearby context (1:1–4, 7, 24, 27–28).

The subsequent reference to Christ as “before all things” (Col. 1:17a) further highlights his role as “firstborn” of all creation (1:15). The three descriptions of Christ in verses 15–17 (“image of God,” “firstborn,” “before all things”) are thus different ways of referring to Christ, the Son, as the archetypal Adamic-like figure. This highlights how the Second Person of the Trinity is what Adam was patterned after, since they were common ways of referring to the first Adam or to those who were Adam-like figures and were given the first Adam’s task to rule over the creation, whether this be Noah, the patriarchs, or the nation Israel. In this respect, “image” is superbly fitting, since in Gen. 1:26–28 it refers to Adam as reflecting God’s image, partly as the king of creation who is to rule over it (following Barth and Blanke 1994: 249–50).

And just as Adam’s temporal priority was not the main point of his purpose but contributed to his ultimate design to be a world ruler, so these names of “firstborn” and the one “before all things” in Colossians were not merely intended to indicate Christ’s temporal priority to the old creation but primarily underscored his sovereignty over it, as most commentators acknowledge.[33](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The pretemporal connotation does not indicate that Christ was the very beginning part of the creation but that he was “born before it” (rightly, according to Moule 1957: 76, in the sense of the “eternal generation of the son”), which places Christ as separate from the rest of the creation; Christ’s separateness from creation and existence before that creation are underscored by the affirmation that he is the agent of creation (and thus the Creator), which the next verse (16) underscores.

As we saw above, it is this point of world sovereignty that Ps. 89 [88 LXX] emphasizes about the “firstborn” messianic king. In particular, “I shall make him a firstborn” in Ps. 89:27 [88:28 NETS] is directly followed by the phrase “high among the kings of the earth,” showing, at the least, that the two notions are inextricably linked, if not the latter being a further explanation of the former.[34](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

As we will see in Col. 1:16, this position of authority is also grounded in the acknowledgment that Christ is the sovereign Creator of the world (v. 16, with an introductory ὅτι, hoti) and sovereignly maintains its ongoing existence (v. 17b). Therefore Christ perfectly embodies in his preexistence the ruling position that Adam and his flawed human successors should have imitated in an ectypal manner. He is, at the same time, the perfect divine Creator of all things, who is separate from and sovereign over that which he has created, especially underscored by the phrase “all things have been created through Him and for Him” at the end of 1:16 (on the latter, see Dunn 1996: 90–91).

As widely recognized, while Col. 1:15–17 refers to Christ’s sovereignty over the first creation, verses 18–20 affirm his sovereign position in the second, new creation that has been launched by his resurrection. In this respect, the identical title of “firstborn” is reapplied to Christ in verse 18 in order again to indicate Christ’s rule over the new order by virtue of his resurrection from the dead (v. 18c). His priority in the new creation entails his kingship over it (see also Heb. 1:2–5 and 2:5–9 for a relevant parallel).

As we will see, the reasons given for Christ’s position of rule in the new redemptive-historical age are stated in Col. 1:19–20: (1) Christ is the full expression of deity, amplified in 2:9, “In Him all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form”; and (2) he has inaugurated the process of bringing creation back into harmonious relation to itself and to God (i.e., “reconciling”). In verse 18 Paul portrays Christ as God and end-time Adam in the flesh in order to affirm that “Jesus fulfills the purposes which God had marked out both for himself and for humanity” (N. Wright 1986: 70–71). The design for humanity originally reaches its completion in the last Adam. The first Adam’s failure to obey God and rule under him left a gap of needed obedience for humanity to reach its eschatological completion, so that even the first Adam’s disobedience typologically pointed to another Adam’s obedience and ultimate rule. But in 1:15 Christ is the preexistent perfect Adam-like image, who is the model for the later earthly Adam and who would himself become the last Adam as a manifestation of his preexisting divine image (as v. 18 affirms). The Second Person of the Trinity in his preexistent state is being depicted in language that is like the first Adam in order to (1) set up the archetype-ectype relation of the preexisting Son to the first human Adam (the A-B coordinate in the above chart) while simultaneously (2) providing a context for the first Adam–last Adam typology (the B-C coordinate) and (3) the redemptive-historical coming of the Son as second Adam (in the A-C coordinate).

A number of commentators think that the figurative portrayal of “wisdom” in Judaism and in Prov. 8:22–27 (as “the beginning of His [God’s] way” prior to the creation, who helped bring creation about) is reflected in Col. 1:15–17. This is possible, and to some extent this may be discernible, though the background of Adam-like features applied to the preexistent Christ is uppermost in mind (see the additional notes below on 1:15–17 for elaboration of the possible wisdom background, which we will argue is related to an Adamic notion). If so, wisdom—considered to be an attribute of God, existing before creation, and helping to create—is now identified with the person of Christ.

What is the significance of portraying Christ as a divine preexistent Adam-like figure? Christ as God’s “Son” (v. 13b) and as “redeemer” (v. 14) is also to be understood as the very image of God, which Adam should have consummately and eternally reflected, but did not. Perhaps, as suggested earlier, the conceptual link between Christ as God’s “Son” (v. 13b) and the last Adam is supplied by Gen. 5:1–3, where Adam and Eve are said to have been made “in the likeness of God,” and then Adam “begot a son in his own likeness, according to his image” (NASB). Thus, to be in the “likeness and image” of someone is to be that one’s son. Consequently, in the light of Gen. 5:1–3, Adam was also a “son” of God in Gen. 1:26–27. Likewise, Christ as preexisting with Adamic features is also God’s Son yet so much so that he is divine. Christ later had to become incarnate as the perfect Adam in order to be God’s perfectly faithful human “Son” and in order to be a human representative to take sin upon himself and to accomplish redemption (so see Col. 1:13–14!). But only a divine being could be perfectly faithful in every way. Consequently, Christ became a divine incarnate last Adam in conformity with his preexisting Adam-like image as the eternal Son.[35](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Romans 8:29 refers to Christ as “the image of his Son” (in his incarnation and exaltation) and as “the firstborn” (as the first to rise from the dead), both of which refer to the Son’s relationship to redeemed humanity.

But, as we observed in verse 15, these identifications of Christ with Adam are also projected back before creation, so that now the focus is not on Christ’s incarnate relationship to humanity but on his relationship to creation (here following Fee 2007: 298). He is the perfect divine expression in eternity past of God’s image, the archetype behind God’s design for Adam to reflect God’s image in an ectypal manner and likewise for his people in the new creation to reflect God’s image. As “firstborn” Christ is also prior to and especially divine ruler over the first creation, which became the pattern for him to be the incarnate “firstborn” of the new creation in order to have “supremacy over all things” in the remaining old and emerging new creation. The pattern inherent in Christ as the preexisting divine king of the first creation was the blueprint for him to become the divine-human king of the new creation (cf. likewise Garland 1998: 100). This is part of the pattern reflecting the notion that God “makes the last things like the first things” (Barn. 6.13; see further on 1:18).

The genitival clause “firstborn of all creation” does not likely indicate a partitive genitive[36](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (i.e., Christ as the actual first part “of [among] all the creation”). This is unlikely since, as we will see in verses 16–17, Christ is portrayed as the divine Creator, which has already been intimated in verse 15a with Christ as the preexistent “image of the invisible God.” Thus Christ as the divine Creator is distinguished from “all creation” in verse 16 (Harris 2010: 40). In the light of the emphasis of “firstborn” being on “supremacy over,” the genitival phrase “firstborn of all creation” is best taken as a genitive of subordination (“firstborn over all creation”) instead of more generally as a genitive of reference (“firstborn with reference to all creation”; so Wallace 1996: 238). Besides the sovereign sense that we have seen “firstborn” to have, this genitival option is further confirmed from the following phrase in Col. 1:16a (“by Him all things were created”), which would make little sense in supporting Christ as the first of creation in only a chronological sense. Rather, Christ as the sovereign Creator (v. 16a) supports his role as the supreme sovereign “over all creation” (v. 15b, or “over every created thing”; so Robertson 1934: 772).[37](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The universal idea is supported from 1:16a by observing that the neuter plural τὰ πάντα (ta panta) is a description of “the universe,” as common in the NT, which further elaborates on the “all creation” of 1:15 (so Robertson 1934: 653–54, who observes that neuter plurals of other words are used with a collective meaning for “the sum total” of something).

That “firstborn” focuses on sovereign rule is borne out from Col. 1:18, where the same word is an explanation of “head,” “beginning,” and is further explained by “have supremacy” (see on v. 18 for the “ruling” sense of all these words).[38](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

In summary of the theological significance of the two Adam-like portrayals in Col. 1:15, two theological conclusions may be drawn. First, it is apparent that “image of the invisible God” indicates Christ’s “preexistent consubstantial relation to the Father” (Tipton 2008: 188), which the parallel in 1:19 (and 2:9) clarifies[39](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (see the table at my comments on 1:18–20 titled “Preeminence of Christ in the First Creation and in the New Creation”). Second, “firstborn of all creation” explains Christ’s preexistent relation to creation as its ruler and ultimate cause.

[1:16]

This verse gives the basis (note the introductory ὅτι, hoti) for verse 15’s affirmation that Christ “is the image of the invisible God and the firstborn of all creation.” Verse 16 now explains the reason for Christ’s preceding titles of divine preexistence, which underscore his sovereignty over the cosmos: Christ is the divine image and ruler over all things because he is the agent of all creation and as such he existed before the creation. Verse 16 makes clear that the Adamic contours of the “image of the invisible God” and “firstborn of all creation” refer not to Christ’s incarnational state but to his preexistent status. First, “all things were created by Christ,” so that he is the agent of creation. That agency is in mind is pointed to by the end of the verse, where it is clearly referred to (“through him”), as well as 1 Cor. 8:6 (“all things . . . through” Christ were created) and John 1:3 (“all things came into being through Him [Christ], and apart from Him nothing came into being that has come into being”). Yet most of the uses of ἐν (en, in) with Christ as the object of the preposition in Colossians clearly refer to the idea of sphere.[40](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) On this viable basis, D. Moo prefers the likelihood that sphere is in mind in verse 16.[41](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) In further support of this is Eph. 2:10 (“for we are his creation, having been created in Christ Jesus”*). But what would it mean for “all things” to have been created in the “sphere” of Christ? Would it suggest that his sovereign presence held sway over everything that was brought into being? It is not clear exactly what such a spheric use could mean here.[42](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Foster (2016: 182–83) has offered a viable explanation of the spheric use: Christ “constitutes the domain and locus of power in which the cosmos can be brought into existence.” He rightly concludes that one seeks too much precision by choosing only one or the other of these options and thus concludes that both notions of agency and sphere are included.

Perhaps relevant to Col. 1:16a is Eph. 2:15, “in order that he [Christ] should create the two in himself.”* Here ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō, in himself) certainly designates the sphere within which Christ created, but the fact that Christ is doing the creating shows that he is the agent of creating Jew and Gentile “in himself.” It is thus better to take the phrase in Col. 1:16a to be a thicker reference that includes Christ as both the agent of creation and the sphere in which all things were created. The translation would be something like “with respect to Christ all things were created,” but the reference would mainly be to both agency and sphere together.[43](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The idea of agency may also be more in focus if this phrase about old creation in 1:16a is a parallel with the phrase that expresses part of the new creation in verse 20 (“and through Him to reconcile all things to Himself”).[44](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) As noted on 1:15, that he is the sovereign agent of creation also further indicates that the affirmations about Christ in verse 15 focus on his supremacy over all creation. By associating Christ with an activity typically considered to be a divine prerogative or attribute, his proximity to God is highlighted (Foster 2016: 183). This is tantamount to a divine-identity Christology, whereby an activity of God is attributed to Christ.

“All things” comprises every animate and inanimate thing that has been created.[45](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Included among these created things are “the visible things and the invisible things” (τὰ ὁρατὰ καὶ τὰ ἀόρατα, ta orata kai ta aorata). There are realities in the creation that can be seen and those that cannot be seen with the naked eye.[46](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The latter is especially pointed to by the directly following enumeration of heavenly sovereign forces that cannot be seen with the human eye, so that at least part of the “heaven” in verse 16 overlaps with the “heavenlies” in Ephesians, where the evil powers of Satan reside (Eph. 1:21; 3:10; 6:11–12).

Also among all created things are “thrones,” “dominions,” “rulers,” and “authorities.” The focus is upon invisible heavenly sovereignties, though secondarily earthly rulers are in mind to the degree that they are represented by the heavenly potentates (as, e.g., in Dan. 10:20–21; 12:1; Rev. 2:1, 8, 12, 18; 3:1, 7, 14; on which, e.g., see Beale 1999a: 217–19).[47](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) But that both heavenly and earthly powers are in mind is apparent from the observation that the list of these various kinds of powers is a further explanation of the preceding “things created in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible” (Sumney 2008: 67–68). Texts in Judaism use the same kinds of expressions for the various types of angelic powers that God created (see Arnold 1995: 253–55; Garland 1998: 89). Probably evil authorities in heaven are primarily in mind (though all unseen good angels are in the peripheral vision of Paul), since the same “rulers and authorities” are mentioned in 2:10 and 15 and are malevolent there (e.g., see τὰς ἀρχὰς καὶ τὰς ἐξουσίας, tas archas kai tas exousias, in Col. 2:15 and the same nouns in 2:10).[48](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) It is obvious in 2:15 that the “rulers and authorities” are evil and unseen, but perhaps it is not so clear in 2:10. Nevertheless, we shall see that 2:10 also alludes to evil invisible powers over whom Christ is sovereign. And we shall see there that Christ’s rule over these powers is related to the Colossians’ protection from false teaching (see on 2:8–10).

Consequently, the reference to such powers in 1:16 anticipates part of Paul’s argument against the false teaching besetting the Colossians, though this reference itself has been anticipated earlier by the singular reference to “the authority of darkness”* (τῆς ἐξουσίας τοῦ σκότους, tēs exousias tou skotous) in 1:13, which confirms the malevolent nature of these beings in verse 16 (following Talbert 2007: 187). The point is, as will become more evident in 2:4, 8, and 10, that the hearers should not be deceived, because Christ is the ruler over the invisible powers that are inspiring this wrong teaching through the false teachers.

This is probably why Paul does not focus on other parts of “all” the creation in verse 16 (e.g., trees, seas, mountains, rivers, forests). Rather, he immediately focuses on the invisible authorities that were created, since he is going to say more clearly later that Christ has defeated them and is their king. Some object that the forces in 1:16 can only be good, since evil principalities were not created evil but became sinful later (Callow 2002: 45). However, verse 17 affirms that every single part of creation (“all things,” which occurs twice in v. 16) is preserved or “held together” by Christ’s sovereign power, so that even if the above objection were relevant for verse 16, Christ is still seen as absolutely sovereign even over evil angels in the light of verse 17 (see on v. 17). In 2:10, that Christ “is head over all rule and authority” gives part of the reason why the Colossians are not to be taken in by false teaching but are to hold to the truth of Christ (2:8). The point accordingly is that the hearers should not be deceived because Christ is the ruler over the invisible powers that are inspiring this teaching by the false teachers. As long as the Christians stay planted on the foundational “principles” of Christ, they will be protected from the evil “principles of the world” (2:8; see further on 2:10).

Colossians 1:16 ends as it began by repeating that “all things have been created through [διά, dia] Him.”[49](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This emphatically shows that Christ at the first creation is not the first of created reality nor in any way a part of the creation but is the means or agent of creation (cf. Thompson 2005: 32). At the end of verse 16 is the addition that “all things have been created . . . for [εἰς, eis] him.” According to this use of εἰς (eis), Christ has designed that he himself be the goal of his own creation (see Harris 2012: 88). The idea from the immediate context is that the past act of creating has effects that continue into the present; that is, the ongoing existence of the creation is the effect of his past creating activity. Thus ἔκτισται (ektistai, have been created) carries what many consider the traditional sense of the perfect tense because of the context of verse 16a, where an aorist tense refers to the initial act of creating; then the focus of the perfect tense in verse 16b is on the ongoing state of the creation.[50](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The creation must have an ongoing existence in order to accomplish its future end-time goal in Christ, which is his glorification, as Rom. 11:36 clarifies: “For from Him and through Him and to Him are all things. To Him be the glory forever. Amen.”[51](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) It is amazing to think that the one who was crucified as a criminal and offered “the body of his flesh through death”* (Col. 1:22) is the goal of the entire creation’s existence.[52](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

[1:17]

The thought of verses 15–16 continues here. In particular, this verse expands on verse 16 as the basis of and explanation for verse 15. Verse 17a further explains the divine side of Christ as “the image of the invisible God” and “the firstborn of all creation” in verse 15. The last part of verse 17 is a summary of the end of verse 16 (“All things have been created through Him and for Him”), which affirms that the ongoing existence of the creation is maintained by Christ.

The reference to Christ being “before all things” is a near-synonymous expression for “firstborn of all creation” in verse 15, where both phrases were discussed. And, just as we saw in verse 15 that the phrase includes the idea of temporal priority, so it does here.[53](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) We concluded there, however, that the expression refers to Christ as a preincarnate Adam figure, who not only was in existence before the rest of the creation, but whose temporal precedence also entails that he was sovereign over all things, since in the Gen. 1–3 context temporal priority entails rule over something.[54](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) And just as Adam’s temporal priority to the rest of human creation was not the main point of his purpose but contributed to his ultimate design to be a world ruler, so this idea is projected back onto the preexisting Christ. Psalm 89:27 (88:28 LXX) enforced this idea of world sovereignty, referring to the messianic king as “firstborn” (“I will make him a firstborn”), which is directly followed by the phrase “highest of the kings of the earth.” This indicates that the two notions of kingship and firstborn are inextricably linked and overlapping in sense. Thus Christ’s temporal priority as “firstborn of all things” entails his sovereign position over all things,[55](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) which expands upon the initial description of him as “firstborn of all creation” in Col. 1:15.

But why is the notion from verse 15b restated here? It is to explain the undergirding reason for the ongoing preservation of “all things [that] have been created” until they find their eschatological goal in Christ (v. 16b). That is, Christ’s sovereign rulership will superintend all of creation to its final goal. The present tense ἐστίν (estin) could merely refer to Christ’s “existence” (Christ “exists before all things”), or perhaps preferably, ἐστίν indicates not only that Christ was sovereign over creation at its beginning, but also that he is in control of it throughout time until the end of history.[56](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) As in verse 15, the ἐστίν is probably timeless in the sense that it includes eternity past, present, and eternity future. The truth of Christ’s sovereign existence occurs before, during, and after the creation and in eternity future.

The second half of Col. 1:17 clarifies this very point about the ongoing preservation of creation: “in Him all things hold together.”[57](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Here the ἐν (en, in) plus the dative αὐτῷ (autō, him) is ambiguous. It could connote sphere (so most translations) or agency (the latter being preferred by Douay, KJV, and HCSB), though both might be in mind. Yet since Christ as the agent of creation has been repeated twice in verse 16, it may be that this is more of the focus here. It is by Christ’s sovereign rule that creation is maintained, as it was created from the beginning.

The word συνέστηκεν (synestēken) is rendered primarily in two ways by the translations: “hold together” (NASB,[58](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) NRSV, ESV, NIV, HCSB, NJB) or “consist” (Geneva, KJV, ASV, Douay). The first option is a good translation; the latter is questionable. The verb form συνίστημι (synistēmi) is used elsewhere in Paul and numerously in the Greek OT, Philo, and the Jewish Greek pseudepigrapha. There are many ranges of meaning, but in the NT three notions stand out, especially in Paul: (1) to demonstrate something; (2) to commend someone or something; (3) to refer to God or Christ’s sovereign rule over the creation, though apart from Col. 1:17 this idea occurs only in 2 Pet. 3:5. These ranges of meaning occur also in the Greek OT, Philo, and the Jewish Greek Pseudepigrapha. Obviously the word in Col. 1:17 fits into a creation context and does not fit into the first two above ranges of meaning. For example, 2 Pet. 3:5 says, the “earth was formed out of water and by water.” Here the word is virtually synonymous with “created,”[59](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) referring to the initial creation by God. Yet the verb in creation contexts can also refer to the ongoing sustaining of the creation’s existence instead of the initial creation itself.[60](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The word in Col. 1:17 could refer to the initial creation but, like the phrase “all things have been created” in verse 16b, this one too is in the perfect tense, so that while the first act of creation could be in view, the focus is on a stative notion, the ongoing state or existence of the creation, which by implication in the context is the effect of the initial creation.[61](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Christ “holds together” the creation by his providential power. “Hold together” is a better rendering than “consist” since the clause “All things consist by Christ” is a bit awkward; yet if the ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō) is taken in a spheric manner, then it makes sense: “All things consist in him.”[62](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Furthermore, “consist” would be a rare rendering of the verb. There is little change in meaning, but since we have concluded here that the ἐν αὐτῷ focuses on agency and only secondarily connotes sphere, it is better to go with the “hold together” translation.

That “all things hold together” in Christ is a reference to the old creation that continues into the overlap of the inbreaking new creation narrated in Col. 1:18–20, and by implication it includes all things in the new creation and its consummation in the final, eternal new heavens and earth.[63](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Thus the subsection on the old creation in verses 15–17 does have overlapping ideas with the age of the new creation, which we see at the end of verse 17. We have also seen Christ as an archetypal Adam-like ruling figure in verse 15, which focuses on his preexistence but includes[64](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) within its purview redemptive history and eternity future, so that Christ as such a prototype is true before history and throughout history and onward. This means that Christ continues to be sovereign over the unseen spiritual forces of evil already mentioned as a part of creation (v. 16), which were a spiritual threat to the readers.

2. Christ Is Supreme over the New Creation (1:18–20)

Now the focus changes from Christ as the divine sovereign king over the first creation to Christ as the divine sovereign over the new creation. The thematic and linguistic parallels between verses 15–17 and verses 18–20 are striking in showing Christ’s dual divine kingship over the old and new creations:[65](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Christ’s sovereignty over the first creation in his preincarnate existence is stated first in verses 15–17, since it is the basis and blueprint for the recapitulating idea of Christ’s sovereignty in his incarnation over the second or new creation. This reflects the biblical principle that God “makes the last things like the first” (Barn. 6.13).[67](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The first things of the old cosmos point to, and thus become, the basis and pattern for the recapitulating pattern of the last things of the new cosmos, inaugurated by Christ. The main point in the comparison is that just as the preincarnate Christ was the divine sovereign over the temporary first creation, so certainly he will be the divine-human sovereign over the new, everlasting creation inaugurated by his resurrection, his defeat of evil powers (see on 2:15), the establishment of the “church,” and his “reconciling” work (1:20, 22) and consummated in the entirety of the new heavens and earth (cf. Rom. 8:18–25). Christ’s historical work of bringing about the new creation in his resurrection is not to cause us to lose sight of his preexistence before history, since the former is but the revelation of the latter.[68](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Each of the four parallel themes indicates that what Christ was before the first creation (left-column parallels) is manifested in what he became in the new redemptive-historical creation (corresponding right-column parallels). For example, (1) Christ’s being the “image of the invisible God” (v. 15) is revealed in “all the fullness [of Deity] dwell[ing] in Him” (1:19). (2) Likewise, his being “the firstborn of all creation” (v. 15) is manifested in his being “firstborn from the dead” (v. 18). (3) “He is before all things” (v. 17) is made evident in his being head/beginning, “so that He Himself will come to have first place in everything” (v. 18). (4) The reality that “by Him all things were created, both in the heavens and on earth” (v. 16) is made observable in his task “to reconcile all things, . . . whether things on earth or things in heaven” (v. 20; likewise Tipton 2008: 189).

If this is correct, then it means that verses 18–20 of the so-called poem are the main point, and the beginning of the poem (vv. 15–17) is the important supporting basis that upholds this and highlights this as the main point (see similarly Dunn 1996: 99). These four parallels above will be addressed as they are found in the order of the verses, so that the first parallel will be discussed below with verse 19.

I have followed several scholars (see below) who divide the so-called hymn into two sections: verses 15–18b and verses 18c–20. The parallel structure of the two main sections of the poem can also be discerned through a more in-depth literary analysis, which confirms also most of the parallels observed in the above chart of verses 15–20. The following is a broad chiastic version of a very viable threefold division.[69](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

The chiasm is broad only with A-Aʹ and B-Bʹ parallels and a middle C element. But within each A section are four subordinate parallels. As Wright contends, these parallels show that there is poetic structure in verses 15–20 (N. Wright 1992a: 105–6). This literary analysis supports three of the four parallels seen in the earlier chart. Rather than verse 15 (“He is the image of the invisible God”) being parallel with verse 19 (“For it was the Father’s good pleasure for all the fullness to dwell”), Wright’s more detailed literary structure instead has verse 15 being parallel with verse 18c (“who is the beginning”). This fits better with the detailed literary parallels in Wright’s analysis, but it does not make as much conceptual sense. How is Christ being “the image of the invisible God” parallel with Christ “who is the beginning”? Now, one could say that Christ as the Adam-like image of God was the beginning (i.e., ruling creator) of the first creation (as we argued earlier, on v. 15), and therefore it makes parallel sense that he is “the beginning” of the new creation. Or one could say that “image of the invisible God” and “beginning” are parallel conceptually because they both refer to attributes of “wisdom.” But it might make even better conceptual sense to see verse 15 being parallel with verse 19: Christ as “image of the invisible God” (v. 15a–b) in the first creation is the one in whom “all the fullness” of deity “dwells” in the new creation (1:19; 2:9). In both cases, Christ is the full expression of deity.

It is probably best to let this tension between the thematic and structural parallels stand and to appreciate, to one degree or another, both vantage points. As noted, the main point of the twofold thematic approach would be on verses 18–20, with verses 15–17 as the supporting basis of that main point (and within vv. 18–20, the main point is in v. 18, with vv. 19–20 being the supporting basis [“because”] of that main point). On the other hand, the main point or focus of the threefold chiastic approach would appear to be on verse 17b (“all things hold together in him”), which is the central part of the chiastic threefold structure.[70](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) In appreciating both perspectives, I see verse 17b as a highlighted transition between the two sections leading to the main point of verse 18,[71](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) though the main comparison and contrast of verses 15–20 is between verse 15 (the preincarnate Christ as divine sovereign over the first creation) and verse 18 (the incarnate Christ as divine-human sovereign over the new creation).

[1:18]

Verse 18 is part of the second and third parallels noted above (see the table titled “Preeminence of Christ in the First Creation and in the New Creation”). Part of the third parallel comes first in verse 18. Just as Christ existed “before all things” created in the first creation and was sovereign king over its continued existence (1:17), so he became in his incarnation “the head” and the “beginning” so that “he himself should come to have first place among all things” in the new creation (1:18) as the ruler of it. Christ as “head” indicates his rule over “the church” (which is his “body”). There has been much debate over whether “head” (κεφαλή, kephalē) means a position of authority or a source from which something derives.[72](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) But these positions are not necessarily mutually exclusive. The notions, for example, of the head being in control over the body and yet being the source of nourishment for the body can be complementary. An example of this may be Eph. 4:15–16: “But speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in all aspects into Him who is the head, even Christ, from whom the whole body, being fitted and held together by what every joint supplies, according to the proper working of each individual part, causes the growth of the body for the building up of itself in love.” Here Christ, as “head,” authoritatively directs the development of the body and causes its growth (so Col. 2:19).[73](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The idea of unity is probably also in mind in Eph. 4 and Col. 2. That “head” clearly means “authority over” in Ephesians is clear from 1:22–23: God “put all things in subjection under His feet, and gave Him as head over all things to the church, which is His body, the fullness of Him who fills all in all.” Here in Col. 1:18 headship is a further explanation of “all things in subjection” to Christ, so that “head” expresses his authority. Yet, that Christ also “fills all in all” is probably an echo of God filling the earth with his creation, which is not only an authoritative task but one that also sees Christ as the source of the church’s growth in the sense of being the cause of that growth. Again, here the head connected to a body suggests both authoritative direction and cause of growth, as well as unity.[74](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Likely the same thing is in mind in Eph. 5:22–24, where again the notion of “being subject to” and “head” are combined: wives are to “be subject to” husbands, which is followed by the husband being “the head of the wife, as Christ also is the head of the church.” Thus, as in Eph. 1, “head” again expresses authority, but likely the image of the husband as head of the body, his wife, is presumably a picture that is in mind. If so, then the notion of headship would suggest again one who directs and causes growth; hence the notion of source would be included secondarily,[75](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) as well as unity.

That Col. 1:18 says Christ is “the head of the body, the church” (“church” in apposition to “body”) likewise conveys the notion of his position of authority. But perhaps also included, as in Ephesians, is his position as the causative source of the church’s growth.[76](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Since there are many parallels between Colossians and Ephesians, it should not be surprising that the meaning of “head” in Colossians is like that in Ephesians. Likely included in the picture of the head-body relationship is Christ as the “one” who is the corporate ruling representative of the “many” (so O’Brien 1982: 50). Accordingly, τοῦ σώματος (tou sōmatos) is a genitive of subordination (Campbell 2013: 14): “head over the body.”

“Church” here refers likely to the so-called church universal, though, of course, the local church in Colossae would be included (this is in parallel with the same universal sense of “church” in Eph. 1:22; 3:10, 21; 5:24–32). The universal reference of the church is indicated by the saturation of universal cosmic language throughout Col. 1:15–23. Since “head” and “body” language is used, here the church is likely understood to be the corporate body of Christ (see further on the background of ἐκκλησία in the additional notes, where the church is seen to be the continuation of Israel as God’s “assembly” or “church,” ἐκκλησία, ekklēsia). As Christ is the source of the new creation, the church is “the visible locus of the renewal of creation” (Davids 2008a: 259).

The second and third parallels in the initial chart introducing Col. 1:18–20 are taken from the remainder of verse 18. Christ as the “beginning” (ἀρχή, archē), while including a reference to temporal priority, focuses more on his position of rule (for the latter usage, see BDAG 138, s.v. ἀρχή). At least partly in mind is Gen. 1:1 (“In the beginning [ἀρχή] God created the heavens and the earth”), which indicates that Christ as the “beginning” in Col. 1:18 is the commencement of the new creation. Proverbs 8:22–23 LXX also alludes to Gen. 1:1 in referring to “wisdom” (8:11–21) as “the beginning [ἀρχή] of his [God’s] ways”: “Before eternity he established me [wisdom], in the beginning [ἀρχή, archē].” Since we have seen that “wisdom” is in mind to some extent in Col. 1:15–17 above, likely both Gen. 1 and Prov. 8 are echoed here in verse 18 (see Pao 2012: 100 for these two allusions).

Ἀρχή is further explained by Christ being “the firstborn [πρωτότοκος, prōtotokos] of the dead,” and both these expressions amplify what “head” means. Just as Christ was “the firstborn of all [in the first] creation” (v. 15b), so he is the “firstborn” of the new creation through having been raised “from among the dead ones” (1:18; on Christ’s resurrection as new creation, including Col. 1:18, see Beale 2012: 298–316).

This is one of the clearest texts in the NT that Christ’s resurrection is understood to be the beginning of a new creation, with more to come (i.e., of more resurrected ones from the dead to come; on which see almost identically 1 Cor. 15:20–23). Paul views Christ’s resurrection from the dead “as a manifestation of the one great movement of God in raising his people from the dead” (Tipton 2016). Revelation 1:5 refers to Christ as “the faithful witness, the firstborn [ὁ πρωτότοκος] of the dead, and the ruler of the kings of the earth,” showing the appropriateness of “firstborn” for Christ’s resurrection and that it is inextricably linked to a ruling position, which is also an allusion to Ps. 89:27–29, 36 (88:28–30, 37 LXX). And then Rev. 1:5 is expanded in Rev. 3:14 by referring to Christ as “the faithful and true witness, the Beginning [ἡ ἀρχή] of the creation of God,” which refers not to the first creation in Gen. 1 but to the new creation, as a result of Christ’s resurrection (on Rev. 1:5 and 3:14, see Beale 1999a: 190–92, 297–303).[77](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Not only is this a new-creational[78](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) expression: it also indicates Christ’s rulership, just as we saw that Christ being firstborn of the old creation indicates his rule over it (Col. 1:17a). For example, recall that πρωτότοκος, the word also used of Christ being “firstborn of all [the old] creation” in verse 15, includes the idea of him being ruler over the first creation. In particular, we saw that just as the historical Adam’s temporal priority included the notion that a part of his ultimate design was to be a world king (as mandated in Gen. 1:28), so these names in Colossians about Christ’s temporal priority to the old creation entail his sovereignty over it (esp. as developed by Ps. 88 LXX = 89 Eng.). So πρωτότοκος indicates the same notion of authority here in Col. 1:18. The combination of ἀρχή with πρωτότοκος points to Christ being the authoritative founder of the new redeemed humanity (“firstborn of the dead”) and of the new creation in general (since the new humanity is also part of the beginning of the new creation, sparked off by Christ).[79](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This is further pointed to by the fact that when Christ rose from the dead, he achieved power over death as the last enemy of death (cf. Acts 2:24–36; Rev. 1:18), and when he ascended to the right hand of the Father, he received an escalated position of kingship, greater than during his ministry (as expressed in Col. 3:1 and more clearly in Eph. 1:20–22; cf. also Acts 2:30–36). Thus the resurrection placed Christ at God’s “right hand” and into an even greater stage of redemptive-historical kingship over death and evil heavenly rulers.

The final phrase of Col. 1:18 appears to highlight the meaning of sovereign rule even more than the preceding three clauses. Thus it may be in apposition to or a further explanation of them: “so that He Himself should come to have first place in everything.” However, this last clause of verse 18 is not a mere interpretative restatement of what precedes but more likely expresses the purpose or result of Christ being the “head,” the “beginning,” and the “firstborn of the dead.”[80](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The new added element in the last clause is “in everything.” Christ’s rule over the church and being himself the beginning of the new creation is designed to demonstrate that he is supreme over the entire creation (the first creation and the new creation) at the consummation of history.[81](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Thus the logical weight of the verse supports Christ having “first place” among all things. Verses 19–20 give a ground or reason in support of this last clause of verse 18, thus making it the main point upheld by the first three clauses of verse 18, as well as verses 19–20.[82](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Furthermore, as we have seen, the literary structure indicates that verse 17b is the precise center of the chiasm forming the conclusion to the first section about the old creation and being a transition to the second section, on the new creation. Verses 17–18 are a broader center of the chiasm. Just because verse 18a is a broader part of the chiastic center does not mean (contrary to common opinion) that it is the logical or even conceptual center of the section on new creation. Our above analysis shows that it is supportive of the main point at the end of verse 18, which is the purpose clause, itself supported by verses 19–20.

Rendering πρωτεύω (prōteuō) as “supremacy” (TNIV, NIV), “supreme” (NJB, NLT), “preeminence” (ESV, RSV, KJV, ASV, Geneva), or “primacy” (Douay) is likely better than “have first place” (NASB, HCSB, NRSV), since the latter does not express authoritative position as clearly. The word πρωτεύω has the notion of supreme importance, which usually carries with it the notion of greatest authority. Though the word is used only in Col. 1:18 in all of the NT, it occurs in the LXX and Josephus, as well as other early Jewish writings. An example of this meaning for the word is in the book of Esther, where it says that Haman showed his friends his “wealth, and the glory with which the king had invested him, and how he had caused him to take precedence and bear chief rule in the kingdom” (Esther 5:11 LXX). Here the notion of “taking precedence” is not only that of being “most important” but also carries with it the idea of “bearing chief rule in the kingdom.” Likewise Josephus uses the word a number of times to indicate supreme importance and rank, usually with authoritative connotations (e.g., in Ant. 16.383 he speaks of a “whole army and the officers [πρωτεύω]” over that army). Thus the idea of the word generally is that of being first in rank, along with authoritative overtones (cf. also W. Michaelis, TDNT 6:881–82). An example of this would be that King Henry VIII in sixteenth-century England was the preeminent person in all of the country not only with reference to his paramount importance but also to his authority.

This is not a restatement that Christ would be the “first” of the ensuing new creation in chronological order (though that is partly expressed by “firstborn from the dead”) but that he would hold the “preeminent” position in the new creation, from its inception until its consummation. Thus he is preeminent in “all things” (ἐν πᾶσιν, en pasin)[83](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) pertaining to the old cosmos and the new cosmos. However, this last phrase does draw out the idea of rule from the preceding “firstborn from the dead” by indicating that he would hold absolute authority over everything as the divine-human Messiah from the point of his resurrection onward until the final completion of the age, which means that Christ as the incarnate Messiah even has primacy over the old creation (on the latter point, see Bruce 1984: 72–73).[84](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This is not sovereignty only over “all things” that God “re-creates within the faith community” of the new age (as Wall 1993: 73 contends), but he is ruler even over all forces that do not acknowledge his rule. This notion of “preeminence” in Col. 1:18b also is supported by the other expressions of sovereignty in verses 15–17.

Verse 18 in relation to verses 15–17 needs brief reflection at this point. Jesus’s resurrection brought him a “preeminence” (v. 18) that he did not previously have during his redemptive-historical manner of existence with regard to his human nature. But the Son of God (cf. Col. 1:13) came to have no greater “preeminence” (v. 18) in his resurrection than he had always possessed as the eternal, divine Son. The preeminence that the Son of God came to have through his resurrection entails not only a transformation of the human nature that he took on, but also a new redemptive-historical manifestation of what is always true of his eternal person.[85](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

[1:19]

This verse now completes the first parallel about the old creation and the new creation noted above between verse 15 and verse 19 (see the table titled “Preeminence of Christ in the First Creation and in the New Creation”). That is, just as Christ was already the “image of the invisible God” before and at the time of the first creation (1:15), so in the beginning of the new creation the “fullness” of deity “dwelt in him,” so that he is the perfect representation of God “in bodily form” (1:19 and 2:9, on which see below).

The initial ὅτι (hoti, for) indicates that this verse explains why Christ should “come to have first place in everything” (v. 18b), which itself is a further explanation of the similar words and phrases in verses 15–17. The reason is that Christ is God incarnate. But before elaborating upon this explanation, the OT background needs to be uncovered, since it is essential to understanding how verse 19 supports the goal of Christ coming to “have first place in everything” (v. 18).

Verse 19 can be translated in two different ways: “because in him [ἐν αὐτῷ, en autō] all the fullness [of deity] was well-pleased [εὐδοκέω, eudokeō] to dwell [κατοικέω, katoikeō]”; or “in him he was well-pleased for all the fullness [of deity] to dwell.” The conceptual difference between the two renderings is not significant, though the first is perhaps slightly better (see additional notes). It is “all” of God the Father’s divine fullness that was “well-pleased” to dwell in his Son (“in him”), which is borne out by the allusion to Ps. 67 LXX (68 MT; see below). The parallel with 2:9 (“all the fullness of Deity dwells [in Him] in bodily form”) also supports this first rendering (following O’Brien 1982: 51). It is important to clarify theologically that it is not God the Father’s divine nature that gives the Son his divine nature when he became incarnate, but they are both fully divine from eternity past (see 1:15–17). The point is to identify Jesus with Yahweh’s divine identity in the psalm. Colossians 2:9 reinforces how Christ, even in his estate of humiliation, possesses a fully divine nature (“in Him all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form”). The preincarnate divine Son took on human flesh.

A number of commentators have observed that the combined wording of “well-pleased” and “dwell” in Col. 1:19 is traceable to Ps. 67:17 LXX (68:17 MT; 68:16 Eng.; see additional notes):

The unique wording in common between Ps. 67 (68 MT) and Col. 1 points to the probability of such an allusion: Ps. 67:17b is the only place in the LXX where the verbs “well-pleased” (εὐδοκέω, eudokeō) and “dwell” (κατοικέω, katoikeō) occur together. “Dwell” in Ps. 67:17b LXX and “dwell” (κατασκηνόω, kataskēnoō) in 67:17c and 19c LXX refer to God “dwelling” in his temple. Psalm 67:18c LXX makes this explicit: “The Lord is among them, in Sinai, in the holy place.” Most English translations of the Hebrew text of the verse (Ps. 68:17) see a reference to the temple in the last line of the verse (see the chart above). That Paul often quotes from the Psalms (twenty-four times) throughout his letters further enhances the viability of a Ps. 68 (67 LXX) allusion here.[86](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

There are other OT passages that are very similar to the Ps. 67 LXX (68 MT) passage and could be echoed together with the psalm allusion (see the additional notes below). The temple of the psalm is now applied to Christ in Col. 1:19. The notion of Christ as the temple of God’s presence occurs elsewhere in the NT (e.g., John 2:19–21; Rev. 21:22) and in Paul (Eph. 2:20–22), though Paul usually highlights the church as the temple, no doubt because of its identification with Christ (as in Eph. 2:20–22 and 1 Cor. 3:17; for the church as a temple, see also 1 Cor. 6:19; 2 Cor. 6:16; Rev. 3:12; 7:15; 11:1–2).

Beasley-Murray (1980: 177) explains that the temple background in Col. 1:19 has often been overlooked because there has been too much narrow focus only on πλήρωμα (plērōma, fullness), though consideration of the language of the whole verse reveals that “Christ is portrayed as fulfilling the role assigned to the temple in the Old Testament,” particularly Ps. 67:17 LXX (68:17 MT; 68:16 Eng.; so virtually identically see N. Wright 1986: 78; 1990: 463; and 1992a: 117).[87](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) While seeing only possible allusion to the psalm text, Pao (2012: 102) affirms, on the basis of other relevant background, that “Christ fulfills the role of the temple in which one finds the full presence of God” (see further additional notes). If the allusion is intentional, as some rightly think, then the point would be that God’s presence on earth is no longer in the earthly temple (the holy of holies) but is now in Christ, who eschatologically instantiates and typologically fulfills all that the temple represented[88](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (the subject of “well-pleased” could be God or “all the fullness [of deity],” which amounts to the same thing; cf. discussion in Harris 2010: 45). God’s tabernacling presence on earth has now been more fully expressed in Christ’s incarnation than in the old architectural temple, and God was “well-pleased” to do so. The Spirit of Jesus continues that earthly presence in the church as the true form of the temple, since Christ’s ascension.

But the Ps. 68 background does not appear to provide the language of “fullness” that is integrated into the psalm allusion. However, the repeated idea of divine “glory filling the temple [or tabernacle]” is found in the LXX,[89](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) and πλήρης (plērēs, full)[90](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) is used in some of these LXX texts, where the temple is “full of the glory” of God (Isa. 6:1; Ezek. 43:5; 44:4; cf. Isa. 6:3). This background likely supplements the Ps. 67 LXX (68 MT) temple allusion. That this is God’s “fullness” is clear from the development of this passage in Col. 2:9: “For in Him all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form.”

The presence of God’s holy of holies on earth was the most preeminent reality in all of Israel. So also the reason (ὅτι, hoti, 1:19) Christ should “come to have first place in everything” (v. 18b) is that he is the inaugurated end-time temple in which God’s fullness has begun to dwell. He is the escalated form of God’s presence in the holy of holies, and as such he himself is identified with God and is the most preeminent one together with God in the church and in all the cosmos. The idea is not that God’s presence is in Christ but “that all that God is dwells in Christ” (Sumney 2008: 74). The point is that God should be the preeminent one in all of his creation. Since Christ is divine, he also is the preeminent one in the entire creation.[91](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

This statement about God’s tabernacling divine fullness in Christ is an anticipation of a response to the false teaching in Colossae, since this verse is taken up again in 2:9 (“In Him all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form”) and is given as a reason the hearers are not to be “take[n] captive” by false teaching (2:8a) in contrast to holding true teaching about Christ (v. 8b).[92](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Then 2:10 continues to give a reason for not holding to the false teaching: the readers have “been made full in” (2:10a NASB marg.) Christ’s tabernacling presence, and “He is the head over all rule and authority” (2:10b), which is another reference to Christ’s sovereignty over evil demonic beings who inspire the false teaching alluded to in 1:16. We will see that Paul wants to underscore Christ as the true temple here in order to contrast it with a false view of the temple in 2:18.

[1:20]

The fourth and last parallel between the former creation (vv. 15–17) and the new creation (vv. 18–20) introduced at the beginning of this segment is now picked up. Specifically, just as “all things were created . . . in the heavens and on earth” by Christ in the first creation (1:16), so in the second creation Christ “reconcile[s] all things to Himself,[93](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) . . . whether things on earth or things in heaven” (1:20). The parallelism of “created” (v. 16) and “reconcile” (v. 20) indicates that Christ’s reconciling work is part of the creating of a new creation in which “peace” dwells, a connection found in the OT and elsewhere in Paul and the NT (see Beale 2012: 527–55). Christ thus has begun to restore the disorder, disharmony, and lack of peace of the first creation (so Dunn 1996: 104), and this will be consummated in the future.

Verse 20 continues the thought of verse 19 in explaining the effect of Christ becoming the escalated temple of God’s revelatory presence, which continues to fill out the reason given in verse 19 for why Christ is “preeminent in everything”* (v. 18b). Israel’s old temple was the place of God’s mediating presence, where sinful people could come and have their sins atoned for (though not ultimately). Since Christ is God’s incarnate tabernacling presence, he is able definitively to mediate that peacemaking presence through atonement to the alienated creation in the inaugurated phase of the eschaton, especially to sinful humans. That a rupture has occurred between God and his creation is apparent at points in the epistle, especially as this fracture has occurred through the work of the devil and his spiritual forces (1:13), who have been defeated at the cross (2:15). In this light, Christ as their Creator (1:16) is in a position of sovereign rule over them (2:10; following generally Dunn 1996: 102–3). But the break between creation and Creator also occurred through sinful human agency, as 1:14 and 1:21 make clear, though this was under the influence of evil powers (1:13).

This reconciliation to God from the rupture was accomplished by Christ,[94](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) who has “made peace through . . . His cross.” The way this peace came about for believers is expanded upon later, in Col. 2:14: “having canceled out the certificate of debt consisting of decrees against us, which was hostile to us; and He has taken it out of the way, having nailed it to the cross.” To emphasize in 1:20 that it was the very Christ who accomplished this, Paul abruptly repeats the phrase “through him” (δι᾿ αὐτοῦ, di’ autou; see the first additional note on 1:20) from the first part of the verse and then completes it by expanding on the initial “all things:” “whether things upon the earth, whether things in [the] heaven[s],” the latter phrasing highlighting again the complete cosmic effect of Christ’s work.

This does not indicate “universal salvation,” but that at the consummation Christ will bring about a harmony of all things in the new, eternal creation, after decisively judging evil and putting it in its judicial place (as 3:6 indicates; cf. also 3:25). Thus the idea of “reconciliation” in this passage is a reference to the restoration of all things. Indeed, I have argued elsewhere that “reconciliation” is virtually equivalent to the OT concept of the restoration of Israel (see Beale 2012: 527–48). Given that Israel was a corporate Adam and was representative of humanity, here the idea is broadened to a restoration of all humanity (including evil angelic realities), whether by forced or faithful submission. Therefore, this is a “pacification” of all hostile and friendly forces (human and angelic), which has begun and will be consummated at the very end of history.[95](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Just as Christ created all things in the first cosmos (Col. 1:15–17), so his re-creation and reconciling work must also include every single thing of the new creation (vv. 18–20; cf. Sumney 2008: 77, 79). The hostile forces (evil angelic beings) have been decisively defeated at the cross and resurrection (so 2:15). Though they continue to exist (both angelic and human enemies) to the end of the age, they cannot stop the culmination of the pacification process.

Thus Col. 1:20 is a parallel to Eph. 1:10: “the summing [or heading] up of all things in Christ, things in the heavens and things on the earth.” There Christ is portrayed as overseeing a household management of the cosmos (οἰκονομία, oikonomia), putting into order the disordered creation. Christ’s reordering of the chaotic creation includes bringing humanity into a peaceful relationship with itself and with God, partly as a result of defeating the “rulers and authorities” (cf. Paul’s elaboration of Eph. 1:10 in Eph. 1:20–23; 2:2; 3:10). Indeed, this restoration at the very end likely involves bringing about a harmonious restoration of the entire animate and inanimate creation (e.g., see D. Moo 2006: 137). Romans 8:19–21, for example, affirms that “the anxious longing of the [non-human material] creation waits eagerly for the revealing of the sons of God, . . . [and] the creation itself also will be set free from its slavery to corruption into the freedom of the glory of the children of God” (on which, e.g., see J. Moo 2008). Accordingly, the OT refers to eschatological peace involving peaceful harmony between antagonistic humans, hostile animals, and the fertility of creation as a sure sign that peace has come.[96](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This is not a mere restoration of all things in some nebulous way but a restoration of peace in a new, consummate creation (note Isa. 11; 65; 66). The close linkage of reconciliation with new creation is evident from the parallel with Col. 1:16 (“all things were created . . . in the heavens and on earth”) and the parallel language here (“to reconcile all things, . . . whether things on earth or things in heaven”). To enhance this inextricable connection between reconciliation and new creation is the observation that Paul’s other uses of the reconciliation word group are also interconnected with new creation.[97](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) It is likely not coincidental that the temple idea of verse 19, which appears to be carried over into this verse and related to reconciliation and peace, is also connected with a coming new creation in both the OT and the way it is developed in various texts of the NT, including elsewhere in Paul (see the additional notes on 1:20).

Thus there is a reconciliation of the alienation that has occurred in the spirit world, the human world, and the material world (see Melick 1991: 225). Everything in these three spheres that compose the entire cosmos are put into their proper order in relation to and by Christ, which means everything will be rightly subordinated by Christ (Melick 1991: 227).

3. The Effects and Purpose of Christ’s Cosmic Supremacy for Sinners Is to Reconcile Them to God and Make Them Acceptable in His Holy Tabernacling Presence (1:21–23)

[1:21–22]

This subunit is discernible from the change of topic: the effects and goal of Christ’s cosmic sovereignty are immediately stated to apply to the redemption of sinners. The unit is also recognizable by the change from the focus on Christ to the repeated emphasis on “you.” The verse starts with “and,” which is a loose connector, likely indicating a further explanation of Christ’s reconciling work in verse 20. Immediately, a concessive idea is introduced, which serves to highlight the contrasting statement in verse 22:[98](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) despite the fact that the Colossians (“you”) were “alienated and at enmity in mind in their evil works, nevertheless” (δέ, de, BDAG 213) Christ “reconciled”* them to God. This reconciliation has occurred “in the body of his flesh through death.”* “In the body” (ἐν τῷ σώματι, en tō sōmati) is ambiguous. Does it refer to the means (“by”) of reconciliation or the sphere (“in”) in which reconciliation takes place? The focus is likely on the means of reconciliation, since verse 20 has clearly said twice that “through [or by, δι᾿, di’] him” he “reconciled all things.” Nevertheless, that reconciliation has been achieved in the sphere of Christ’s body is probably not out of mind. But it is not merely by Christ’s body that reconciliation has occurred; it is also “by the death” of that body.

The hearers were formerly in a condition of alienation (the significance of the perfect ptcp., enhanced by the preceding ptcp. ὄντας, ontas, being).[99](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) They were also “at enmity by means of the mind [τῇ διανοίᾳ, tē dianoia; thought]” and “by means of evil deeds” (ἐν [en] + dative).[100](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) What does Paul mean by saying that Christ’s death has “reconciled” (on this reading, see the additional note on 1:20, 22) people who are “alienated” and at “enmity” with God? Paul’s use of reconciliation language elsewhere refers to the OT background of Israel’s restoration out of the exile from God’s presence, especially restoration from Babylonian exile. In fact, reconciliation for Paul is the equivalent of the concept of Israel’s hope for restoration from exile (see the additional note on 1:20, 22). The placement of reconciliation in verses 20–22 is appropriate against the OT background, since these verses are the direct result of Christ becoming the end-time temple of God’s presence in verse 19. According to OT restoration prophecies, one of the expectations was that there would be a latter-day temple built in Israel, at which returning Israelites could worship and experience God’s intimate presence and peace. At his death on the cross, Christ as the summation of true Israel has gone through the ultimate exile from the Father, and his resurrection has restored him as Israel into the Father’s peaceful presence, where Christ sits at the right hand of the throne in the heavenly temple. All who identify with Christ’s exile and his restoration experience restoration or reconciliation to God.[101](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This OT prophetic background of restoration is then seen as fulfilled in Christ and all who identify with him.

“But” (δέ, de) introduces a contrast and reversal to the prior alienated and hostile condition of the readers. Together with this, the introductory “now” (νυνί, nyni) in verse 22 is not the “now” of logical deduction but an eschatological “now.”[102](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) “Now,” at the turn of the ages, the great eschatological restoration promised for true Israel, the church, has begun to take place. This reconciliation came by means of “the body of his [Christ’s] flesh through death”* (v. 22). Christ offered his body as a sacrifice to accomplish “reconciliation.” This entails that the “alienation and enmity” of the sinful Colossians (v. 21), which blocked a peaceful relationship with God, was wiped away by Christ’s death, “the blood of his cross” (v. 20). Christ suffered the “alienation” and death in place of sinful humans, so that their alienation and punishment of death would be removed and they could be “reconciled” to God (see further 2:13b–14). This is how the Colossians have experienced “the redemption, the forgiveness of sins”* (1:14). God is the subject of the verb “he reconciled” (1:22) and not Christ (as according to some commentators, e.g., Pao 2012: 107), since he was the subject of “reconcile” in verse 20 (see our argument), which is also in line with all of Paul’s reconciliation discussions in his other epistles, where he always has God as the subject of reconciliation.

The second part of verse 22 gives the purpose of Christ’s reconciliation: “in order to present you holy and blameless and beyond reproach.” That Christ has “reconciled” the Colossians “in His fleshly body” (adj. genitive) implies that “both before and after his resurrection Christ possessed a body” (Harris 2012: 128), since Christ had to “present” them to God after his sacrifice. Colossians 2:9 confirms this by affirming that “all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form,” since context suits the idea that “dwell” (κατοικεῖ, katoikei) is a gnomic present, which cannot be limited to only one point in Christ’s earthly ministry or heavenly session at God’s right hand (likewise, for the same reason κατοικῆσαι [katoikēsai] is a gnomic aorist used in the same way in 1:19). Christ “presenting” the believers as “holy and spotless and blameless”* (1:22) is the language of acceptable sacrifices, which OT priests were to offer in the temple.[103](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

The three words “holy” (ἅγιος, hagios), “spotless” (or “blameless,” ἄμωμος, amōmos), and “blameless” (ἀνέγκλητος, anenklētos)[104](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) are generally synonymous, indicating that which is set apart as pure or “holy.” In the LXX ἅγιος is employed especially in Leviticus to designate sacrifices (of grain or animals) as that which is “holy” to the Lord (Lev. 7:1 [6:37 LXX]; 27:9, 10; Num. 18:17) or first holy to the Lord and then holy for the priests (Lev. 2:3, 10; 6:17 [6:10 LXX]; 10:17; 14:13). The word ἄμωμος is used often in Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers to refer to “spotless” or “unblemished” animal sacrifices offered at the tabernacle (e.g., Exod. 29:1, 38). The “unblemished” nature of the sacrifices made them “acceptable to the Lord.”[105](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Some of these “unblemished” sacrifices were “peace offerings” (e.g., Lev. 3:6, 9), and some were for “sin offerings” (e.g., Lev. 4:3, 14, 28, 32), to appease by atonement God’s wrath about sin committed by Israelites (e.g., Lev. 5:18; Ezek. 43:22; 45:18).

Probably, all of these associations of “unblemished” offerings are included in Paul’s purview to one degree or another. Thus ἄμωμος here is best translated as “spotless” or “unblemished,” referring to Christians as holy and “acceptable” sacrifices “before him [God].” The combined use of “holy” and “blameless” likely then reflects the sacrificial nuances of the words in Leviticus and Numbers (though when the words are used separately later in the OT, they refer merely to moral purity of the saints). Christ is apparently viewed as the priest in the new temple who performs his reconciling work through his own priestly, acceptable sacrifice (“through the body of his flesh,”* v. 22; and “through his blood,” v. 20)[106](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) to make holy and acceptable sacrifices to God of those who were sinful and tainted with sin (cf. Caird 1976: 182). The phrase “to present you before Him [κατενώπιον αὐτοῦ, katenōpion autou]” following “holy and blameless” likely also reflects part of the sacrificial terminology of the tabernacle (= temple), where ἄμωμος is used. The “blameless” sacrifice was to be presented as “acceptable before the Lord [ἐναντίον κυρίου, enantion kyriou]” in the temple (Lev. 1:3; so also 9:2; cf. similarly 4:14; 5:15).[107](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Christ’s cultic offering resulted in “peace” with God for those formerly “alienated” and at “enmity,” as also was the case with the OT unblemished sacrifices.[108](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

[1:23]

“If” the Colossians “remain” or “continue in the faith” in Christ’s work just narrated in verses 18–22, then they will benefit from it (the conditional clause should not be restricted only to verse 22 or only a part of verse 22, though it is uppermost in mind). It is possible that “faith” refers to the doctrinal content of what Christians believe, but it seems perhaps better to view it as a reference to the act of faith on the part of the readers (the word has this sense everywhere else it occurs in Colossians [1:4; 2:5; 2:12; esp. see the parallel of 2:7]). Furthermore, elsewhere in Paul ἐπιμένω (epimenō, continue) plus the dative (as in Col. 1:23) can designate the disposition of people (e.g., Rom. 6:1; 11:22, 23, following Sumney 2008: 87). Persevering in faith is thus the basis for benefiting from Christ as supreme over the new creation and as the end-time temple, which mediates reconciliation to sinful and alienated people. Paul likely has confidence that they will finally persevere in their salvific condition (in light of 1:28b; 2:5 [cf. O’Brien 1982: 69]; 3:12), or at least the majority will.[109](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Nevertheless, despite his confidence, this is a real condition: if the Colossians do not persevere, then they will not benefit from Christ’s work described in verses 19–22. This is developed further in 2:6: “Therefore as you have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him.” If they do not persevere, does it mean that they will lose the benefits of Christ’s redemptive work that have been applied to them as the “elect of God”* (3:12) in 1:21–22 (and elsewhere in the epistle)? Or does it mean that they were pseudo-believers all along and never really benefited from that work? We think the latter is the case.[110](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) However, this classic theological problem involves tensions between election and accountability in relation to warnings, which cannot be fully elaborated on here.[111](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

The means by which they are able to continue in the faith are “by being founded” and being made “steadfast.” “Founded” (τεθεμελιωμένοι, tethemeliōmenoi) is an adverbial participle modifying the verb “continue” by giving the means or instrumentation by which the believers are able to maintain their ongoing faith.[112](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

It is likely not coincidental that “founded” and “steadfast” are usually architectural terms that refer to the stability of a structure (see below). Accordingly, the best translation is “with your foundation established and your structure immovable.”[113](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The structural concept here is their sure maintenance as a part of the temple in Christ, with whom they are identified.[114](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Accordingly, these two words convey architectural language of the temple. What points further to the continuation of the temple idea from 1:19–22 is the generally acknowledged parallel with Eph. 3:17–18: “so that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith; and that you, being rooted and grounded [τεθεμελιωμένοι] in love, may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth and length and height and depth.”[115](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Likely Eph. 3:17–18 develops the temple portrayal of Eph. 2:20–22 (see further in the additional note on 1:23a): “Having been built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus Himself being the corner stone, in whom the whole building, being fitted together, is growing into a holy temple in the Lord, in whom you also are being built together into a dwelling of God in the Spirit.”

If Eph. 3:17–18 continues the temple portrayal of Eph. 2:20–22, and if Col. 1:23 is truly its parallel (as broadly recognized), then it makes good contextual sense that Col. 1:23 continues the temple idea laid out in the immediately preceding text of 1:19–20.[116](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The Ephesians and Colossians passages also have in common the link between “reconciliation” and the temple (Eph. 2:16; Col. 1:20–22), the identically common use of the aorist infinitive κατοικῆσαι (katoikēsai, to dwell: Eph. 3:17; Col. 1:19), and the same use of the perfect passive participle τεθεμελιωμένοι (Eph. 3:17; Col. 1:23).

Christ being the temple (Col. 1:19) was the basis for his being supreme over the new creation (1:18), and the Colossians’ identity with Christ as the temple here thus continues to provide part of the basis for Christ’s new-creational supremacy.

The following participle μὴ μετακινούμενοι, (mē metakinoumenoi, not moved away) is adverbial, like the preceding τεθεμελιωμένοι, and thus likewise indicates the means by which they “continue in the faith.” If they are “founded and steadfast” as a part of God’s temple in Christ, it means they will also be immovable (again, see the conceptual parallel in Rev. 3:12a). To be a permanent part of the temple in Christ is to be “not moved away from the hope of the gospel,” since the gospel is about Christ.

The last phrases of verse 23 are a repetition of the almost identical wording in verses 4–7:

| Colossians 1:4–7 | Colossians 1:23b |
| --- | --- |
| Since we heard of your faith . . . because of the hope laid up for you in heaven, of which you previously heard in the word of truth, the gospel, which has come to you, just as in all the world, . . . just as you learned it from Epaphras, our beloved fellow bond-servant, who is a faithful servant of Christ | From the hope of the gospel that you have heard, which was proclaimed in all creation under heaven, and of which I, Paul, became a servant. |
| Note: Dotted underlining in both columns indicates synonyms and not identical wording in Greek, whereas solid underling indicates the same words in Greek. |  |

The repetition has, at least, a twofold purpose. First, it forms an inclusio around virtually the entire broad literary unit of 1:3–23 (though the parallel at the start of the unit begins at v. 4 instead of v. 3). Both verses 4–7 and verses 21–23 contain four references to “you” (either as the pronoun or as the second-person pl. verb), which points to the abrupt change to “and you” (καὶ ὑμᾶς, kai hymas) in verse 21 as signaling the start of a new and concluding subunit within verses 3–13. This is further evidence that the broad literary unit ends at verses 21–23 (esp. concluding with v. 23).

Second, by concluding the same way the segment began, Paul is underscoring three things: (1) the importance of the gospel that the Colossians have heard and in which they have put their hope; (2) the gospel’s arrival among the Colossians as just one example of its spreading to humans throughout the whole created world order (see the additional note on 1:23b); (3) Paul as the human, apostolic authority behind this gospel, of whom Epaphras “is a faithful servant of Christ on our [Paul’s!] behalf” (v. 7). Epaphras is a worker under Paul’s authority, who himself was commissioned as an apostle to the Gentiles by God. The concluding phrase “of which I, Paul, became a servant”* in verse 23 is a shorthand reference to Paul’s Damascus road Christophany experience, where he was both converted and appointed a prophetic apostle to go to the Gentiles. This is apparent from noticing the unique parallel between Col. 1:23 (and 1:25, which repeats “of which I became a servant”*) and Eph. 3:7 (“of which I became a servant”*).[117](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) It is clear that Eph. 3:7 continues (from earlier in chap. 3) one of Paul’s clearest autobiographical accounts of the commission to Gentiles at his Damascus road experience: “If indeed you have heard of the stewardship of God’s grace which was given to me for you; that by revelation there was made known to me the mystery, as I wrote before in brief, . . . that the Gentiles are fellow heirs and fellow members . . . and fellow partakers of the promise in Christ Jesus” (Eph. 3:2–3, 6).

Additional Notes

1:15a. Several commentators correctly understand that the reference to Christ as the “image of the invisible God” is, at least in part, an allusion to Gen. 1:27, “God created man in His own image, in the image of God He created him”: seeing Gen. 1:27 as an echo are G. Kittel, TDNT 2:395–96; Simpson and Bruce 1957: 193; Yates 1993: 22; and Wall 1993: 66–67. Seeing the Genesis passage as part of the background are O’Brien 1982: 43; Masson 1950: 98–99; and Hugedé 1968: 52–54. Abbott (1897: 210) sees Col. 1:15a rooted in Gen. 1:26. Pokorný (1991: 74) sees allusion to Gen. 1:26–28; a possible variant of this is Burney’s 1926 article (“Christ as the ΑΡΧΗ of Creation”), in which he argues that Paul exegeted Gen. 1:1 (“in the beginning”) via Prov. 8:22, on which see our discussion of Proverbs below (for expansion of Burney’s complicated view, see Caird 1976: 175); cf. Fee 2007: 299–301, who sees reference to Adam as the image of God.

Early Judaism viewed Adam to have been created in God’s image. This was a corporeal Adam composed of body and mind, who “was fashioned after the image of his Maker” (Philo, Heir 56, citing Gen. 1:27; 2:7)[118](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) and who was “the faithful impression of the divine image” (Philo, Heir 57; so also Heir 231, citing Gen. 1:27; Virt. 204, where Adam is said to be in the “image” of the “eternal God”). Philo (Plant. 18–20) also underscores that the corporeal Adam was created “to be a genuine coinage of that dread Spirit, the Divine and Invisible One” who “breathed into his face a breath of life” (Gen. 2:7), and this Adam “has been made after the Image of God [Gen. 1:27], not however after the image of anything created”; likewise Philo (Worse 86) says “the invisible Deity stamped on the invisible soul the impress of Itself, to the end that not even the terrestrial region should be without a share in the image of God.” Noah, as a second Adam figure, is viewed as being given the original Adamic commission from Gen. 1:28 and, accordingly, is said to be “born to be the likeness of God’s power and image of his nature, the visible of the invisible [εἰκὼν τοῦ ἀοράτου, eikōn tou aoratou]” (Philo, Mos. 2.65; cf. Col. 1: 15, εἰκὼν τοῦ θεοῦ τοῦ ἀοράτου, eikōn tou theou tou aoratou).[119](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Here Noah is a physically visible image of God (composed of mind and body), as was the corporeal Adam.

On the other hand, Philo also sees that there was an Adam-like figure who was pure mind and incorporeal. In QG 1.4, Philo in commenting on Gen. 2:7 distinguishes “the sense-perceptible man and a likeness of the intelligible type.” But there is also one who “is [made] ‘in accordance with the image [of God]’” and “made in accordance with [God’s] form,” who is the “intelligible and incorporeal and a likeness of the archetype,” after which the “sense-perceptible man” is made. This incorporeal Adam figure is here called the “Logos of God, . . . the Archetypal idea.” Since the corporeal Adam is “a likeness of the intelligible type” in this passage, he appears to be made after “the Archetype” of the incorporeal Adam.[120](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Here and elsewhere Philo conceives of two Adam-like figures, one being a Platonic and genderless intelligible type (the figure created in Gen. 1:26–27) and the other a mixture of the corporeal and intelligible (the one created in Gen. 2:7), though Philo is not always consistent in how he makes the contrasts (e.g., sometimes he sees Gen. 1:26–27 and Gen. 2:7 to refer to the creation of the same being). In alluding to Gen. 1:27, Philo in QG 1.8 refers to the “molded man,” composed of mind and body, and contrasts him with the “man made in his image,” who is “the intelligible and invisible, . . . in the class of incorporeal species” (likewise QE 2.46). So also in QG 2.56, Philo says that Gen. 1:27–28 refers to the Adam-like figure “who was [made] in the form and likeness of the truly incorporeal Being,” who is the purely intelligible ideal in contrast to the Adamic “molded and earthy man.”[121](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Israelite saints can become “sons of His invisible image . . . [who] is the eldest-born image of God,” the latter of whom is the incorporeal Adam-like figure (Conf. 145–47). Accordingly, Philo can conceive of an Adam-like figure being in the image of God, but not existing in a visible body. Especially notable with respect to the two Adamic figures is Alleg. 1.31:

There are two types of men; the one a heavenly man, the other an earthly. The heavenly man, being made after the image of God, is altogether without part or lot in corruptible and terrestrial substance; but the earthly one was compacted out of the matter . . . which Moses calls “clay.” For this reason he says that the heavenly mind was not molded, but was stamped with the image of God; while the earthly is molded work of the Artificer, but not his offspring.

A classic example of these two kinds of Adam figures in Philo is found in Creat. 134:

“God formed man by taking clay from the earth, and breathed into his face the breath of life” [Gen. 2:7]. By this also he shows very clearly that there is a vast difference between the man thus formed and the man that came into existence earlier after the image of God: for the man so formed is an object of sense-perception, . . . consisting of body and soul, . . . by nature mortal; while he that was after the (Divine) image was an idea or type or seal, . . . incorporeal.

Philo’s repeated reference to an incorporeal and eternal Adam-like figure and a subsequent corporeal Adam is a striking parallel to Col. 1:15, where Christ is portrayed as existing before creation yet with Adamic features. This is a precedent showing that one could be thought to be in the image of God and not have bodily, visible existence before the creation of the earthly Adam in Gen. 1–2. However, there appears to be no direct dependence of Paul on Philo at this point, though presumably Paul may have been aware of Philo’s writings.

Occasionally other Jewish writers referred to a physical Adam being the image of the “invisible” God, but they did not distinguish between two Adam-like figures in the way Philo did. Thus LAE 35 (ca. 100 AD) refers to God as Adam’s “unseen Father” because “he is your image.”

1:15b. Early Judaism closely associated the notions of Adam’s sonship and his being in God’s image. Philo refers to “God forming man” from clay and God’s own breath (Gen. 2:7), which is a contrast to “the man that was born earlier [than the earthy Adam] according to the image of God”[122](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (Creat. 134; cf. 135–39),[123](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) and “the firstborn . . . imitating the ways of the Father” and being in the “divine image” (Conf. 62–63).[124](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Israel was called God’s “firstborn” (πρωτόγονος, prōtogonos, Sir. 36:11) and his “very first πρώτιστος [prōtistos; e.g., Philo, Post. 63] offspring.” See also Jer. 31:9 (38:9 LXX): “Ephraim is my firstborn [πρωτότοκος, prōtotokos]”; Jub. 2.20 (though the Greek text is not extant at this point); Philo, Flight 208: “Israel, the son free-born and firstborn” (πρωτόγονος); Israel was also God’s “firstborn, only begotten” (2 Esd. [4 Ezra] 6:58), his “beloved son and . . . firstborn” (Pss. Sol. 13.9), and “a firstborn, only begotten son” (18.4). Thus Israel as “firstborn” and the law of primogeniture are part of the background for “firstborn” in Col. 1:15b.

Similarly, Philo further strikingly refers to the highest ranking angelic figure as one “who holds the eldership among the angels, an archangel as it were,” who is also “God’s First-born, the Word, . . . ‘the Beginning,’ . . . the Word, and the Man after His image, . . . that is, Israel,” and people who want to be represented by this angelic figure can become “sons of God” (citing Deut. 14:1; 32:18) and “sons of His invisible image, the most holy Word. For the Word is the eldest-born image of God” (Conf. 145–47); see also Philo, Dreams 1.215 (heavenly “High Priest, Firstborn, the divine Word” of whom the human priest is the “visible image”); see likewise Philo, Agr. 51 (“true Word and Firstborn Son,” who is identified as an “angel”); Conf. 62–63 (“Son . . . firstborn” in the “divine image”). The angel probably was conceived to be Israel’s heavenly representative.[125](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Similar to the use of Ps. 89:27 (88:28 LXX) in Colossians is Ps. 2, which underscores a similar idea: the Lord enthrones the “Messiah” (2:2), the end-time “king,” and declares that he is “begotten” as God’s “Son” (2:6–7). The result of this enthronement is the “Son’s” rule over the entire world (2:8–12). Thus “begotten” (γεννάω, gennaō) in Ps. 2 does not refer to a time when the king was conceived or born biologically but rather when he was enthroned. The idea is that the king was born to rule at some point, so it is natural that when that point comes, he can be said to be “begotten.” When the main purpose of his birth is reached, he can be said to be truly “born.” It is likely not coincidental, in light of our discussion so far in Col. 1:15–17, that the Messiah’s “inheritance” in Ps. 2 includes the “nations” and “the ends of the earth [as] your possession” (2:8), which likely develops the Adamic Gen. 1:28 mandate reiterated throughout the OT.[126](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This background may enhance even more the notion that “firstborn” in Col. 1:15 has a clear sense of “sovereignty over.”

1:15–17. To one degree or another a “wisdom” background for the portrayal of Christ is probably included in verses 15–17 in the background but not the foreground (see Caird 1976: 177). Many commentators believe that the figurative portrayal of “Wisdom” in Judaism and in Prov. 8:22–27 LXX (as “the beginning of his [God’s] ways” before the creation) lies behind Col. 1:15–17. For example, some commentators (Lohse 1971: 47–56; N. Wright 1986: 66–71; Simpson and Bruce 1957: 197; and Wall 1993: 68) mention Gen. 1 and OT Wisdom, especially Proverbs (comparable to Proverbs, see further Sir. 24:9 LXX, “From eternity, from the beginning [ἀρχή, archē], he created me [Wisdom]”). In the case of Prov. 8, the personification of “Wisdom” as being in God’s presence directly preceding his creation of the world is to indicate that God’s Wisdom enabled him to be the perfect craftsman in accomplishing this marvelous work. Such a background is certainly plausible, but the allusion is not as clear as so often indicated.

Fee (2007: 300–301, 317–25, 595–630) has made the strongest case against the presence of any verbal allusions in Col. 1:15–17 to Prov. 8 or to the Hellenistic Jewish background of “wisdom” (so similarly Fowl 1990: 118–21). While Fee’s analysis is plausible, at certain points he places too much emphasis on the lack of linguistic parallels. For example, in contrast to Col. 1:16, which has the preposition “through” (διά, dia) for creation “through” Christ, this preposition does not occur in the OT or Jewish texts; rather, “in” (ἐν, en) appears or no preposition at all. On this basis, he sees none of the Jewish texts affirming that creation came “through” so-called Wisdom. But these texts do speak of “Wisdom” conceptually as the agent (if there is personification) or means of creation (even if wisdom is conceived of as a divine attribute, i.e., conveying the notion that God through his own wisdom created; see Wis. 7:22 [7:21 LXX]; 8:4–6; 9:1–2; cf. also Prov. 8:28–30 and the use of ἐν σοφίᾳ [en sophia] in Ps. 104:24 [103:24 LXX] and Prov. 3:19–20, where in each passage both sphere and instrumentation may be in mind, as in Col. 1:16).

Another example adduced by Fee is that the Jewish literature does not use the precise word “firstborn” (πρωτότοκος, prōtotokos) for “Wisdom.” Nevertheless, such strikingly similar expressions about “Wisdom” as the following are used: Prov. 8:22–24 LXX: “the Lord created me the beginning of his ways, . . . before the ages he established me in the beginning, before he created the earth”; likewise, see Sir. 1:4, “Wisdom was created before all things”; and 24:9, “He created me [Wisdom] from the beginning, before eternity.” In addition, Philo (QG 4.97) speaks in linguistic terms close to the language “firstborn of all creation” in Col. 1:15: “Wisdom, who is the firstborn mother of all things” (in Flight 50–51, Philo holds that “Wisdom” is also a “father” and possesses “the powers and activities of consummate men”). Since this section of Philo is based on an Armenian translation, we cannot be sure if the Greek word being translated “firstborn” is πρωτότοκος (prōtotokos) or πρωτόγονος (prōtogonos). Whichever it is, the two are essentially synonymous; yet it needs to be remembered that the logos or wisdom in Philo is created by God and not coexistent with him in eternity past (see Caird 1976: 177), as is true of Christ in Col. 1:15–17, though this might not preclude Paul from being influenced by this background, going from the lesser to the greater. Again, as in the preceding example, Fee’s argument about the absence of “firstborn” in Judaism seems to be too narrow a distinction, bordering on the “word-concept” fallacy.

From another perspective, Fee says that the parallels of OT-Jewish “wisdom” are not strong, since these texts do not speak of “wisdom” as a “hypostasis” or “personification,” but only as an attribute of God. On the other hand, Fee says, the purported application of “wisdom” to Jesus is not conceived as an attribute but the instantiation of wisdom in Christ as a person. But could Paul not be identifying Christ with what was a divine attribute in the OT and Judaism and then be going from the lesser to the greater, thereby seeing that what was only a divine attribute was now to be identified as a divine person? Thus Fee seems to be drawing very thin descriptions from OT-Judaism, when some of these expressions seem “thicker” conceptually than he allows. Part of the problem is that Fee distinguishes a “wisdom tradition” from the use of “wisdom” as a divine attribute, which not all scholars would do. On the other hand, the word “wisdom” (σοφίᾳ, sophia) does not occur in Col. 1:15–17, which should caution us against seeing direct allusions, though echoes may be detected. Pizzuto (2006: 241) pays heed to Fee’s caution yet still sees that the “Colossians author” has taken over themes and language from the “conceptual reservoir of Jewish wisdom speculation.”

Though further discussion of this issue is not possible here, it appears that, in addition to various OT influences noted above (esp. Gen. 1), among plausible Jewish backgrounds that may have influenced Paul to one extent or another throughout Col. 1:15–17 is a pastiche of passages about wisdom from Wisdom of Solomon:[127](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

1. Wisdom, identified with God’s Spirit (in light of 1:6), “holds all things together” (1:7 NRSV).
2. Wisdom is the “reflection of the everlasting light, the unspotted mirror of the power of God, and the image of his [God’s] goodness”* (7:26).
3. Wisdom “makes all things new”* (7:27); “sweetly does she order all things”* (8:1), and “she works all things”* (8:5).
4. Wisdom is above “scepters and thrones” (7:8 NRSV; cf. 6:21).[128](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)
5. “Wisdom . . . was present when you made the world” (9:9 NRSV); “I will trace her [Wisdom’s] course from the beginning of the creation” (6:22 NRSV).[129](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)
6. Wisdom was the agent or instrumentation through which God created the world (7:22 [7:21 LXX]; 9:1–2; so also Philo, Heir 199; Names 137).
7. “You formed man through your wisdom that he should have dominion over the creatures”* (Wis. 9:2), and wisdom “guarded the first-formed father of the world, who was created alone, and brought him out of his own transgression [i.e., his “fall” into sin], and gave him power to rule all things”* (10:1–2).

Boring, Berger, and Colpe (1995: 483–84) mention most of these passages from Wisdom as forming part of the background for Col. 1:15–17. Whereas the fullest manifestation of earthly wisdom in the OT was the Torah, now the Messiah is the greatest expression of divine wisdom on earth, the former pointing to the latter.[130](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Such a wisdom background is not necessarily mutually exclusive of a Gen. 1 Adamic background (so also D. Moo 2008: 118), though others are adamant that only the “wisdom” background is in mind and not Adam in Genesis (so, e.g., as noted above, see Aletti 1993: 94–116; Schweizer 1982: 66; Dunn 1996: 87–88). That the two backgrounds are compatible (so Pokorný 1991: 68) is evident from recalling that Philo applies Gen. 1:27 allusions to a preexistent Adam figure being in God’s image, which Philo sometimes associates with “Wisdom.”[131](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Likewise, we saw in the Wisdom of Solomon that the preexistent “wisdom” “guarded” the earthly Adam, so that there is close connection between the two (See Wis. 9:2 with 10:1–2). Furthermore, an Adamic background in Col. 1:15 is apparent from recalling that one of Wisdom’s key roles was to come to dwell in Israel (cf. Proverbs, Sirach, and Wisdom of Solomon),[132](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) the corporate representative of humanity, to enable them to be truly wise humans and to rule over the creation. This became connected to messianic expectation focused around a coming eschatological king (e.g., Ps. 89:27 [88:28 LXX]; following N. Wright 1992a: 112–13). In addition, the attributes of “Wisdom” were necessary for someone to be truly wise and were “required if humans are to be truly human” (N. Wright 1992a: 113), which Wis. 9:2 (noted just above) comes very close to affirming about wisdom in relation to Adam (“You formed man through your wisdom that he should have dominion over the creatures”*). Likewise Sir. 24:28–34 says that “the first man knew her [wisdom] not perfectly,” but nevertheless “wisdom” continued to “send forth her light afar off . . . to all ages forever . . . for all them that seek wisdom,”* so that wisdom’s knowledge was to be an attribute of truly wise humans. Thus wisdom Christology and Adam Christology are very close, since they are both Israel Christologies (N. Wright 1992a: 113). Recall that the preexistent Christ, as Wisdom, is primarily in view in Col. 1:15a, so that what was true about the earthly Adam’s wisdom would certainly be true of his precreational archetype (at least from Paul’s and Philo’s perspective). Nevertheless, we saw in 1:15 that the timeless present tense “is” (ἐστίν, estin) includes Christ not only being in the Adam-like image of God before creation, but also remaining in that archetypal “image” through to his incarnation, exaltation, and onward forever.

The Gen. 1 kingship background is dominant throughout in Col. 1:15, and Christ as a ruling figure continues throughout Col. 1:16–20 (see also Dunne 2011). That a wisdom background may well be included to some degree is pointed to by the description of the incarnate Christ as the repository of true wisdom in Col. 2:3: “in whom [Christ] are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge,” which, as we will see, is important in countering the purported wisdom of the false teachers (see on 2:3). A wisdom background is also suggested by the idea that one of “wisdom’s” dwelling places on earth was the temple; Col. 1:19 says God’s dwelling place now is in the new temple of Christ (see comments and notes on 1:19).

If Hellenistic Jewish wisdom is part of the background behind Col. 1:15–17, then there would also be included some reference to Christ as the perfect revelation of the invisible God.

1:15–20. The Structure. There have been various proposals for the structure of the so-called hymnic section. In addition to dividing the passage into two parts (for scholars holding this view, see Baugh 1985: 229), there are several who hold to a tripartite structure (1:15–16; 1:17–18a; 1:18b–20; cf. the summary of views by Mitchell 2000: 65). Pizzuto (2006: 118–205) offers his own chiastic analysis, as well as surveying and evaluating other structural analyses. Pizzuto’s own chiasm has verse 17b (“and all things hold together in him”) as the center, verse 17a (“and he is before all things”) and verse 18a (“and he is the head of the body, the church”) as the inner ring and verses 15–16 and verses 18b–20 composing the outer ring. Virtually identically, Reyes (1999: 139–46), following Baugh (1985) among others, proposes the following viable threefold structure: (A) verses 15–16; (B) 17–18a, and (C) verses 18b–20. Sections A and C serve as the two main outer rings, section C is the central matrix, and verse 17b is the core of that matrix and the pivot point of the hymn. Thus verse 17b (“In Him all things hold together”) is the focus of the entire hymn. If this is correct, then the point of the hymn is that everything in the sphere of creation and redemption finds its cohesion in Christ. This structural analysis is attractive, is certainly viable, and may indeed be a refinement of the twofold approach. In addition to D. Moo 2008: 116, another survey of various past structural analyses of Col. 1:15–20 is given by Baugh (1985: 228–35) and Gordley (2007: 5–16).

1:18. There has been debate about the background and origins for Paul’s use of the word ἐκκλησία. Trebilco (2011), building on the arguments of others, thinks the word (ἐκκλησία, ekklēsia, church) is to be understood mainly from its Septuagint background, where it refers to Israel, often in its congregated form. Van Kooten (2012) has responded to Trebilco by contending that the word is best understood, not against an LXX background, but from the Greco-Roman background, where it refers to gathered public assemblies of a political nature. Trebilco (2011: 445–46, 486n84) acknowledges that there may be Greco-Roman influence but affirms that it is slight. Similarly, Van Kooten (2012: 537) recognizes some degree of LXX influence but argues throughout his essay that the Greco-Roman impact is far more dominant.

Of course, there cannot be thorough discussion here. We believe the LXX background to be a greater influence than the Greco-Roman.[133](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) If this is going in the right direction, then it points to the conclusion that the church is the Israel of God in the new-covenant age.[134](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Sometimes, however, the Greco-Roman background is implicit, so that the church is viewed as the true assembly of the heavenly commonwealth in contrast to the assembly of the world.

Nevertheless, the OT background of ἐκκλησία exercises a dominant influence on Paul. Paul refers sixty times to ἐκκλησία as the Christian “church,” among which are twelve references to “the church of God” (ἐκκλησία + θεός, ekklēsia + theos; including once in Acts 20:28, where Paul is the speaker, and once in the plural). Some commentators have asserted that these uses are likely allusions to the repeated references to “the church [assembly] of the Lord” in the LXX (ἐκκλησία + κύριος, ekklēsia + kyrios; seven times),[135](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) including presumably “the church [assembly] of God” (Neh. 13:1).[136](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Commentators have also observed that ἐκκλησία by itself occurs seventy-three times in the LXX as a translation of the Hebrew קָהָל (qāhāl, congregation), which refers to Israel assembled to hear God’s law from Sinai or from Joshua, or it can refer to the nation gathered together for festive occasions.[137](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

The point has not been formerly made, however, that the references to “church of God” (ἐκκλησία τοῦ θεοῦ, ekklēsia tou theou) in Paul appear to focus most specifically on Neh. 13:1. The Nehemiah passage is the only place in all of the Septuagint where the phrase ἐκκλησία τοῦ θεοῦ occurs. Nehemiah 13:1 actually quotes Deut. 23:2, “assembly of the LORD,” but renders it “assembly of God.”

But even if Neh. 13 is not always the focus, if the proposal by some (e.g., Marshall 1973, noted earlier) is accepted that Paul abbreviates “church of God” in his other numerous single references to ἐκκλησία by itself, then it is possible that Paul also viewed the single uses of ἐκκλησία in the Greek OT to be abbreviations of “assembly of God” and “assembly of the LORD.” In this respect, Paul may have assigned an even wider scope of reference to ἐκκλησία throughout the LXX, triggered initially by the “assembly of God” and “assembly of the Lord” references. Whether this is the exact pathway of associations in Paul’s mind may be hard to prove, but something roughly similar to it seems likely. Did Paul always have centrally in focus the OT references to “assembly of God” and “assembly of the Lord” behind all of his own multiple “assembly of God” uses, or did he even have clearly in mind the OT uses of ἐκκλησία by itself in his own single uses of the Christian ἐκκλησία? The likelihood is that the OT was uppermost in his mind at times, though at other times it was just in his peripheral cognitive vision. Nevertheless, even when not the central focus, he probably had the OT tacitly or latently in mind to some degree, being in what we might call his cognitive peripheral vision.[138](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Whichever is the case, the above analysis points to the probability that the LXX uses of ἐκκλησία τοῦ θεοῦ and ἐκκλησία κυρίου and ἐκκλησία by itself are the quarry from which Paul in some way drew for his understanding of the “church,” including his reference in Col. 1:18. The saturation of OT and Jewish allusions in Col. 1:15–22 also points to ἐκκλησία having an OT background. The upshot is that the Christian church is the continuation of true Israel of the OT.

1:19. This verse may be rendered in two different ways: “because in him [ἐν αὐτῷ, en autō] all the fullness [of deity] was well-pleased [εὐδοκέω, eudokeō] to dwell [κατοικέω, katoikeō]” (RSV, ESV); or “in him he was well-pleased for all the fullness [of deity] to dwell” (NASB, KJV, NIV, Douay, HCSB, NJB). In the first, “all the fullness” is taken as a nominative clause as the subject of “well-pleased.” In the second, “all the fullness” is taken as an accusative clause as the subject of the infinitive “dwell.” Two observations support the first rendering: (1) it is more awkward to add “for” (or other words) to make the second translation work; (2) the allusion to Ps. 68:16–17 (67:17–18 LXX; see below) points to the first translation, since it more directly expresses the structure of the psalm reference to God being pleased to dwell in the temple (the psalm has “be pleased” [εὐδοκέω, eudokeō] followed directly by the infinitive “to dwell” [κατοικεῖν, katoikein]; so also D. Moo 2008: 131). The second rendering is supported by Col. 2:9, where “all the fullness of Deity” is the subject of the verb “dwell,” but the first translation also reflects the same thing (“all the fullness was well-pleased to dwell”; complementary infinitival clause). The first translation could seem awkward: “all the fullness was well-pleased to dwell and to reconcile.” Perhaps this awkwardness is intensified by recognizing that a neuter (πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα, pan to plērōma) is joined with a masculine participle in verse 20 (εἰρηνοποιήσας, eirēnopoiēsas). But this awkwardness is softened if this is taken as a “construction according to sense” (Barth and Blanke 1994: 210–11) and is paraphrased as “God in all his divine fullness was well pleased to dwell and to reconcile”[139](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (a paraphrase supported by the parallel of 2:9; cf. Barth and Blanke 1994: 212–13). However, the difference in meaning between either translation is not significant.

In addition to Beasley-Murray 1980: 177; N. Wright 1986: 78; 1990: 463; 1992a: 117; Pao 2012: 102; and Beetham 2008: 143–46, several other scholars have seen an allusion to Ps. 68:16–17 (67:17–18 LXX; 68:17–18 MT). So Pizzuto 2006: 228–29; Münderlein 1962: 266–70, seeing an undoubted connection between Ps. 67:17 LXX (68:17 MT; esp. the targumic version) and Col. 1:19; D. Moo 2008: 131–33, admitting a “probable echo” of Ps. 67:17 LXX (68:17 MT); Abbott 1897: 219, hearing an echo of the psalm text; Barth and Blanke (1994: 212) include Tg. Ps. 68:17 and Tg. 1 Kings 8:27 in the echo; cf. likewise Lona 1984: 127–28. Pao (2012: 102) sees a “possible allusion” to Ps. 68:16 (Ps. 67:17 LXX); Stettler (2000: 254) takes the wording of Col. 1:19 to be a “close analogy” to the psalm verse and cites similarly Ps. 132:13–14 (131:13–14 LXX); MacDonald (2000: 63) says the wording “calls to mind Jewish Scripture, . . . LXX Ps. 67:17, for example”; Wolter (1993: 85) argues that the wording comes from the Jewish OT background, especially Ps. 68:16–17 (and secondarily from Ps. 132:13–14; 2 Macc. 14:35; 3 Macc. 2:16); likewise Lona (1984: 127–28) includes Tg. Ps. 68:17 and Tg. 1 Kings 8:27 in the echo; Talbert (2007: 194) says “the image comes from” Ps. 67:17 LXX or 2 Macc. 14:35; Gnilka (1980: 71) affirms that “especially near in the formulation” of the wording in Col. 1:19 is Ps. 67:17 LXX; O’Brien (1982: 52) says only that the combination of the two verbs in both Ps. 68:16 (67:17 LXX) and Col. 1:19 “is of special significance” (see Hay 2000: 62, more tentatively); Wall (1993: 75) sees a reference to the OT temple but does not adduce convincing parallels.

Haggai 1–2 contains language and concepts quite close to those of Ps. 67:17 LXX (68:17 MT) and Colossians: “Build the house, and I will take pleasure in it [εὐδοκήσω ἐν αὐτῷ, eudokēsō en autō], and I will be glorified” (Hag. 1:8); “I will fill [πλήσω, plēsō] this house with glory” (2:7); Tg. Zech. 3:2 similarly has “the Lord who has been pleased to make his Shekinah dwell in Jerusalem.” Likewise 2 Macc. 14:35 affirms, “O Lord of all, who has need of nothing, you were pleased [ηὐδόκησας, ēudokēsas] that there be a temple for your habitation [σκηνώσεως, skēnōseōs] among us.”

The Targum to 1 Kings 8 also repeatedly links God’s “good pleasure” with his “dwelling” (Shekinah) in Solomon’s temple (Tg. 1 Kings 8:13–15, 23–24, 27–30; 2 Chron. 6:4–5, 20; 7:16); it also twice says that God’s glory cloud “filled the house of the sanctuary” (Tg. 1 Kings 8:10–11), language having further affinity with Col. 1:19.[140](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Similarly to Col. 1:19, the LXX also reads forms of κατοικητήριον (katoikētērion) in 1 Kings 8:39, 43, 49 with reference to the heavenly temple in relation to its extension to the earthly temple; so also see κατοικέω (katoikeō) and κατοίκησις (katoikēsis) in 2 Chron. 6:18, 21, with reference to the earthly temple, and see κατοικητήριον (katoikētērion) in 2 Chron. 6:30, 33, 39 with respect to the heavenly temple in parallel to that of 1 Kings 8:39, 43, 49; so also Pao (2012: 102) sees this language of temple “dwelling” in 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles to lie behind the use in Col. 1:19. Linguistically, among all the above parallels outside Ps. 67:17–18 LXX, the closest is 3 Macc. 2:15–16, which affirms, “for, indeed, your dwelling place [κατοικητήριον, katoikētērion], the heaven of heaven, is unapproachable to men; but since you were well-pleased [εὐδόκησας, eudokēsas] to set your glory in the midst of your people Israel, you sanctified this place [the temple].” This passage itself may likely also be an allusion to Ps. 67 LXX.

Thus Ps. 68:16–17 (67:17–18 LXX) appears to be alluded to in Col. 1:19. But, even if one doubted it, at the very least Col. 1:19 reflects this general background of God being well-pleased to dwell in and fill the temple. In particular, Colossians may resonate with interpretative rings of the Targum (or perhaps the exegetical tradition that it reflects) together with Ps. 68 (67 LXX); and the 1 Kings background at least shows that Paul’s allusive language of Ps. 68 is about God’s dwelling in the temple.[141](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Also cf. Tg. Ps. 132:13: “For the Lord is pleased with Zion; he has desired it for his habitation”; the MT has “For the LORD has chosen Zion, he has desired it for his habitation” (NJPS), and verse 14b reads, “Here I will dwell, for I have desired it”; the LXX renders the MT’s “desired” (אִוִּתִי, ʾiwītî) with “choose” (αἱρετίζω, hairetizō).

Another intriguing observation is possibly related to Col. 1:19. There appears to be some relationship between “wisdom” and the temple in early Jewish understanding of these OT notions, and this might also stand in the background of Col. 1:19. It appears significant, in the light of the generally acknowledged allusion to “wisdom” in Col. 1:15–18, that early Judaism viewed “wisdom” as having its “headquarters” in the temple, as, for example, Sirach affirms: “So the Creator of all things gave me [wisdom] a commandment, and he that made me caused my tabernacle to rest, and said, ‘Let thy dwelling be in Jacob, and thine inheritance in Israel. He created me from the beginning before the world, and I shall never fail. In the holy tabernacle I served before him; and so I was established in Sion’” (Sir. 24:8–10 KJV).[142](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Likewise, Philo says that “God’s dwelling-place, the tabernacle,” is symbolic of “wisdom,” which “is the court and palace of the All-ruler” (Prelim. 116–17).[143](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Very similarly, Philo also says, “Do you not see that also when he received the tabernacle from God, and this tabernacle is wisdom, in which the wise man tabernacles and, dwells, he fixed it firmly and founded and built it up strongly. . . . ‘And it was called the tabernacle of testimony’; that is to say wisdom was borne witness to by God. For every one who seeks the Lord went forth out of his house” (Alleg. 3.46).

This background about the association of “wisdom” and the “temple” points further to a temple reference in Col. 1:19 and may even make more sense of why Col. 1:9 alludes to Exod. 31:3 and 35:31, where God filled people with the Spirit in order to have “wisdom” to be able to build the tabernacle. In verse 9 Paul may already subtly be anticipating the OT temple allusion of verse 19. Verse 9 significantly mentions “wisdom” of the believer, which ultimately is related to one’s identity with Christ, presented as “wisdom” in verses 15–18, since only by growing in “wisdom” can one be “complete in Christ” (1:28), “in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom” (2:3; likewise Col. 3:16).

1:20. Some MSS (e.g., B and D*) omit the phrase “through him” (δι᾿ αὐτοῦ, di’ autou), but it is certainly the harder reading because of its abruptness, and most of the earliest as well as best MSS support the insertion (e.g., 𝔓46, ℵ, A).

1:20. The connection between Christ’s reconciling and peacemaking work as part of a new creation and his identification with the end-time temple of God’s presence observed above in verse 20 finds striking parallel in Eph. 2:14–22:

For He Himself is our peace, . . . by abolishing in His flesh the enmity, . . . thus establishing peace, and might reconcile them both in one body to God through the cross, by it having put to death the enmity. And he came and preached peace to you who were far away, and peace to those who were near. . . . So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints, and are of God’s household, having been built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus Himself being the corner stone, in whom the whole building, being fitted together, is growing into a holy temple in the Lord, in whom you also are being built together into a dwelling of God in the Spirit.

Since Christ is the new temple of the divine presence, he is able to mediate that presence to the alienated cosmos, especially sinful humans in the inaugurated eschatological epoch (for the same connections, see 2 Cor. 5:17–7:1, on which see Beale 1989). Stuhlmacher (2018: 437) thinks the background of verse 20 is the atonement executed in the temple, which was oriented to the renewal and preservation of the cosmos and which brought about reconciliation.

1:20, 22. Discussed above was the notion that Paul’s view of reconciliation in Christ is the beginning fulfillment of the OT promises of Israel’s restoration from exile. We have already explained how this is related to the restoration hopes of the end-time temple, which verse 19 says is Christ. But there is more.

Contrary to the apparent near consensus that there is no OT background for Paul’s reconciliation language (καταλλάσσω, katallassō, word group), there does appear to be a specific OT background in some of the key passages where Paul explicitly discusses reconciliation. These crucial passages are 2 Cor. 5:14–21 and Eph. 2:13–17. After one determines the background and meaning of reconciliation in these two passages, Paul’s other “reconciliation” texts (Rom. 5:1–6:11; 11:11–31; Col. 1:15–22) should be analyzed in their respective contexts, and then the relation of these texts to 2 Cor. 5 and Eph. 2 should also be discussed. We have done this study elsewhere, so only the results can be summarized here.[144](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The primary OT background is Isa. 40–65. One of the crucial Isaiah texts alluded to in the new-creation-reconciliation passage of 2 Cor. 5:14–21 is Isa. 43:18–19, which closely connects the hope of new creation to restoration (a connection we have also seen in Col. 1:18–22 above). The Isa. 43 context is only one of a series of sections in the so-called Book of Consolation (Isa. 40–55) that explains the restoration of exiled Israel as a new creation or at least integrally associates the two concepts of restoration and creation.[145](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Isaiah 60:15–22; 65:17–25; and 66:19–24 continue the same thematic emphasis. God’s act of new creation as restoration is also described outside of Isa. 43 as his “redemption” of Israel (e.g., 44:1–8; 44:24–45:7; 54:1–10) and as a new exodus.[146](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Moreover, Isa. 40–66 describes Israel’s exile as an expression of divine “wrath” (51:20; 60:10), “anger” (47:6; 51:17, 22; 54:8; 57:16–17; 64:5, 9), “forsakenness” (49:14; 54:6–7; 62:4), “rejection” (54:6), “hiddenness” (54:8; 57:17; 59:2; 64:7), and consequent “separation” between God and the nation (59:2). All these texts assume that sin or iniquity is the cause of Israel’s forsaken condition, and this cause is sometimes explicitly stated for the sake of emphasis (cf. 50:1; 51:13; 57:17; 59:1–15; 64:5–9). God’s restoration of Israel from this estrangement is described not only as a redemptive new creation, but also as a time when the nation will “not be forsaken” (62:12) and will be reunited with God (45:14) and “know” him (43:10) because of his gracious initiative in regathering them (54:6–8; 57:18). And God “wipes out their transgressions” (43:25) and frees them from the bondage resulting from sin (42:6–9; 49:8–9) by the sacrificial death of the servant, who becomes the guilt offering for the people (53:4–12). Therefore, the return from exile is a period in which there is a cessation of anger, when “peace” is reestablished between the nation and its God (cf. 48:18; 52:7; 57:19). This is a peace that results from and is characteristic of the new creation, modeled on the original paradisial conditions.[147](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) In fact, in the context of Isa. 43, restoration and new creation are to be viewed as brought about through the payment of a ransom (43:3–7.) and the forgiveness of sins (43:22–25). The vicarious suffering of the servant in Isa. 53 probably has the same function.

Therefore the complex of ideas found in 2 Cor. 5:14–21 can already be seen in Isa. 40–66. The same ideas appear to be present in Col. 1:18–22, especially in connection to the temple idea in verse 19. In light of the thematic overview of Isa. 40–66, it is plausible to suggest that in Col. 1:20, 22 “reconciliation” in Christ is Paul’s way of explaining that Isaiah’s promises of “restoration” from the alienation of exile have begun to be fulfilled by the atonement and forgiveness of sins in Christ.[148](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The believer’s separation and alienation from God because of sin has been overcome through the divine grace expressed in Christ, who has restored the believer into a reconciled relationship of peace with God.

1:21. The periphrastic participial clause in the phrase “and you being alienated [ὄντας ἀπηλλοτριωμένους, ontas apēllotriōmenous]” is best rendered concessively (“though you were formerly alienated”), apparently with the sense of an adverbial or circumstantial accusative-absolute construction, modifying the verb “reconciled” in verse 22 (“nevertheless, now he has reconciled”). This is parallel to the same kind of construction in Eph. 2:1 (see Wallace 1996: 191) in relation to the main verb in 2:4, and the construction is repeated in Eph. 2:5 (which picks up on the beginning of 2:1). The initial ὑμᾶς (hymas) in Col. 1:21 serves as the object of the following verb ἀποκατήλλαξεν (apokatēllaxen) in verse 22 (in agreement, Pao 2012: 106; cf. Lightfoot 1875: 161).

1:22a. Most of the earliest and best MSS read the third-person active verb “he reconciled” (ἀποκατήλλαξεν, apokatēllaxen), though a few early and quality MSS (𝔓18 and B) read a second-person passive verb “you were reconciled” (αποκατηλλάγητε, apokatēllagēte). The latter is certainly the more difficult reading for two reasons: (1) it clashes with the “you” in verse 21, which should be nominative to harmonize with the subject of “you were reconciled” (so Callow 2002: 55); (2) it is followed by an infinitive with a different subject (God) than the preceding verb and better explains the other minor variants, as well as the aorist reading. With respect to the latter, the passive reading is not impossibly difficult, since the subject of the infinitive could well be conceptually Christ, the subject of the preceding phrase: “in the body of his flesh through death.” Though this is a hard textual decision to make, in the end it seems slightly better to side with the majority of the earliest and best MSS and see the passive variant as having arisen in some accidental way early in the transmission of the text. See further accordingly Lohse 1971: 64; O’Brien 1982: 64; Metzger 1994: 554–55, here citing the majority opinion of the UBS editorial committee, though Metzger himself favors the passive; Fee (2007: 314–16) and Lincoln (2000: 606) also prefer the passive, the former providing more explanation than others in support.

1:22b. D. Moo (2008: 142–43) rejects the idea that “holy and blameless” allude to the OT sacrificial background and says that the verb “present” (παρίστημι, paristēmi) together with “blameless” (ἀνέγκλητος, anenklētos) indicates presenting believers as innocent before the judgment seat of God. Abbott (1897: 226–27), Lohse (1971: 65), and O’Brien (1982: 68–69) agree with D. Moo; though they acknowledge that in the LXX ἅγιος (hagios) and ἄμωμος (amōmos) describe sacrificial animals set apart to the Lord, they deny that such thoughts are carried over into the Colossians expressions, claiming that the words have only a judicial sense (somewhat similarly, Mitchell 2000: 72; Sumney 2008: 85–86; Sumney contends that the judicial sense is primary). Among the parallels adduced by D. Moo and containing some of this judicial language, however, only one clearly refers to a judgment context (Rom. 14:10); he cites Eph. 1:4 and 5:27, but these could just as easily fit into an OT sacrificial context. One could add 2 Pet. 3:14 (ἄσπιλοι καὶ ἀμώμητοι, aspiloi kai amōmētoi), which is clearly part of a final judgment scene (cf. 3:10); though only one of the two words from Colossians occurs in 2 Pet. 3:14, the phrase appears to be synonymous with that of Col. 1:22. The word παρίστημι (paristēmi) is used in a variety of ways and only rarely refers to a judgment context (Acts 27:24; Rom. 14:10). Significantly, there is a striking parallel in Rom. 12:1, which refers to the OT sacrifices: “Present your bodies a living and holy [ἁγίαν, hagian] sacrifice, acceptable to God, which is your spiritual service of worship” (likewise the verb παρίστημι is used in Luke 2:22 in a temple context for “presenting” Jesus “to the Lord”). In fact, ἵστημι (closely related to παρίστημι) in the LXX can refer to “presenting” a sacrifice “before the Lord” (Lev. 14:11; 16:7, 10). Also, Paul’s use of παρίστημι a few verses later (Col. 1:28) is not judicial but refers to people becoming progressively “complete in Christ” (Pao 2012: 108).

A sacrificial context in Col. 1:22 is also suggested by the observation that the first part of the verse portrays God as reconciling “in the body of [Christ’s] flesh through death”; a similar sacrificial picture is given in verse 20: “to reconcile . . . through the blood of His cross.” In this light, it is surprising that Mitchell (2000: 72) can say, “The immediate context does not suggest a primary interest in sacrifice.” However, to boost the proposal of a judgment context, D. Moo (2008: 142–43) contends that ἀνέγκλητος (anenklētos) is a legal term (Col. 1:22). However, its other uses by Paul do not have a context of judgment clearly in mind; the majority of his uses refer to candidates for the office of elder or deacon being “above reproach” as one of the necessary qualifications (1 Tim. 3:10; Titus 1:6, 7), although 1 Cor. 1:8 could include a judgment scene. Harris (2012: 247) also sees κατενώπιον αὐτοῦ (katenōpion autou) as expressing a judicial atmosphere (Col. 1:22). In support, he cites Eph. 1:4 and Jude 24, neither of which clearly allude to a juridical context (though Jude 23 is set in a context of judgment).

So it is possible that such a judgment context is echoed here with the word, but it is not clear. Yet if so, it would be a legal metaphor combined with the sacrificial background (as, e.g., Dunn 1996: 109–10), much as Rom. 3:24–25 combines a legal background (“justification”) both with slave-market language (“redemption”) and sacrificial language (“propitiation” or “mercy seat”). In fact, the sacrifices in the OT temple system carried both notions of purity and a legal sense of “not guilty.” The Hebrew word for “atone” (כִּפֶּר, kipper) conveys the notion of “ransom,” which is a payment that delivers a guilty party from a just punishment by the offended party (God) and appeases the offended party, thus restoring peace to the relationship. But the idea of “purify” is likely also included in the word’s meaning, since this is sometimes seen to be the effect of the “atoning” (e.g., see Lev. 16:33a; Ezek. 43:20–26). In reality, in all of the contexts where the verb (kipper, atone) occurs, it is very difficult to choose one idea over another, since one notion may be the focus but the other may still be implied or echoed secondarily (see Beale 2011: 486–90). So the word ἀνέγκλητος (anenklētos) is also not inappropriate for the sacrificial context already suggested by “holy and spotless” (Col. 1:22). Pokorný (1991: 92) likewise sees the idea here of presenting sacrifices acceptable to God to be similar to the forensic notion of ἀνέγκλητος, “beyond reproach”; Garland (1998: 67) recognizes both a sacrificial background that leads to “lawcourt imagery.” Moule (1957: 73) and Caird (1976: 182) understand the words “present,” “unblemished,” “holy,” and “before him” to be sacrificial language, though they do not comment specifically on ἀνέγκλητος. Pao (2012: 108–9) also sees that a sacrificial idea is dominant in Col. 1:22b for some of the same reasons as noted above; he does not see an exclusively future reference to “before [κατενώπιον, katenōpion] him,” since Paul frequently uses the word with reference to the present age.

1:23a. On the viability of the parallel temple imagery of Col. 1:23a with Eph. 3:17–18, note the following, which points to Eph. 3:17–18 being the development of the temple portrayal of Eph. 2:20–22: (1) κατοικέω (katoikeō) of Eph. 3:17 probably develops κατοικητήριον (katoikētērion) of 2:22, clearly referring to God’s “dwelling” in his temple of the church. (2) In both texts such “dwelling” occurs through the “Spirit” (πνεῦμα, pneuma, Eph. 2:22; 3:16). (3) Both passages speak of Christ providing a “foundation” for believers: “having been built on the foundation [θεμέλιος, themelios] of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus Himself being the corner stone” of the foundation (Eph. 2:20), “so that Christ may dwell in your hearts” and thus you may be “grounded [τεθεμελιωμένοι, tethemeliōmenoi] in love” (3:17). (4) The purpose of this indwelling through the Spirit and resulting “grounding/founding” is so that believers “may be able to comprehend . . . what is the width and length and height and depth.” What are these dimensions about? Likely they are about believers’ understanding the amazing scope of Christ’s indwelling in them as his temple. It is not coincidental that these dimensions are sometimes descriptions of God’s temple in the OT. For example, 1 Kings 6:20 LXX affirms with the same three Greek words as Eph. 3:18 that the “length . . . and the width . . . and the height” of the inner sanctuary (the holy of holies) were all of equal measurement. Indeed, the same three Greek words are used for the ark in the tabernacle (Exod. 25:10), for the equal measurements of the “ten brazen bases” in the temple courtyard (1 Kings 7:27 [7:14 LXX]), for the bronze wash basins in the temple courtyard (Eup. 2.17), for the measurements of “the portico in front of the house [temple]” (2 Chron. 3:3–4 LXX), for the “four tables of hewn stone” for the whole-burnt offerings in the temple courtyard (Ezek. 40:42), for the foundation of the temple (Eup. 2.13),[149](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) and for the measurements of the walls surrounding Israel’s temple in the Second Temple period (Let. Aris. 84).[150](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

(5) The word “rooted” (ῥιζόομαι, rhizoomai) found in conjunction with “founded” in Eph. 3:17 is not typically an architectural metaphor, but the verb is used in association with the temple (Sir. 24:10–12), and so is the noun form: the “Lord . . . left us a root and a name in the place of thy sanctuary” (1 Esd. 8:78 KJV [8:75 LXX]). While it may seem odd to have an agricultural metaphor used with an architectural metaphor to describe the temple, it is not unprecedented in Paul, since the arboreal garden of Eden was a sanctuary, whose various garden-like features were replicated in design in Israel’s later temple (see Beale 2004: 66–80, e.g.). This explanation of such apparently mixed metaphors appears best to account for Paul’s abrupt shift from speaking about “God’s field” and “God’s building” in one breath (1 Cor. 3:9), and then elaborating on this in terms of the church being a temple (1 Cor. 3:10–17; Beale 2004: 245–52). We find both agricultural and architectural words in the temple passage of Eph. 2:21: “the whole building, being fitted together, is growing into a holy temple in the Lord” (emphasis added; Beale 2004: 263). Thus “rooted and grounded/founded” in Eph. 3:17 is consistent with the agricultural and architectural language of the temple in the OT and elsewhere in Paul (see Eph. 2:20–22, which appears to be developed in 3:17–18).

1:23b. Some translations have “to every creature under heaven” (e.g., RSV, KJV, NIV), which for those favoring this rendering would mean “to all humankind.”[151](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Other translations have “in all creation under heaven” (e.g., ESV, NASB, HCSB), which would refer to all animate and inanimate creation. The verbal parallel with 1:4–7 favors the latter as an all-inclusive reference to creation (so also Zerwick and Grosvenor 1981: 605; Robertson [1934: 772] renders this “every created thing”). The all-inclusive meaning is confirmed from the reference to “in all the world” in 1:6, to “all creation” in 1:15 (cf. Wall 1993: 85; though human creation is likely the focus here), to “all things” being created in 1:16, and “all things” being “held together” in 1:17, all of which refer to the original creation of all things, which has its background in the Gen. 1 creation account.[152](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

The use of κτίσις (ktisis) in the NT (including Paul) can refer to all “creation” or particular “creatures” (the latter possibly even in 2 Cor. 5:17 and Gal. 6:15), though when Paul uses κτίσις and κόσμος (kosmos) together, they refer to the entire created world (Rom. 1:20, “the creation of the world”). The same two words are used together in Mark 16:15: “Go into all the world and preach the gospel in all of creation”* (though the last phrase could also be translated “to all creation” or “to every creature”). What may suggest that here in Mark the “world” and “creation” also refer to the entire creation is the observation that κτίσις has this reference elsewhere in Mark (10:6; 13:19; and 14:9 suggests the same: “wherever the gospel is preached in [the sphere of] the whole world [κόσμος]”).

What points further to κτίσις in Col. 1:23 being a reference to the entire creation and not merely human creatures is that, as we have seen in verse 20, restoration at the very end of time involves bringing about a harmonious restoration of the entire animate and inanimate creation (see Rom. 8:19–21). Thus the gospel is preached throughout the sphere of the whole created order to humans who inhabit that order (for further support of this idea and “in all creation under heaven” possibly being a hyperbole, see on 1:6 above). It is possible to see the expression of cosmic universality in 1:23 and its parallel in 1:6 to be a synecdoche of the whole for the part (so Bullinger 1968: 620). The purpose of such a figure is to emphasize the significance of the part for which the whole is substituted. The point would be that the gospel has begun to penetrate into part of the Gentile world outside of Israel, and it will inevitably spread throughout every part of the earth by the consummation of time. Thus it is better to take the expression literally in an “already and not yet” eschatological sense rather than as hyperbole (see Pao 2012: 110). Christ’s divine sovereignty over every part of creation demands that he will eventually have absolute dominance over that creation at the eschaton (cf. 1 Cor. 15:24–28; see Pao 2012: 111–13).

# III. Letter Body: Christians Should Strive for Maturity (1:24–4:6)

| I. | Letter Opening: Paul and Timothy Desire Grace and Peace for the Colossians (1:1–2) |
| --- | --- |
| II. | Letter Thanksgiving: Paul Thanks God for the Readers’ Christian Lives (1:3–23) |
| ➤ III. | Letter Body: Christians Should Strive for Maturity (1:24–4:6) |
| IV. | Letter Closing: Paul Emphasizes His Pastoral Concern for the Colossians (4:7–18) |

Here is a fuller statement of the main idea of this unit: Christians should strive for maturity, not by keeping earthly legalistic laws but by faithfully seeking the heavenly Christ, which should result in putting off the old sinful lifestyle and living a new-creational lifestyle with a view to prayer for the gospel’s spread and witness.

Now Paul comes to the main part of the letter. This largest segment of the letter is divided into eight sections. (1) Paul takes joy in his struggle to proclaim Christ (1:24–2:5); (2) Christians should base their lives only on Christ (2:6–15); (3) it is not necessary for saints to submit to extraordinary religious rituals (2:16–23); (4) believers should concentrate on things in heaven and not things on earth (3:1–4); (5) saints should put off the sinful lifestyle characteristic of the old world (3:5–11) and instead (6) put on a new-creational lifestyle with a focus on Christ (3:12–17); (7) this new-creational living should especially take place in the workplace and family (3:18–4:1); (8) such new-creational living should result in prayer for the spread of the gospel (4:2–6). The first section (1:24–2:5) focuses on Paul’s rejoicing in his struggle to proclaim Christ and his rejoicing in his ministry to the Colossians to bring them to maturity in Christ and to prevent them from falling into deception.

## A. Paul Rejoices in His Struggle to Proclaim Christ (1:24–2:5)

| III. | Letter Body: Christians Should Strive for Maturity (1:24–4:6) |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | ➤ A. | Paul Rejoices in His Struggle to Proclaim Christ (1:24–2:5) |
|  | B. | Believers Should Live Only on the Basis of Christ (2:6–15) |
|  | C. | Believers Should Not Be Required to Submit to Extraordinary Religious Practices (2:16–23) |

Here is a fuller statement of the main idea of this unit: Paul rejoices in his struggle to proclaim Christ as the revealed mystery of the OT, which leads to rejoicing in his struggle to bring believers to eschatological maturity in Christ and to prevent their deception.

This unit circles back to the main point of 1:3–23, which is to “walk worthy of the Lord to please him” (v. 10a). The goal of 1:24–2:5 is that believers be “complete in Christ” (1:28), which is achieved by them not being “deluded” by false teaching, so they can maintain “good discipline” and “stability of faith” (2:4–5). And this becomes the basis of “walking in” Christ in 2:6 (the beginning of the next section), which explicitly expands on “walking” worthily in 1:10a.

The focus of 1:3–14 was on God the Father, which leads to a focus on Christ the Son (1:15–23), and this leads to Paul, the minister of Christ (1:24–2:5; Pao 2012: 119).

Paul begins this section by rejoicing in his sufferings on behalf of the Colossian Christians (v. 24a), especially because his suffering “fills up” some of “what is lacking” in the amount of decreed “afflictions” for the body of Christ, the church (v. 24b). This suffering is explained to be what is entailed in being a “servant” appointed as a steward by God to announce “the word of God” (v. 25). God’s “word” is the formerly hidden “mystery,” which has now been revealed (v. 26). Paul defines this declared “mystery” to be “Christ,” “the hope of glory,” “among the Gentiles” (v. 27). In response, Paul “announces” this revealed mystery about Christ in order to “present every [hu]man complete in Christ” (v. 28). It is in view of this goal that Paul rejoices to “suffer” (v. 24) or “struggle,” and he is spurred on to do so because of the power that God imparts to him (v. 29). The aim of “presenting every man complete in Christ” is the main point of verses 25–29, since everything else in the paragraph supports it.

Colossians 2:1–5 elaborates on how the goal of “presenting every man complete in Christ” (1:28) is to be accomplished. Paul wants all in the region of Colossae to know his joyous struggle in the goal of bringing them to completion in Christ (2:1). The purpose of imparting this knowledge is “that their hearts may be encouraged” (v. 2a). The grander purpose of such knowledge is that the Colossians would attain “to all the wealth,” and this is immediately explained to be “a true knowledge of God’s mystery, which is Christ”* (v. 2b). Christ is the content of this “true knowledge of God’s mystery” because “in him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (v. 3). Paul underscores that Christ is the content of “a true knowledge of God’s mystery” to achieve the goal for the readers not to be “deluded with” false teaching (v. 4). To accomplish this goal, Paul rejoices in wanting to bring the readers to “stability of . . . faith” (v. 5), and to achieve the goal to “present” the believers “complete in Christ” (1:28). Thus “presenting every man complete in Christ” (1:28b) is also the main point of 2:1–5, since that section undergirds it.

The boundaries of this new literary unit are apparent from the new topic introduced in 1:24, which extends through 2:5. This new topic is Paul’s suffering servanthood in making known the mystery. The boundaries of this segment are also observable from its broad chiastic structure, where the parallels are formed not merely by concepts but also by the very same Greek terms[1](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21):

A “Rejoice” (1:24)

B “make known,” “riches,” “mystery” (1:27)

C “struggling” (1:29)

Cʹ “struggle” (2:1)

Bʹ “knowledge,” “riches,” “mystery” (2:2)

Aʹ “rejoicing” (2:5)

1:24–29 and 2:1–5 are also each loosely structured chiastically, revealing them as literary subsections of this larger literary unit (see below).

Exegesis and Exposition

24Now I rejoice in my sufferings for your sake, and in my flesh I do my share on behalf of His body, which is the church, in filling up what is lacking in Christ’s afflictions. 25Of this church I was made a minister according to the stewardship from God bestowed on me for your benefit, so that I might fully carry out the preaching of the word of God, 26that is, the mystery which has been hidden from the past ages and generations, but has now been manifested to His saints, 27to whom God willed to make known what is the riches ⌜of the glory⌝ of this mystery among the Gentiles, which is Christ in you, the hope of glory. 28We proclaim Him, admonishing every man and teaching ⌜every man⌝ with all wisdom, so that we may present every man complete in Christ. 29For this purpose also I labor, striving according to His power, which mightily works within me. 2:1For I want you to know how great a struggle I have on your behalf and for those who are at Laodicea, and for all those who have not personally seen my face, 2that their hearts may be encouraged, having been knit together in love, and attaining to all the wealth that comes from the full assurance of understanding, resulting in a true knowledge of ⌜God’s mystery, that is, Christ Himself⌝, 3in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge. 4I say this so that no one will delude you with persuasive argument. 5For even though I am absent in body, nevertheless I am with you in spirit, rejoicing to see your good discipline and the stability of your faith in Christ.

1. Paul Rejoices in His Struggle to Proclaim Christ as the Revealed Mystery of the Old Testament (1:24–29)

Colossians 1:24–29 reflects a loose thematic chiastic arrangement, which identifies it as the first subsection of 1:24–2:5:[2](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

A The apostle’s suffering (v. 24)

B The apostle’s commission (v. 25)

C The mystery (vv. 26–27)

Bʹ The carrying out of the commission (v. 28)

Aʹ The apostle’s toil and struggle (v. 29)

[1:24]

The introductory νῦν (nyn, now) is not primarily temporal (as it can be elsewhere) but indicates in context further explanation of something that precedes (“now, at this point in my argument, I want to elaborate”).[3](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Paul begins to expand on what it means in the directly preceding verse that he was “made a servant” in “proclaiming” the “gospel” (v. 23b). This link with the preceding is clear from the verbatim repetition of “I became a servant”* in verse 23 and in verse 25. His servanthood entails “sufferings for your sake” (v. 24a), that is, “for your benefit,”[4](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) though precisely “for your sake” modifies “I rejoice,” which means his “rejoicing in sufferings” is for the benefit of the readers. The reason for his “rejoicing” in suffering and the explanation of his suffering[5](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) is that he “fills up what is lacking in the tribulations of Christ,”* which are said again to be “for the benefit of” (ὑπέρ [hyper] + genitive) not only the Colossians but also the wider corporate body of believers (Christ’s “body”). This repetition highlights that Paul’s labors in ministering to the Colossian church are for their benefit and intended to reveal the mystery of Christ (1:25–27; 2:2) in order to “present [them] complete in Christ” (1:28), which is the main point of all of 1:24–2:5. It is hard to know if these are sufferings due to Paul’s imprisonment or some other kind of sufferings.

In Ephesians, Paul likewise directly links his revelation of “the mystery of Christ” (3:2–11) to his imprisonment “on behalf of the Gentiles”* (3:1) and his “tribulations on behalf of” believers (3:13). This suggests that the “sufferings” in Col. 1:24, indeed, also refer to imprisonment. References to “being bound”* in Col. 4:3 and “my chains” (NRSV) in 4:18 further point to Paul’s sufferings being imprisonment (Foster 2016: 213). Also in Eph. 3:1 is the repetition of ὑπέρ (hyper) + genitive, which underscores that Paul’s manifestation of the mystery through his ministry is for the “benefit of” the believers, especially Gentiles. This benefit is explicitly said in Ephesians to be the readers’ “glory” (3:13).

Assuming that the notion of glory from Eph. 3 carries over, how does Paul’s suffering relate to the readers’ glory? When believers persevere in the midst of weakness, God causes his glorious strength to shine through them (2 Cor. 4:16–18; 12:9–10; 13:4). At least part of what appears to be entailed in the “glory” in Eph. 3:13 is that Paul’s suffering for the readers “is producing” even in the present “an eternal weight of glory” (2 Cor. 4:17) not only for Paul but also for them, since both they and he are corporately identified together as Christ’s body and share common core realities that are part of that “body.” Since they share the same sufferings as the body of Christ, so they share the same glory. This notion from Eph. 3 is likely in mind in Col. 1:24, since in the following verses several specific parallels with Eph. 3 can be observed (see below). Furthermore, as in Eph. 3:13, so Col. 1:27 mentions “glory” twice: “the richness of the glory of this mystery among the Gentiles, which is Christ in you, the hope of glory.” Here the focus on “glory” is on “Christ in” the Gentiles. It is Paul’s suffering ministry (1:24) that has resulted in the revelation of this mystery to the Gentiles and their sharing in the glory of Christ, in which Paul also shares.

That Paul “fills up what is lacking in the tribulations of Christ” does not mean that there was an insufficiency in Christ’s atoning work at the cross, to which some other human could make addition, since everywhere else Paul is convinced of the sole and complete sufficiency of Christ’s atoning work for sinful people. Furthermore, if Paul were contending that Christ’s death was not completely sufficient, it would support the false teachers’ contention (later in the letter) that something more than Christ is required to receive divine blessings (Sumney 2008: 99). According to this perspective, these are not the “afflictions” of the historical Christ but the “afflictions of Christ” in “His body (which is the church),” part of which Paul experiences (“fills up”) in his own “flesh.” Since the “church” is explained to be Christ’s corporate “body” at the end of the verse (so also in Eph. 1:23), Paul’s “filling up” of Christ’s trials means that he does his part (“in my flesh”) as a fellow member of God’s new covenant people in enduring the trials (patterned after Christ’s sufferings) destined throughout the interadvent age for the benefit of[6](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Christ’s corporate body, the church.[7](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This is in line with Christ’s apostolic commission of Paul in which he wanted to show to Paul “how much he must suffer for my sake”* (Acts 9:16; following O’Brien 1982: 76).

Thus, “what is lacking of the tribulations of Christ”* could well be a wholative genitive, indicating all the decreed suffering of the universal church, a part of which Paul himself suffers[8](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (for alternative ways to take the genitive see Schweizer 1982: 103; Barth and Blanke 1994: 254). Revelation 6:11 could express this a little more clearly: deceased saints in heaven are told to continue to “rest . . . until the number of their fellow servants and their brethren who were to be killed even as they had been, would be completed [πληρωθῶσιν, plērōthōsin] also” (so NASB, RSV, NRSV, ESV, HCSB, NLT, NIV [cf. NJB] rightly insert the notion of a “number to be completed”). In the light of Rev. 6:9–10, the final judgment will begin when all believers whom God has decreed to suffer finally fulfill their destiny. Paul is among the number of those who are in the process of fulfilling part of the decreed suffering destiny of the corporate body of Christ. The idea is not so much that Paul’s suffering is part of the decreed afflictions of the corporate body of Christ so that other Christians will have to suffer less (so Caird 1976: 184) but that all have their allotted portion of suffering to endure.[9](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

The church is destined for suffering because of its identification with Christ, who went through inaugurated end-time tribulations during his own earthly ministry. In Acts 26:15, Jesus says to Paul at the Damascus Christophany, “I am Jesus whom you are persecuting.” But Paul was persecuting Christians and not the very person of Jesus on earth. Jesus’s statement indicates that he corporately represents those who trust in him. Consequently, when his followers suffer, he can speak of himself as suffering.[10](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The reason believers suffer, however, is that they are first identified with Jesus and his earthly suffering destiny. Revelation 1:9 testifies to this: “I, John, your brother and fellow partaker in the tribulation and kingdom and endurance in Jesus.”* This threefold self-description in verse 9a is modeled on that of Christ’s in Rev. 1:5a because John views Christians as corporately identified with Jesus.[11](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This corporate identity is the basis for the latter-day tribulations that confront them, as well as for their strength to endure such trials and to participate in the kingdom as kings[12](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) (cf. Luke 24:46; Acts 14:22; Phil. 1:29).[13](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Corporate identity with Jesus’s suffering is a theme found elsewhere in Paul (Rom. 8:17; 2 Cor. 1:5; 4:10–12; Phil. 3:10; following Schweizer 1982: 104).

Another less probable version of this view is that Paul’s sufferings being “for you,” that is, “for his body, the church,” means that the greater amount of destined suffering Paul incurs, the less other believers (including the Colossians) will endure and the sooner the messianic glories of the age to come will materialize.[14](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

While we believe the above corporate view is viable, there is another view of “filling up Christ’s tribulations” that is more persuasive and fits better with the meaning of ἀνταναπληρόω (antanaplēroō). Some affirm that Paul sees his “filling up that which is lacking in Christ’s afflictions” to be something unique only to him and his apostolic role on behalf of the church. But a difficulty with this view is the recognition that the apostolic circle’s experiencing “the sufferings of Christ” is not unique to them but is shared by the Corinthian Christians (2 Cor. 1:5–7; cf. also 2 Cor. 4:10, “carrying about in the body the dying of Jesus,” which seems to refer to sufferings of the apostolic circle that are extended to the Corinthian believers [4:14–18]). Accordingly, it would seem unlikely that Paul portrays himself as the only one suffering and “filling up Christ’s afflictions” for the sake of the church, which does not share in the apostle’s unique suffering.

But elsewhere Paul can refer to things about his apostolic role that are unique to him and to things that he holds in common with all believers. The question is which is the case here. We think the scales are tipped in the direction of his unique apostolic role. The following considerations favor this perspective. (1) Col. 1:15–23 highlights the unique redemptive-historical person and work of Christ and how that affects believers in the inaugurated era (being reconciled, v. 22) and at the eschatological consummation (“presented before Him holy and blameless”). (2) Col. 1:25–29 also focuses on the unique redemptive-historical role of Paul as an apostle. Accordingly, 1:24 fits naturally into this unique redemptive-historical context. In fact, Col. 1:24–29 presents Paul in his unique suffering role as a mirror image of the unique Christ in his suffering role (his death, 1:18, 22; his “blood,” 1:20; Clark 2015: 107–27).[15](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Also, (3) recent lexical study of ἀνταναπληρόω has clarified the meaning of the word here in Col. 1:24. A consensus about the meaning previously was generally “filling up something that is lacking.” Clark (2015) has investigated every use of ἀνταναπληρόω in extrabiblical Greek literature (twelve relevant uses, including the noun form; Clark 2015: 14–50). He found a pattern of meaning that is more specific than the above-cited general notion: (a) that which achieves fullness comes from a source other than the beginning contribution of the original source and in place of its lack (this is the semantic significance and contribution of the prefix ἀντί [anti]); (b) seven of the uses have the idea of “add so as to bring to completion in place of another.”[16](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Furthermore, what adds is similar or identical to what originally contributes. In fact, six of the uses exhibit the syntagmatic pattern of verb + direct object + genitive, which is identical to the pattern in Col. 1:24. Furthermore, of the remaining six uses, four have the direct object clearly expressing the notion of “lack,” as also in Col. 1:24 (using words such as ὑστερέω, hystereō; ἐνδέω, endeō; or λείπω, leipō). Two other uses have the idea of a “filling” that brings “balance or proportion,” which does not appear to be an idea included in Col. 1:24. Clark (2015: 42) concludes, “The contribution made in the action of ἀνταναπληρόω in Col. 1:24 is probably one that is similar/identical to the former contribution, so that it has a completing (versus balancing) effect.” The first contribution failed to bring completion (for whatever reason), and in the action of the verb ἀνταναπληρόω, a second or subsequent “contribution is made in addition to (or in place of) the original contribution” to bring about fullness (Clark 2015: 42). The point is that a final completion is achieved or desired.

Specifically, according to this meaning, the afflictions could be either those that Christ had suffered (or in some way was still suffering) or that the church was enduring (and was continuing to suffer). But the latter possibility is ruled out by the meaning of ἀνταναπληρόω referring to a second contribution from another source, which added to the first and fully completes what the first began to do. That is, the notion cannot be that the church began to suffer (as the first contribution) and that Paul and later people in the church continue to “fill up” the afflictions, since the second contribution (in this case, Paul) fully completes what began with the first. There are no further contributions of suffering after the second is added.[17](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Also, that Paul and later generations of the church do the “filling up” would mean that the second contribution comes from the same source (the church), and we have seen the word as indicating that the second contribution comes from a different source.[18](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Likewise ruled out is the notion that Christ made the first contribution of suffering on earth and then continued to suffer as the exalted Lord, since the ἀνταναπληρόω refers to the second contribution being added from a different source.[19](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Rather, the most viable view is that Christ made the first contribution of suffering, which was incomplete, and that Paul necessarily had to suffer additionally in place of Christ’s incomplete sufferings in order to bring those sufferings to definitive completion. This conclusion is based on seven (at most nine) uses in extrabiblical writings (and four of these from only one writer, Apollonius Dyscolus, 2nd cent. AD). Is this enough to establish a pattern of usage to be determinative for Col. 1:24? Probably, especially if the context also fits with the meaning, which it does.

Paul is doing this completing “on behalf of the church.” Thus Paul is completing the prophesied suffering of the Messiah. This does not mean that Christ’s unique atoning sufferings were incomplete or inadequate but that the general, imitative sufferings of Christ in fulfilling the prophecy of Israelite and Gentile salvation were incomplete. We saw above that all Christians share in and are destined to be conformed to the exemplary sufferings of Christ.[20](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) So Col. 1:24 is applying this general concept in a unique way to Paul as Christ’s suffering apostle. Paul does not suffer in an atoning manner for the church as Christ did, but he suffers as an apostle like Christ in completing the task of reconciliation that Christ began. It is very important to remember that the contribution in the use of the verb ἀνταναπληρόω (antanaplēroō) completes either in an identical or similar manner as the original contribution. It is clear that Paul’s completing sufferings are not identical to Christ’s (they are not atoning), but they are similar or imitative.

In this respect, Paul likely would have noticed that his own sufferings were similar to the Messiah’s nonatoning sufferings, especially as portrayed in the suffering servant of Isaiah.[21](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The servant was “despised and rejected by men” (Isa. 53:3 ESV), and Paul gives testimony that he was likewise treated (1 Cor. 4:9–14; 2 Cor. 4:8–9; 6:4–10; 11:23b–27). The servant is depicted as having a marred appearance, to which people reacted with shock (Isa. 52:14); Paul reports that the Galatians and Corinthians had similar responses to his visage (Gal. 4:14; 2 Cor. 10:10). The servant was afflicted and stricken by God (Isa. 53:4); Paul likewise suffered bodily afflictions (2 Cor. 12:7; Gal. 4:13). The servant was punished with stripes (Isa. 53:5 KJV); Paul also was flogged with whips and rods (2 Cor. 11:24–25). Just as Paul would have seen these prophesied sufferings of Christ as comparable to his own, so would he have seen his own as predestined.[22](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Both the sufferings of Christ and of Paul further the act and message of reconciliation (Clark 2015: 154).

Christ began to fulfill the Isaianic prophecy to Israel, and Paul completed the fulfillment in his mission to the Gentiles. The understanding of prophesied Jewish and Gentile salvation as integral to understanding “the filling up” will be elaborated on further below. Paul’s unique and definitive redemptive-historical “completing” of Christ’s sufferings is also pointed to by the parallel of Christ as “the head of the body, the church” (1:18), with Paul “filling up the afflictions of Christ . . . on behalf of the body, which is the church” (cf. 1:24; Clark 2015: 109). Similarly, just as Christ’s reconciling work is to “present you holy and blameless and without reproach” (1:22), so Paul as Christ’s apostolic agent is to “present every man[23](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) . . . complete in Christ” (1:28). Accordingly, Christ fulfills his part of the prophecy about reconciling the church (1:18, 20, 22), and Paul completes this reconciling ministry as Christ’s apostolic agent. This is in line with Paul’s unique apostolic role in being given the message of reconciliation in 2 Cor. 5:14–21 (as argued by Clark 2015: 133–57).[24](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) In fact, in the 2 Cor. 5 context Paul affirms that if the Corinthians do not reject him but are reconciled to him, it is evidence that they have been reconciled through Christ to God (see Beale 1989: 579). Thus reconciliation to Paul is not identical to but closely associated with reconciliation to God through Christ.

Not all Christians (but only Paul) have been given “the ministry of reconciliation” (2 Cor. 5:18), nor is every Christian (except Paul) given the unique apostolic “word of reconciliation” to proclaim (2 Cor. 5:19), nor are all believers (only Paul) serving as “ambassadors for Christ” in giving the message “Be reconciled to God” (2 Cor. 5:20). Just as Isaiah proclaimed himself as unclean as all other Israelites and was provided with an atonement that would be required for any other Israelite (Isa. 6:5–7), this does not suggest that his subsequent commission as a prophet (Isa. 6:8–10) was not uniquely applicable to Isaiah alone. And so it is with Paul.[25](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Consequently, Paul’s and Christ’s “afflictions” are (1) qualitatively different from the sufferings of saints in general; (2) the “afflictions” have the same general goal in that they both achieve reconciliation, especially in “presenting” saints to God at the eschaton (Col. 1:22, 28); (3) the “afflictions” are qualitatively distinct in that their roles are not the same: Christ’s suffering in his life and his atoning work led to reconciliation, and Paul’s suffering led to that reconciliation being uniquely proclaimed. Paul, says the Lord, “is a chosen instrument of Mine, to bear My name before the Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel, for I will show him how much he must suffer for My name’s sake” (Acts 9:15–16).[26](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Therefore, Paul’s completion of the Messiah’s suffering “for your sake” and on “behalf of his body, the church” consists in the fact that Paul brought the message of reconciliation to the Gentiles for their great benefit, a ministry that did not occur through the earthly Christ’s ministry. The unique apostolic role of Paul will continue to be seen in 1:25–29.

[1:25]

Paul now explains that his suffering for the benefit of the Colossians and the broader “church” (v. 24)[27](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) is expressed through him being a “servant” (διάκονος, diakonos). The Greek word can refer to one who is an authorized agent to transmit an important message. But the word also can refer to one who “serves” another who is a superior. Both ideas are in mind here.[28](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Thus Paul is an apostle commissioned faithfully to convey the gospel and to do so as a servant of God. Both these nuances of the word occur elsewhere in Paul (1 Cor. 3:5; 2 Cor. 3:6; 6:4; Eph. 6:21), including elsewhere in Colossians (Col. 1:23; 4:7). In fact, Paul here is elaborating on what he has just said in 1:23, that he “became a servant” (CEB) of the gospel that he has proclaimed to the readers and others: note the virtual verbal repetition of οὗ ἐγενόμην ἐγὼ . . . διάκονος (hou egenomēn egō . . . diakonos) from 1:23 here in verse 25 (ἧς ἐγενόμην ἐγὼ διάκονος, hēs egenomēn egō diakonos). The point of the repetition is now to emphasize this servanthood and to show that it involves suffering on his part for the sake of God’s people.

Paul became a “servant [minister]” because he has been commissioned for a “stewardship” from God (“of God” in v. 25a being a genitive of source, though many take it as a subjective genitive). The word οἰκονομία (oikonomia) can be rendered “stewardship” or “household management.” The idea is that one who manages the affairs of a household (οἰκονόμος, oikonomos) for the owner is one who has received a stewardship (οἰκονομία). This is its typical use in classical and Hellenistic Greek (cf. BDAG 697–98, s.v. οἰκονομία; in rabbinic writings the “son of the house,” a slave, is the household manager). In the LXX, οἰκονόμος can refer to a manager of the Israelite king’s palace or household.[29](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Closely linked, οἰκονομία indicates the office of such a management (Isa. 22:19, 21). Likewise in the NT, οἰκονόμος can refer to one who manages the affairs of a household for the owner.[30](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

In Ephesians οἰκονομία occurs three times (1:10; 3:2, 9), each time implicitly or explicitly referring to Paul’s stewardship of announcing the “mystery” of Christ and the gospel, which focuses on the unity of Jew and Gentile (cf. Eph. 2:11–22 with 3:1–10, esp. 3:6). That Paul was “a servant according [κατά, kata] to the stewardship from God given to him”* (Col. 1:25; cf. Eph. 3:2–3; 3:7–9) indicates that his servanthood was to be carried out by performing the responsibilities entailed by the stewardship (or his servanthood came about because of the stewardship).[31](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The nuance of God’s plan is included in οἰκονομία in that Paul’s calling was part of God’s planned management of the latter days, which was determined long ago (cf. O’Brien 1982: 81) and kept hidden as a mystery, but now has been revealed. Thus οἰκονομία in Colossians (and Ephesians) expresses the ideas of an office of management and the execution of the management, both of which concern management of God’s revealed end-time mystery or plan (cf. similarly Reumann 1967: 165; BDAG 697–98).[32](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) That Paul is a “servant” commissioned for such a management reflects the typical picture of a slave or servant who was appointed to manage a master’s household affairs.[33](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Paul’s stewardship of the mystery in Ephesians, as in Colossians, also entails him suffering “tribulations on behalf” of the readers. Thus, here in Col. 1:25, Paul has been given the responsibility as a servant to manage the affairs of the church by faithfully proclaiming the gospel despite and through suffering. In line with Ephesians, the very next verse labels the “word of God” from verse 25 as a “mystery” (v. 26) whose content concerns Gentiles (v. 26) and Christ (v. 27; the significance of the Ephesians parallels will be brought out in vv. 26–27).

Paul explains that his “stewardship from God which has been given”* to him is “for the benefit [of]” (εἰς ὑμᾶς, eis hymas; cf. Harris 2010: 61) the readers, underscoring again the same idea already repeated twice in verse 24 and reflecting further the parallel in Eph. 3:13, noted above.

Paul defines his stewardship as the commission to “fully carry out the preaching of God’s word” of the gospel (v. 25b). It is viable to translate this as “to fulfill” (so numerous translations), which can be understood in the sense of “fulfilling the word [i.e., promise or prophecy] of God” from the OT (see the additional note on 1:25). However, this rendering may not be best since the closest verbal parallel is with Col. 4:17: “Take heed to the ministry [διακονίαν, diakonian] that you have received in the Lord in order that you should fulfill it [πληροῖς, plērois],”* which does not appear to have a prophetic nuance. Both verses have in common the διακονία/διάκονος (diakonia/diakonos) word group, a ministry received from God, and the use of πληρόω (plēroō). Since the idea of “fulfilling” without a prophetic sense in 4:17 is clearly that of faithfully executing or completing a ministry, it appears quite plausible that the same sense is to be found in 1:25.[34](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

But in light of the immediate context of Col. 1:15–24 and 1:26–29, there appears to be a prophetic notion in πληρόω in 1:25, even if it is best understood here in the sense of execute or “fully carry out” the (prophesied) task of preaching the gospel (e.g., NASB). A better translation than any of the above may be prophetically “completing” (cf. NJB, NET) the commission of preaching God’s word or “fulfilling the word [prophecy] of God” (see the additional note on 1:25). If so, the idea would be that of Paul “completing” the prophesied task of announcing God’s end-time salvation, a task that was begun by Jesus as the predicted Isaianic servant. There is the very same prophetic idea in a very close parallel use in Rom. 15:19 (see the additional note on 1:24).[35](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

[1:26–27]

“The word of God” at the end of verse 25 is now identified as “the mystery” (τὸ μυστήριον, to mystērion). The Greek word is sometimes translated with such renderings as “secret,” “secret purpose,” “hidden truth,” or “secret plan of salvation” (cf. Harris 2010: 62 for the options), though all connote the same idea. “Secret” could be misleading, since as we will see, the Greek word does not express something that was completely unknown but a notion of making much clearer something in the OT that was to some small degree partially understood in the OT itself. Accordingly, “mystery” may be more preferable a rendering, since it is more susceptible of referring to something that may not be completely unknown.[36](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The word “mystery” occurs again in 1:27 and 2:2, where the “mystery” is identified to be “Christ.” “Mystery” appears finally in 4:3, where Paul asks for prayer that he would be given opportunities to “speak forth the mystery of Christ.” This could be rendered “the mystery which is Christ” or “the mystery about Christ.” Though “mystery” is not defined in 1:26, the three remaining uses in the letter define it to be “Christ,” which is likely also the case in 1:26. Verse 26 concludes by saying that this “mystery [was] hidden from [former] ages and generations, but now has been manifested to His saints [holy ones].”

The word “mystery” (τὸ μυστήριον in the LXX and רָז, rāz, in the MT), with a prophetic and eschatological sense, occurs in the OT only in Dan. 2 (2:18–19, 27–30, 47).[37](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) There the revelation of the “mystery” refers to the hidden interpretation of the king’s dream, the content of which was a giant statue representing four world kingdoms. A rock is cut out without hands and smashes the statue, and the rock grows into a mountain and fills the earth. The rock is understood to represent God’s kingdom, which will defeat the evil kingdoms of the world in the end time. The primary point of the dream was that in the end times God will destroy the kingdom of evil and establish his own eternal kingdom (2:44–45).

The use of “mystery” elsewhere in Jewish apocalyptic and Judaism is associated with eschatological issues in general, and sometimes with the end-time coming of a messianic figure in particular, occasionally in association with Dan. 2 (e.g., 2 Esd. [4 Ezra] 12:37–38; 1 En. 46; 52; 71; Qumran, e.g., 1QS 9.18–19; 1QpHab 7.5–14).

“Mystery” (τὸ μυστήριον) occurs twenty-eight times in the NT, where it is linked to eschatological issues, often with respect to the prophesied kingdom. A feature noticeable in many of the occurrences is that the word is directly linked with OT quotations or allusions (sometimes from the book of Daniel itself). In almost all of these cases, at least, “mystery” appears in order to indicate two things: (1) OT prophecy is beginning fulfillment, and (2) this fulfillment is unexpected from the former OT vantage point. With respect to this last point, it is apparent that various NT authors understand OT texts in the light of the Christ event and under the guidance of the Spirit, leading to new interpretative developments though still not out of line with the OT idea. Thus, though Daniel may not always be in mind in all of these NT uses, the notion of an eschatological prophecy needing further inspired interpretation is. While such a notion appears not always to be directly alluding to Dan. 2, this idea of “mystery” probably has its ultimate origin there.

There is not space to elaborate on the uses in Judaism or the majority of uses elsewhere in the NT nor to defend the thesis just laid out, though I have done that elsewhere, especially with respect to Qumran and the NT uses.[38](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) How do these uses of “mystery” in Daniel, Judaism, and especially elsewhere in the NT relate to those in Col. 1? The wording of the “mystery” phraseology in Col. 1:26–27 appears to be based on the verses in Dan. 2.

The above underlined five lexical combinations (“this mystery,”[39](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) “hidden,” “made known,” “wisdom,” and “understanding”) are found in biblical and Hellenistic Greek only in Dan. 2; Eph. 3:3, 9; and Col. 1–2. For example, the phrase “this mystery he [God] made known” occurs only in Dan. 2:30 Theod. (MS 230, ἐγνώρισεν τὸ μυστήριον τοῦτο, egnōrisen to mystērion touto)[40](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) and Col. 1:26–27 (γνωρίσαι . . . τοῦ μυστηρίου τούτου, gnōrisai . . . tou mystēriou toutou).[41](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This last phrase from Dan. 2:30 by itself may be an allusion.

These combined unique verbal correspondences go beyond mere general apocalyptic terminology.[42](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) Most commentators, however, do not see an allusion to Dan. 2, apparently not detecting the unique verbal parallels. Therefore, they do not discuss the context of Dan. 2 as background for this passage. However, there is sufficient basis for positing an intentional allusion without the danger of parallelomania, which then warrants asking how Paul might have seen the Daniel background informing his exposition here. As in Eph. 3, so also here the unique combination of wording expresses eschatological realities that are thematically likewise in line with the end-time focus of the Dan. 2 “mystery.”[43](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Daniel concerns the establishment of the eschatological kingdom, and Ephesians and Colossians focus on the end-time messianic king (christos) and the eschatological nature of the “mystery”[44](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) revolving around him. The explicit identification of the “mystery” in Col. 1:27 as “Christ in you [the Gentiles],”and the similar identification of the “mystery” as Christ in 2:2, points to the same kind of mystery as in Eph. 3: Jews and Gentiles are fused together on a footing of complete equality by means of corporate identification in Jesus the Messiah, the true Israel:[45](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) they are in Christ and Christ is in them (presumably through the Spirit). This is a “mysterious” fulfillment of Daniel’s prophecy[46](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) because it is not clear in the OT that Israel’s theocratic kingdom would be so thoroughly transformed that it would find its continuation only in the sphere of the end-time, Israelite messianic king himself.[47](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This may well have been hinted at by the four-kingdom parallel in Dan. 7, where the Son of Man’s reign replaces the image of the stone of Dan. 2. There in Dan. 7, the Son of Man represents the saints of Israel, so that what is true of him is also true of them and vice versa (see further Beale 2011: 393–96). This link between the Dan. 2 stone and the Dan. 7 Son of Man is explicitly made by 2 Esd. (4 Ezra) 13:3–7, where the Dan. 7 Son of Man figure “carved out for himself a great mountain [grown from a stone] and flew up upon it.”

Is this revealed mystery something that was completely hidden in Daniel and the OT, or was it partly revealed? Colossians 1:26 might seem to indicate that it was entirely hidden: “the mystery which has been hidden from the past ages and generations, but has now been manifested to His saints.” It is best, however, to see this as parallel to Eph. 3:4–5, where “the mystery of Christ . . . in other generations was not made known to the sons of men, as [ὡς, hōs] it has now been revealed to his holy apostles and prophets.” The ὡς directly preceding “now” (in Greek) indicates that Ephesians views the revelation of the mystery there as one of degree. The content of the mystery was evident to some small degree in the OT, but “now” it is fully revealed. There is no reason not to see the same transition from partial to full revelation in Col. 1:26, especially since we have seen that the inclusion of the Gentiles in verse 27 was something partly revealed in Dan. 2. In fact, even in Dan. 2 and 4, Daniel’s revealed interpretation of the mystery about Nebuchadnezzar was a clarification or fuller revelation of something about which the king had some partial knowledge (see the additional notes on 1:26–27). But what does not appear to have been revealed at all is how Gentiles become members of the end-time Israelite kingdom on the same footing as Jews through identification with the Messiah and not the nationalistic tags of the law. However, even this may have arisen from some seedlike ideas in the OT (see the additional notes on 1:26–27).

The mention of “now” (νῦν, nyn), in contrast to former “ages and generations,” indicates that the revelation of the mystery is part of the inauguration of the latter days. Paul elsewhere uses “now” (νῦν) with an inaugurated end-time sense (Rom. 8:18; Rom. 13:11–12; 2 Cor. 6:2; 2 Thess. 2:6).[48](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The above-mentioned parallel with Eph. 3:5, as well as 3:9, and its use of “now” is significant since the word in Eph. 3:5 and 10 is developing an inaugurated sense of “the fullness of the times” from Eph. 1:10, which is the time when the “mystery” there was “made known.”[49](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) This use of “now” continues the “now” (νυνί, nyni) in Col. 1:22, where “reconciliation” is part of the commencement of the latter days.[50](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

Colossians 1:27 explains the mystery, only briefly introduced in verse 26. Several genitival phrases here are difficult to define precisely (see the third additional note on 1:27). Despite this difficulty, the point of verse 27 is that “the glory of this mystery among the Gentiles” is “Christ,” who is “the hope of glory.” Is this to be understood as the “mystery [Christ] [preached] among the Gentiles” or more straightforwardly as “the mystery [Christ] [displayed] among the Gentiles,” since they have accepted him by faith? There is not much difference of meaning here, but the directly following phrase “Christ in you [the predominant Gentile church]” favors the notion of a focus on Christ’s actual presence being among the Gentiles and the translation “the mystery [Christ] [displayed] among the Gentiles.”[51](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) But this is not a mere presence among the Gentiles as a corporate group but “in” the Colossian Gentile Christians in particular and in each believing person among these Gentiles, as verse 28 highlights by expanding on the plural “you” (ὑμῖν, hymin) with three repetitions of “every [hu]man” (πάντα ἄνθρωπον, panta anthrōpon). “Christ in you” thus emphasizes that the mystery of Christ has included the Gentiles along with believing Jews,[52](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) whereas Gentiles had been excluded from the OT covenant community where God’s promissory revelation was experienced (cf. Calvin 1972: 322). Of course, a remnant of believing Jews are included in the new covenant community, along with Gentiles, to whom the mystery is being revealed (v. 26). Formerly Gentiles were “separate from Christ” and “having no hope” (Eph. 2:12), but now Christ “is in” them, and they possess “the hope of glory,” which is Christ (Col. 1:27; so Eadie 1856: 101). Such presence of Christ in individual Gentiles occurs through the Spirit (see Eph. 2:21–22; cf. Rom. 8:9). Thus “every [hu]man” focuses on Gentiles but certainly includes a remnant of believing ethnic Jews.

Thus, while it is true that the above Ephesians parallels in context highlight the Gentile-focused mystery more than Colossians,[53](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) the idea of the mystery being concerned with Gentiles still carries over into Colossians at this point. The earlier references to the worldwide effect of the gospel (1:6, 20, 23) have been leading up to this explicit statement about Gentile inclusion. And Col. 3:11 later highlights the same thing: the renewal of people occurs in the sphere of Christ “in which there is no . . . Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave and freeman, but Christ is all and in all” (see on 3:9–11).

Christ is “the hope of glory” for the believing Gentiles. The phrase is an objective genitive, “hoping in the glory.” Colossians 3:4 is the nearest parallel that illumines the idea here: since “Christ . . . is [the] life” in which believers are bound up, when “he should be manifested” at his final coming, so will they “be manifested in glory.”* Thus their present trust in and union with Christ’s resurrection life and “Christ in you” is what gives them “hope” to share “glory” with Christ at the consummation.

Paul’s focus on Christ as the specially revealed mystery probably counters the false teachers’ claim that special revelatory visions of heaven justify their view that self-effort supplements faith in Christ, which comes by grace (Sumney 2008: 105–6).

[1:28]

In response to the revelation of Christ as messianic king among the Gentiles and “the hope of glory,” Paul “announces” this mystery, since it is his “stewardship” to fulfill as a “servant” of God (cf. 1:25). This verb “announce/declare” (καταγγέλλω, katangellō) refers to Paul’s preaching, as in Acts, and is used only by Paul in his writings to refer to his or others’ proclamation of the gospel (so, e.g., O’Brien 1982: 87). The adverbial participles “admonishing” and “teaching” explain the manner by which Paul announces the mystery of Christ. He admonishes “all men” and teaches “all men.” The repetition of “every [hu]man” (πάντα ἄνθρωπον, panta anthrōpon) after each participle and a third time at the end of the verse underscores that the revelation of Christ in the latter days (cf. the eschatological “now” of v. 26) has been designed to extend the kingdom of Christ and his salvation to the Gentiles throughout the world.[54](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) In this respect, “all men / every human” develops the preceding mention of Christ being “among the Gentiles” (v. 27) and the earlier note that the gospel has been “proclaimed in all creation” (1:23; so likewise in 1:6, “in all the world”). This emphasis on universality and “wisdom” for all may be a contrast to the notion of the local mystery religions, in which the esoteric mysteries were reserved for an elite informed group and which may even have been a feature of the false teaching and pseudo-wisdom in Colossae (see Lightfoot 1875: 169–71; see on 2:4; cf. O’Brien 1982: 88).

On the other hand, “every man” also includes within it reference to that remnant of Jews who came to faith in Christ, along with Gentiles.

Paul has prayed that the readers would be “filled with the knowledge of His [God’s] will in all . . . wisdom [ἐν πάσῃ σοφίᾳ, en pasē sophia]” (1:9), and now he likely sees his efforts of “admonishing” and “teaching . . . with all wisdom [ἐν πάσῃ σοφίᾳ]” in 1:28 as one of the ways his prayer will be answered.[55](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) The same verbatim expression from 1:28 is used again in 3:16, presumably because Paul thinks his prayer will also be answered if the believers “let the word of Christ richly dwell within . . . , with all wisdom teaching and admonishing one another [ἐν πάσῃ σοφίᾳ διδάσκοντες καὶ νουθετοῦντες ἑαυτούς, en pasē sophia didaskontes kai nouthetountes heautous].” The “wisdom” of 3:16 is found in OT “psalms and hymns and spiritual songs,” which are OT psalms or based on such psalms,[56](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21) part of the Bible of the early church.

The “wisdom” of 1:28 likewise originates from the same sources, though, as we will see, Christ is the zenith point of the OT revelation (2:3), so that this older revelation must be seen through a Christ-centered lens (just as Col. 3:16 says that the “word of Christ” is expressed in the OT “psalms and hymns and spiritual songs”). He is the full source of wisdom. The verb νουθετέω (noutheteō) probably specifies part of what the wise “teaching” is about: “counsel about avoidance or cessation of an improper course of conduct” (BDAG 679, s.v. νουθετέω). This definition is relevant, given the directly following references to cautions about false teaching in 2:4 and 2:8. “Stand perfect [τέλειος, teleios]” in Col. 4:12 confirms that this “wise” teaching and counsel is a condition of the believer being “fully assured” about and following “all the [true] will of God” (see Lohse 1971: 78), despite being confronted with falsehood.

The purpose of Paul’s task to “announce” this mystery to “all men in all wisdom” is in order to “present all men complete in Christ.” The word for “complete” is τέλειος (teleios), which Paul uses elsewhere to refer to believers who are “mature” (1 Cor. 2:6; 14:20; Phil. 3:15; so also Heb. 5:14). The use in Eph. 4:13 is closest to that here: “until we all attain to the unity of the faith, and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to a mature man, to the measure of . . . the fullness of Christ.” Both in the Ephesians text and here the idea is that of an eschatologically consummated “full-growth” or “maturity” (cf. BDAG 995, s.v. τέλειος 2). This end-time growth to maturity in Christ is explained further in Eph. 4:15: “We are to grow up in all aspects into Him” (cf. also Eph. 4:16). Believers have begun to be identified with Christ in the inaugurated end-time phase of the revelation of the mystery, and at the completion of the latter-day epoch, they will be fully identified with or conformed to him (cf. Rom. 8:29), that is, “fully grown” in him. This goal of Paul’s latter-day “presentation” (παρίστημι, paristēmi) of believers “fully grown in Christ” (Col. 1:28) further explains 1:22, where Christ’s reconciliation was designed “in order to present [παρίστημι, paristēmi] you before Him [God] holy and blameless and beyond reproach.” In verse 22 we saw that this was sacrificial language, which may be continued with the use of τέλειος (teleios), since it can have that nuance in the OT (Exod. 12:5). Paul’s apostolic efforts are crucial in accomplishing Christ’s work of presenting believers before God on the last day. This full consummate eschatological maturity as an acceptable sacrifice likely includes the notion of moral and spiritual perfection (O’Brien 1982: 89).[57](https://www.notion.so/sand-22488f468092803e8718d67e9d0bc643?pvs=21)

[1:29]

How can Paul fulfill such a task of “presenting” believers “complete in Christ” at the eschaton? He does it by “strenuous labor,” literally, “I labor, struggling” (κοπιῶ ἀγωνιζόμενος, kopiō agōnizomenos) or, more paraphrastically, “I labor in a struggling manner” (“struggling” being an adverbial ptcp. of manner). Paul can use the verb (κοπιάω, kopiaō) and the cognate noun “labor” to refer to work done in service to God (see Beale 2003: 46–47, 161). This is a further explanation of verse 24, what it means that Paul suffers (so D. Moo 2008: 159). Paul’s fellow servant Epaphras also “struggles” to achieve the same goal as Paul (Col. 4:12, where ἀγωνίζω + τέλειος, agōnizō + teleios, also occurs). The two colleagues worked hard toward the same purpose.

Paul’s struggle, however, is not an autonomous effort on his part. He is able to continue this hard labor only by God’s power: “on the basis of [κατά, kata] his working, which works in power in me”* (κατὰ τὴν ἐνέργειαν αὐτοῦ τὴν ἐνεργουμένην ἐν ἐμοὶ ἐν δυνάμει, kata tēn energeian autou tēn energoumenēn en emoi en dynamei). The repetition of the synonyms “working,” “work,” and “power” emphasizes that Paul’s struggle for his readers is not rooted in his own ability but in God’s power working in him. Almost the same phrase is used in Eph. 3:7 (κατὰ τὴν ἐνέργειαν τῆς δυνάμεως αὐτοῦ, kata tēn energeian tēs dynameōs autou; cf. also Phil. 4:13), which is referred to as “the gift of [or which is] the grace of God.” God’s grace is how Paul is able to perform his ministry. Paul’s ministry is not performed by a synergistic activity involving his own independent contribution together with the help of God’s grace. Rather, as he says in 1 Cor. 15:10, “By the grace of God I am what I am, and His grace did not become empty in me, but I labored more than all of them, yet not I, but the grace of God with me.”*
