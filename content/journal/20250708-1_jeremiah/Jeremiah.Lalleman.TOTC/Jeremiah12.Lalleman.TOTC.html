Additional note: The plea for vengeance2

The plea for vengeance in verse 20 and elsewhere in Jeremiah (cf. 12:3; 18:19–23) is a stumbling block to many modern readers, and is certainly not an easy issue. In the Scriptures we often read of people who cry out to God asking him to avenge them (see e.g. Pss 58; 69; and end of Ps. 139, often regarded as one of the most beautiful psalms). There are a few things to note here (see also Peels 2003: 87–106). First, the prophet does not take revenge himself. He pleads his case before God, the only righteous Judge (v. 20; Rom. 12:19; Heb. 10:30). Secondly, Jeremiah is not just complaining about his own personal grief; his plea concerns what is done to him as a prophet. It is all about God’s message; it is God’s case. Similarly, we read in Revelation 6:10 that the souls of the martyrs cry out in a loud voice, ‘How long, Sovereign Lord, holy and true, until you judge the inhabitants of the earth and avenge our blood?’ God will not let injustice go unpunished; everything will be brought under his judgment.

Does this mean we can pray for revenge against our enemies? We are told in Romans 12:14, 19–20 to bless instead of curse, just as Jesus forgave his enemies (Luke 23:34). This is our primary task. But at the same time, we may commend our own or other cases of injustice to God, trusting that he is the righteous Judge who will bring righteousness and justice in the end.

ii. The prophet’s lament and God’s answer (12:1–6)

Context

Scholars have often linked these verses with 11:18–23 to make up the first ‘Confession’ (for structure of 12:1–6, see Context section of 11:18–23). But not all the words are spoken by the prophet in a kind of lament. Verses 5–6 are God’s answer. In psalms of lament, an assurance of faith is often given, but in this case God’s words are not comforting at all. This is just the beginning of Jeremiah’s suffering, and things will get worse. These verses address the problem of why the wicked prosper, a well-known theme in some of the psalms (cf. Ps. 73). Although this seems to be a different issue from 11:18–23, there is a connection if we assume that the wicked are the enemies referred to in the first lament.

Comment

12.1. This is the language of a lawsuit: Jeremiah has a case, as God had a case with Israel (2:9). TNIV has when I bring a case before you, whereas NRSV translates as if Jeremiah is bringing charges against God: ‘You will be in the right, O LORD, when I lay charges against you; but let me put my case to you.’ TNIV highlights that Jeremiah wants to speak to God about legal issues (a case is lit. ‘legal decisions, judgments’), because he knows God is righteous and acts according to a person’s deeds: so why does the way of the wicked prosper?

2. The righteous ones, whose lives are in accordance with God’s will, usually bear fruit (see Ps. 1; Jer. 17:7–8). So the prophet asks how it is possible that the wicked bear fruit, those who speak of God as if they live close to him but behave in a way that contradicts this. The Hebrew word for hearts is literally ‘kidneys’, which were regarded as the seat of the emotions (see comment on 11:20).

3. On God ‘knowing’ Jeremiah, see 1:5. God tests whether Jeremiah’s thoughts are genuine and in accordance with his will, as he does for everybody (see 11:20). Jeremiah asks for his enemies to experience what they had planned against him (11:19), to be slaughtered like sheep at the day of God’s judgment. Set them apart (lit. ‘sanctify them’) indicates that they are specifically designated for God’s judgment, an ironic use of the idea of sanctification. Jeremiah’s prayer can be seen as a cry for justice, rather than an utterance of personal revenge, and he commends his case to God (see also 11:20 and Additional note, p. 138).

4. The beginning of verse 4 can be translated: ‘How long will the land mourn?’ There are many other texts where nature is said to be affected by sin and its consequences. This is evident right from the beginning of the Bible (Gen. 3:17–18; see also Jer. 4:23–28; 9:10; Hos. 4:1–3). The land, and everything that lives in it, is part of the covenant, in that it shares the blessings and the curses (cf. Deut. 28). No further information is given, but the verse seems to describe a drought situation (see also 14:1–6). The moral behaviour of the covenant people has an impact on how the land as a whole suffers, including the animals. He will not see what happens to us – it is unclear whether he refers to God or Jeremiah. The LXX has added ‘God’. If Jeremiah is intended, his enemies expect him to have died before he can see what is happening to them. However, in accordance with other verses (e.g. Deut. 32:20; Pss 10:11; 73:11; Jer. 7:11), he probably refers to God. The people’s wickedness is partly that they ignore God. They think they will go unpunished and God will not see.

5–6. God’s answer can be summarized thus: be strong; things will only get worse. This is hardly a comfort, yet it is consistent with what was announced in Jeremiah’s call as a prophet (1:8, 17–19). If we consider 12:1–6 to be connected to 11:18–23, as is the case in its canonical context, we now know that those who participated in the plan of the people of Anathoth (11:21) are in fact Jeremiah’s own family and friends from his youth who belong to the prophet’s enemies. The people on foot cannot compete with the horses, which are much stronger. The safe country is Jeremiah’s birthplace, Anathoth, a relatively safe, small town compared to Jerusalem, where the prophet awaits further persecution and opposition from more important people, such as priests, prophets and kings. The focus moves from the smaller issues and problems to the more serious concerns. The thickets by the Jordan (lit. the ‘pride’, ‘splendour’ of the Jordan) are the thick bushes growing where the river holds plenty of water. Wild animals, such as leopards, wild bears, hyenas and wolves, would hide there (see 5:6).

Meaning

Like Job, Jeremiah addresses God directly. Who else should he turn to? He even brings the matter of injustice to God in a personal ‘conversation’. But in addition to Jeremiah’s personal suffering, the land and the natural realm are also suffering. Sin has widespread consequences. Jeremiah suffers with his people because of what has become of the once beautiful Promised Land. His own suffering arises from their betrayal of God (11:9).

The warning in verse 6 confirms what has been said in 9:8: beware of your neighbour, who may speak well of you, but will soon betray you. This message has become a reality in Jeremiah’s own life.

iii. God’s lament (12:7–13)

Context

This passage contains a lament expressed by God. Although it is not necessary to place it in the same historical context as the previous section, in its canonical context it forms a counterpart to the prophet’s lament in 12:1–4. Verses 4 and 11 correspond in their description of the suffering of land and nature. Judgment and disaster, caused by God’s enemies, have come upon God’s people, who in their stubborn behaviour have forsaken him and his commandments.

Comment

7–9. God laments that he is placing the one most dear to him into the hands of the enemies. The reason is found in the wider context (cf. 11:1–17): the people have forsaken God and served other gods. They have ‘left their husband’ and loved ‘other men’ (see Jer. 2 – 3). Yet Israel is still referred to as my house (v. 7). ‘House’ can also be God’s temple (11:15), but here it means people. They are called my inheritance, ‘the beloved of my soul’ (the one I love, TNIV). In verses 7–9 my inheritance is repeated three times. The repetition of these possessive adjectives in the first person (see also my vineyard and my pleasant field, v. 10) strengthens the impression of God being hurt and emotionally moved by the unfaithfulness of his people (see also 11:15; 31:20; Hos. 11:8). God’s people are compared with a roaring lion in their protest against God. The second image of a speckled bird of prey is more complex, since the word for speckled occurs only in this verse. LXX reads it as ‘hyena’ (see NRSV) and the Hebrew word for ‘bird of prey’ as ‘cave’ or ‘lair’. Judah would then be like a hyena’s lair, full of the carcasses of her enemies. Yet speckled bird of prey is a good translation, with Judah becoming the prey of other birds. Lundbom (1999: 656) points out that there is a chiastic structure in vv. 8–9: lion – bird of prey – bird(s) of prey – beasts. That God now ‘hates’ his beloved means that he is giving her over into the hands of her enemies – a striking contrast here between love and hate.

10–13. Israel is described as God’s ‘portion’ (twice in v. 10, NRSV; field, TNIV), whereas God is called Israel’s portion in 10:16. The land has been made desolate by many shepherds (shepherd often means ‘king’ or ‘leader’; see Jer. 23:1–4). In this verse the shepherds are the rulers of foreign enemies who attack Judah. TNIV uses the future tense, but it can also be translated in the past tense. In both cases, however, it is a prophetic judgment on the people’s behaviour. Verse 10 ends with a Hebrew word meaning ‘desolation’ or ‘desolate’. This theme is continued in verse 11, which contains three forms of the same root. There are also a few other words in verse 11 with the same sound. All this strengthens the impression of hopelessness and destruction. TNIV’s translation that the land is parched (v. 11) is better rendered as ‘it mourns’ (cf. v. 4). No-one seems to care about what is happening or is prepared to change. The barren heights in the desert are also mentioned in 3:2 and 4:11. The enemy is invading through the desert area, but heights may also imply the places of idolatry where Israel used to worship, which are now places of danger. In his anger, God is turning against his own people; he is the one behind the devastating actions of the enemies. Despite all the hard work, there will be no harvest or fruitfulness, and the whole land will suffer under the covenant curses (cf. Deut. 28).

Meaning

After Jeremiah’s lament comes God’s lament. Jeremiah is disappointed by his family; God is disappointed by his beloved. Their sufferings are connected, because both are the result of disobedience by the covenant people. God’s lament is like a love song, as he mourns separation from the one he loves.

iv. Other nations (12:14–17)

Context

Although verses 14–17 are written in prose, unlike the poetry of the previous passage, and seem to deal with a different subject, there are several links: the message of these verses is intended for the enemies described in verse 12; judgment has come upon Judah for serving other gods and imbibing the teaching of the surrounding nations (v. 16); the catchword inheritance links verses 14–17 to verses 7–13.

Comment

14–17. These verses are a prophecy for the neighbouring countries which have caused God’s people harm. They are instruments in God’s hand, but that does not mean they will go unpunished. Israel is still called my people (for inheritance, see also verses 7–9). These nations and Judah will all go into exile. However, God will also make them return. Three things are clear: what happens to the other nations is closely connected with what will happen to Israel’s land and God’s people, from both a positive and negative point of view. Secondly, God apparently rules over all nations and has given them their inheritance, their own land (cf. Amos 9:7). In the ANE, various lands were believed to have their own gods, yet the God of Israel is quite different. He is not bound to one people or to one land; he rules over all the earth. Thirdly, the nations can become part of God’s chosen people if they worship his name (see also 3:17; Isa. 2:1–5). The other nations have led Israel astray to worship idols (i.e. swear by Baal). If they continue to do this, they will be uprooted and destroyed completely (see also 1:10; 18:7–10).

Meaning

God is in control, even when mighty nations seem to be much stronger than God’s chosen people. He is not restricted to one place or nation; he is the God of the whole universe. Justice will be carried out on a personal level (cf. Jeremiah’s plea for justice) and on an international scale. God’s justice is not confined to one nation – neither is his compassion!
