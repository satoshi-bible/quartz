N. Chapter of despair (20:1–18)

i. Pashhur’s name change (20:1–6)

Context

Jeremiah 20:1–6 continues the story of chapter 19 and is written in prose, unlike the subsequent lament which is in poetic form. (For the relationship between 20:1–6 and the passage that follows, see Context section, p. 176).

Comment

20:1–2. Pashhur the priest is the official in charge of the temple (‘the chief officer’, NIV) and, as such, he is responsible for law and order in the temple and the temple area (cf. 29:26). The message of disaster (19:15), coming even to Jerusalem (and the temple), is regarded as a threat to order. Pashhur has Jeremiah beaten for it (or does it himself) and puts him in the stocks, probably an instrument used to hold him in a bent position (cf. Acts 16:24). The Upper Gate of Benjamin belongs to the temple and is not to be confused with the city gate (37:13; 38:7). For the first time in the book, Jeremiah is referred to as the prophet. He is a messenger of God! This makes Pashhur’s act all the more serious. He is opposing a true messenger of God (cf. Amos 7:10–17 for the confrontation between the prophet Amos and the priest Amaziah).

3–6. After being held in custody for one night, Jeremiah continues to speak God’s word, repeating the message of doom (as in the previous passage), but also applying it to the personal situation of Pashhur, who receives the nickname Terror on Every Side (Heb. Magor-Missabib; cf. 20:10). Terror will be everywhere when the king of Babylon arrives, and the people, including Pashhur’s family and friends, will be taken into exile and killed. Later in 29:25–26, we read that Pashhur is no longer the priest-officer. Lundbom (1999: 847) comments that the word ‘friends’ is used ironically by Jeremiah, and translates: ‘I am making you a terror to yourself and to all your dear friends.’ In time of need these friends will be of no help. The word all in verses 4–6 emphasizes the all-encompassing nature of the judgment to come; it will affect everyone and everything. For the first time in the book, the ‘enemy from the north’ is mentioned by name (Babylon, four times). Apart from being a priest, Pashhur also appears to have been a prophet, but a ‘false’ one who may have prophesied ‘peace’, as did the others (6:13–14; 8:10–11). In several of the following chapters (23, 26 – 29), the theme of false prophets, and Jeremiah’s confrontation with them, is developed. This is a recurrent major topic in the book (see Introduction, p. 47).

Meaning

The physical punishment of the prophet is mentioned for the first time, although there are references to threats on his life earlier in the book (Jer. 11:18 – 12:6; 18:18–23). Jeremiah is now a public figure and is opposed by someone in authority and openly humiliated. In opposing Jeremiah the prophet (v. 2), Pashhur opposes God’s word, of which Jeremiah is a faithful messenger. But Pashhur will suffer the consequences of such behaviour.

ii. A prophet despairs (20:7–18)

Context

In its present context, this so-called ‘Confession’, one of the personal laments of the prophet, can be read as a reaction to his harsh treatment by Pashhur. The nickname given to Pashhur is now applied to the prophet himself (v. 10). This is the most intense and despairing lament of the whole book. Verses 14–18 may originally have been a separate lament, but it makes sense to keep them together (see below).

Comment

7–10. As in other laments (cf. 11:20; 12:1, 3; 15:15–16; 17:12–14; 18:19, 23), the prophet begins by addressing God directly, and then goes on to speak about God in the following verses. For the use of a verb in two different forms to provide greater emphasis, see the comment on 17:14. The verb deceived (‘enticed’, NRSV) has the meaning of ‘strongly persuade’ or ‘(sexually) seduce’ someone. In this context, the first meaning (‘strongly persuade’) is preferred. The verse refers to Jeremiah’s call and his protest against it. He was not deceived, for he knew it would be hard. God told him so and even removed the obstacle of his being too young to speak. He also reassured the prophet of his help and presence in times of need. In this ‘Confession’, however, the prophet expresses how he feels trapped because he cannot escape his ministry; his suffering is hardly bearable. Jeremiah’s enemies are ridiculing him because of his message of doom (20:1–2; also 18:18). In verse 8 the Hebrew reads, ‘Whenever I speak, I cry out – violence and destruction I shout’ (proclaiming violence and destruction, TNIV). This is the content of his message, but it may also imply that the prophet is crying out because there are plans of violence and destruction against him. Jeremiah was beaten and put in the stocks by Pashhur, and he knew his enemies were plotting against him (v. 10; see also 6:7). The prophet cannot hold God’s message inside; he feels compelled to proclaim what God wants him to say (v. 9; see 5:14; 23:29 for the word of God as a fire). I cannot at the end of verse 9 is the same verb as prevail in verses 7, 10 and 11. Jeremiah expresses his great sense of powerlessness: God is too strong for him, the prophetic message is overwhelming him and so are the prophet’s enemies. For Terror on every side! (v. 10), see also 6:25; 20:3; 46:5; 49:29 and especially Psalm 31:13, a psalm with which Jeremiah could have identified and which similarly alternates between negative and positive. This description may summarize Jeremiah’s message, but may also be a taunt name. Or it may express the prophet’s anxiety and be read as a kind of interruption of the verse. But it is probably a nickname, just as Pashhur’s is (20:3).

The friends are people Jeremiah knew well – those closest to him cannot be trusted (see also 12:6). The enemies try to deceive the prophet and to prevail (v. 10). The verb deceived is the same as in verse 7, and the word for prevail is also used in verses 7, 9 (cannot) and 11. Everyone seems to have power over the prophet and overrule him. God is too strong for him (v. 7), the prophetic message is making him weary (v. 9) and his enemies are plotting against him (v. 10). The sense of being overwhelmed is strengthened by the following phrases: all day long (vv. 7, 8); everyone (v. 7); all my friends (v. 10); Terror on every side (v. 10). There is the constant threat everywhere of people targeting the prophet.

11–13. The tone of the lament seems to change dramatically. However, this is not unusual in psalms of lament (see the above-mentioned Ps. 31). In the context of Jeremiah’s lament, the prophet seems to realize that, although he is indeed not stronger than his enemies, God certainly is. God examines people and ‘sees’ their inner being (cf. 1 Sam. 16:7), so he knows Jeremiah is innocent and his persecutors are guilty (v. 12; see also 11:20; 12:3; 17:10). The revenge his enemies want to take (v. 10) is turned against them in verse 12, using the same word (your vengeance). For this kind of prayer, see Additional note on p. 138. For [not] prevail in verse 11, see comment on the previous verse.

14–18. In their present position in the canon, we can interpret verses 14–18 as an even more desperate cry for help. Lundbom (1999: 869) comments on verse 14: ‘In this unreasoned outburst, Jeremiah at least stops short of cursing the divine name, which would have been blasphemous (Exod 22:28; Lev 24:10–16).’ Neither does he curse his father and mother, ‘which would have been a capital offense (Exod 21:17; Lev 20:9 …)’ (Lundbom 1999: 871). Instead, Jeremiah focuses on the day he was born and on the man who brought news of his birth to his father. He mentions his father and mother but does not curse them. It becomes clear that the prophet would like to be able to reverse his calling (cf. 1:5). Before he had even been formed in the womb, God had chosen him. Jeremiah wants to reverse his mother’s pregnancy, just as Job wants to blot out the day of his birth from the calendar (Job 3).4 The words ‘give birth, born’ are used repeatedly in this passage (three times in vv. 14–15), as well as the word ‘womb’ (three times in vv. 17–18), thus emphasizing the theme of Jeremiah’s cry. Also, several contrasting words are used in verses 14 and 15: cursed – [not] be blessed; cursed – very glad. The towns the LORD overthrew without pity are Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen. 19; see also Jer. 23:14; 49:18).

Meaning

Jeremiah cries out to God for help. God seems to be his enemy in verse 1, but soon he realizes his enemies on earth are to blame. They want to prevail against him. It is as if Jeremiah comes to a realization that it is better to be ‘prevailed’ over by God than by his enemies, so he commits his case to the Lord. He knows God cannot be blamed, but neither can the prophet fight against the enemies: God is the only one who can help and justify him. This ‘confession’ comes in the middle of the lament. However, the despair continues and, since he cannot blame God, Jeremiah curses the man who informed his father of his birth. That is where this lament ends. His complaint began by focusing on God and then his enemies. He then moves on to lament the very fact of his birth and even his mother’s pregnancy, which he sees as a curse rather than a blessing. If he had not been born, he would not have been a prophet. His birth is intimately linked with his calling as a prophet (Jer. 1). This inclusio reinforces the idea of chapters 1 – 20 as one cluster of prophecies within the book. The despair of the poem may reflect the time after Jehoiakim’s burning of the scroll: a hopeless situation, it seems, both for the prophet personally and for the people. If there is to be any help at all, it has to come from God (and it will! Cf. Jer. 30 – 33).
