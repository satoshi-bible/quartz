G. All beauty gone? (13:1–27)

i. A useless garment (13:1–11)

Context

This prose passage (13:1–14) describes one of several symbolic actions that Jeremiah is commanded to perform (cf. Jer. 16; 19; 27:2; 32; 35; Isa. 8:1; Ezek. 4 – 5; 12). Prophetic symbolic actions show God’s words in a visible and tangible form and thus bring home the message in such a way that nobody can escape it. There is an element of ‘making the message happen’, but it is not magic, since it is God who remains in charge of his word.

The passage can be structured as follows:

verse 1 God’s command
verse 2 Jeremiah acts in obedience
verses 3–4 God’s second command
verse 5 Jeremiah acts in obedience
verse 6 God’s third command
verse 7 Jeremiah acts in obedience
verses 8–11 God’s explanation to the prophet

Comment

13:1–2. There is some discussion about the exact object Jeremiah is ordered to buy. Some say it is a belt to tighten his clothes; others believe it is a loincloth, a loose-fitting garment close to the skin. A loincloth would express the closeness of God and his people (see v. 11). On the other hand, verse 11 also emphasizes that Israel is like an ornament, something beautiful, which fits better with the idea of a belt. The object is made of linen, which may relate to the fact that Jeremiah is from a priestly family (Exod. 28:39), although it was also worn by ordinary people. The linen may refer to God’s people being a ‘priesthood’ (Exod. 19:6). Commentators differ in interpreting the fact that the loincloth or belt should not touch water. It may be that it will deteriorate more quickly if it is not washed when dirty; the following verses elaborate upon this.

3–5. Some versions, such as NRSV and NKJV, translate ‘Euphrates’, whereas TNIV chooses Perath and gives the other option in a footnote. However, the Euphrates is too far away for the prophet to travel to, so this would make it a visionary event. There is a place called Parah close to Jerusalem and Anathoth, where Jeremiah comes from. It has rocks where the loincloth could indeed be hidden. At the same time, people may have heard the sound of ‘Euphrates’ in the word, which gives a hint about where the enemy who will judge Judah will come from, the area of the Euphrates and the Tigris (the enemy turns out to be Babylon).

6–7. The third order is to dig up the loincloth or belt, which has completely deteriorated and is now useless. The word now (it was ruined) is actually ‘Look! Behold!’, implying surprise.

8–11. Jeremiah has done what he was ordered to do, without any recorded words on his part. Once he has acted in obedience, the explanation follows. The loincloth or belt was ruined, just as God will ruin the pride of Judah and the great pride of Jerusalem. The temple and everything the nation was proud of will be destroyed by the enemy, and the people will be humiliated by being taken into exile. The ongoing idolatry is like decay – it has gradually ruined Israel’s beauty, just as the beauty of the new, pure white linen loincloth or belt had been ruined by decay. The only thing to do is throw it away (send the people away into exile). For Israel as God’s treasured possession (v. 11), see Exodus 19:5 and Deuteronomy 26:18–19. The closeness between God and his people is depicted as Israel being bound to him (see also Deut. 4:4; Josh. 23:8 where the same Hebrew verbs are used).

Meaning

In various ways the prophet has to bring the message home to the people that they have fallen short of God’s expectations. This was expressed in terms of marriage and adultery in Jeremiah 2; here Israel is visualized as God’s precious treasure, but she has fallen far short of this ideal, so there is a great sense of disillusionment (v. 11).

ii. Broken pieces, broken people (13:12–14)

Context

The connection with the previous passage is fairly loose, but both contain visual aids to bring home the message of judgment. There is also repetition of the Hebrew word for ‘ruin’ or ‘destroy’ (vv. 7, 9, 14). Verses 12–14 may have been spoken at a wine festival.

Comment

12–14. The commonplace image of jars filled with wine depicts God’s wrath (wine) being poured out on everyone – even the kings on the throne of David with whom God had made an everlasting covenant (2 Sam. 7). The name David is an implicit reminder that they have failed their calling. Other passages contain a similar message of God’s wrath making the people, and even the nations, ‘drunk’ – they will be filled not with joy, but with grief (see 25:15–16; 48:26; 51:39, 57).

Meaning

Jeremiah is turning an everyday event in what was probably a festive setting into a message of doom. A shocking image, but will it awaken those who still think everything is all right?

iii. Shame all over (13:15–27)

Context

Jeremiah 13:15–27 is a poetic section, which is loosely connected to the previous passages. The common themes – the announcement of judgment and the loss of pride – run through the chapter (vv. 9, 18, 20, 26), yet the historical background for the different verses may vary. In verses 15–17 the possibility of repentance is still open, so this can be dated before the burning of the scroll by Jehoiakim (see Introduction, p. 24), whereas in verses 18, 19 and probably 20 the king and the queen mother are addressed (Jehoiachin was led into exile in 597 together with his mother and many others).

In verses 21–27 Jerusalem is addressed using the second-person feminine (vv. 22, 26–27). This form is also used in verse 25, although we may assume Judah to be the addressee of verses 24–25, as is the case in verse 23.

Comment

15–17. Several contrasts are highlighted in this passage: hearing (v. 15) and not hearing (v. 17); darkness and light (v. 16). Furthermore, the Hebrew word for ‘cry’ and ‘tears’ is used three times in verse 17 (cf. 9:1, 18; 14:17). The prophet envisages that the people, God’s flock, will be led into exile if they do not repent. Verse 20 picks up the idea of the flock again (see also 23:1–4).

18–19. Jehoiachin and his mother lost their royal status when they were led into exile. They have been lowered, even humiliated, by this event. Their pride (v. 15) is gone. There is no escape from the enemy of the north, not even to the south/the Negev. The prophet envisages a completely desolate land, more so after the fall of Jerusalem in 587 than in 597. Exaggeration seems appropriate in the expression of despair here.

20. The proposed reading by the Masoretes changes the singular imperative into a plural (Look up and see), referring to more than one person (not apparent in English). Who is the addressee? If the reading is singular, it may be the queen mother (v. 18), although LXX adds ‘Jerusalem’, and the city is addressed as a woman, as is more often the case. If the forms are changed into the plural, the king and his mother may have been addressed. The ‘beautiful flock’ (NRSV) is literally the ‘flock of your [sing.] beauty’ and corresponds with the ‘beautiful crowns’ in verse 18 (the sheep of which you boasted, TNIV).

21. The first half of the verse is very difficult to translate. All the Hebrew words are familiar, but the word order makes a literal translation almost incomprehensible. Nevertheless, the TNIV and NRSV seem to be not too far off the mark. They differ in that the TNIV includes God as the one who appoints (brackets indicate that this is not in the original text), whereas the NRSV takes ‘they’ as the subject. A possible interpretation is: ‘Those who Jerusalem trusted as their allies and friends will rule over it in such a way that terror will strike.’ The image of a woman suffering in childbirth is used elsewhere to indicate anxiety, fear and distress (see 4:31; 6:24; 22:23; 30:6; 49:24).

22. Because of her unfaithfulness to God, she (meaning Jerusalem) will be humiliated. Such language expresses the idea of violent rape, which happened literally during wartime and exile (see also Isa. 20:4; Ezek. 16:39). The verb torn off (‘lifted up’, NRSV) is the same as the word for ‘going into exile’, emphasizing the threat of exile.

23. Two rhetorical questions express the idea that the people are so used to sinning (lit. they are ‘schooled’, trained to do evil). A ‘Cushite’, a dark-skinned African, cannot change his colour. Cush was probably located in northern Sudan/southern Egypt (cf. 38:7). A leopard cannot get rid of its spots. Can Israel do good? The implication is that they cannot – sinning has almost become part of their nature. The NRSV translates in the affirmative (‘Then also you can do good’), which must then be understood ironically: if the leopard can change his spots (which he cannot), then you might be able to do good!

24–25. The chaff is what remains of the grain. It is useless and the wind blows it away. Similarly, the people will be ‘blown away’ into exile. The term lot (v. 25), meaning ‘portion’ or ‘share’, can be a positive thing (e.g. land in Josh. 15:1; 16:1; 17:1), but here it is used negatively, as in Isaiah 17:14. The second word, translated as portion, has the sense of being ‘measured’ by God. God punishes the people, because rather than trusting in him, which is essential behaviour for the people of God, they have turned to ‘lies’. TNIV interprets this as false gods, but it could also include false prophets and other political or religious leaders (cf. 8:8, 10).

26–27. God will punish acts of unfaithfulness, idolatry and spiritual adultery or leaving God for others (which may also have had a literal meaning, including going after different political allies, Israel’s ‘lovers’; see Jer. 2), putting Jerusalem to shame. How long will you be unclean? sounds more like an utterance of despair than a reflection of hope, as suggested in the note of the NIV Study Bible.

Meaning

Two things are clear from this passage: nobody is excluded from judgment, whether it be the king, the queen mother or the ordinary people. Sin is widespread. Secondly, sin has settled into the people’s inner being. It sticks to them as spots to a leopard and has become part of their very nature. Jerusalem, the chosen city and pride of the nation, where idolatry and injustice have taken place on a grand scale, has become totally unclean, and her pride (v. 9) will be taken away.
