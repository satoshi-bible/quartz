D. Searching for the righteous (5:1–31)

Context

Jeremiah 5 continues the message of the previous chapter and expands on the theme of the people’s rebellion and sinful behaviour. The prophet has the task of searching for righteous people amongst the nation. Judgment in the form of the ‘enemy from the north’ is announced in this chapter, as in the previous one (see also Context, Jer. 4, p. 91).

Comment

5:1–2. The command to search everywhere in Jerusalem for a righteous person, so that God might forgive ‘her’ (Hebrew; translated this city in TNIV), is expressed in the plural. Jeremiah and others are to go to the narrow streets of Jerusalem, as well as to the wider squares. The possibility of forgiveness if a righteous person is found is reminiscent of Genesis 18:22–33. The truth, however, is that people are using the name of the Lord in oath making, yet they do not speak the truth; they are swearing falsely (a word often used in Jeremiah; see e.g. 9:5–6 and Introduction, p. 47). They cover up their lies by misusing God’s name.

3–6. The question at the beginning of verse 3 is not a real question, as if the prophet doubts whether God is looking for truth. Rather, it is intended as an affirmation, a reminder to the hearers that this is what God wants: the truth – the opposite of swearing falsely. Even God’s warnings (events such as Josiah’s early death or other disasters may be implied here) did not have any effect (v. 3). The people made their faces harder than stone – they were resistant to correction. They refused to repent (šûb; see also Hos. 11:5). The NRSV translates ‘turn back’; there is frequent use of the word šûb in Jeremiah 3 (see also Introduction for the occurrence of the word throughout Jeremiah). The prophet assumes (I thought, using the emphatic form of ‘I’ in Hebrew) that the poor, the needy people in the lower echelons of society, are acting against God’s will because they have never been properly taught and do not know what they are doing (v. 4). However, what is truly shocking is that the leaders (lit. ‘the great ones’, contrasting with ‘the poor ones’ in v. 4) do not know God’s commandments either – they ought to know, but wilfully ignore them and have broken off the yoke and torn off the bonds (v. 5; cf. 2:20). The image is of an animal wearing a yoke with straps, rebelling against its master. The people have neglected God’s commandments, which would have kept them on the right track. Both poor and wealthy are guilty of this. Therefore (v. 6) introduces the verdict, the result of the analysis given in verses 4–5. The enemy approaching with military power is likened to a lion (see 4:7), a wolf and a leopard, animals of prey ready to devour their victims. (Lions were depicted on the walls of the Ishtar Gate, the main entrance to Babylon. Animal symbolism played an important part in the way the Assyrians and Babylonians presented themselves to the world, although we cannot assume that this was known to Jeremiah at that stage.) The enemy will execute God’s judgment on the rebellion, the wilful trespassing of God’s commandments, and the people’s backslidings (lit. ‘their turnings’, from the verb šûb, as in 3:22).

7–9. Using the ‘I-form’, God asks why he should forgive. Your children, Jerusalem’s inhabitants, have forsaken, ignored and neglected their God and exchanged him for other gods that are not gods (v. 7). God gave his people everything they needed in the desert and in the Promised Land – food, affluence, fertile land – yet they left him for others (they committed adultery) by worshipping the Canaanite fertility god, which probably involved prostitution (see 2 – 3:5; also 13:27). God will punish them and avenge himself, in the sense of setting things right as Judge. The word nation (v. 9) is normally used of a pagan nation, so here it is meant in a derogatory sense to indicate the depths to which the chosen people of God have fallen.

10–11. The enemy is exhorted to (lit.) ‘go up’ to the land’s vineyards, higher up on the hillside terraces, and to destroy them, but not completely. The vineyards can be interpreted literally, but Israel as a whole is also compared to a vineyard (see Isa. 5; Jer. 2:21; 6:9; 8:13; 12:10). TNIV interprets ‘for they’ (do not belong to the LORD) as for these people. If ‘they’ is a continuation of the previous verse, we may also read ‘their branches do not belong …’ In both cases, God’s choice and possession now falls into the category of ‘not of God’, just as the people have worshipped not gods (v. 7). The phrase utterly unfaithful (v. 11) translates two forms of the same verb in Hebrew, thereby adding emphasis.

12–13. They are the prophets of verse 13, who say that disaster will not come. He will do nothing is a very short expression in Hebrew which in the Old Testament occurs only here (lit. ‘not he’). The prophets mislead the people by saying that God will not bring about the oracles of judgment as proclaimed by Jeremiah, such as the one in verses 10–11. The prophets are but wind, or ‘will become like wind’. The word means both ‘wind’ and ‘spirit’ and can also refer to the Spirit of God. But these prophets are not inspired by God’s Spirit; they will disappear like the ‘wind’.

14–17. Therefore introduces the consequence of the previous analysis. TNIV has Because the people have spoken these words, but the Hebrew reads, ‘Because you have …’ This could refer to the prophets of verse 13. The remainder of this verse and the following verses contain pronouncements of judgment against the people, so ‘you’ at the beginning may be interpreted as referring to the false prophets, although the rest of the people are included in the judgment because of their part in believing them and being rebellious themselves (v. 11). I will make my words in your mouth a fire is spoken to the prophet Jeremiah (see 1:9–10; 20:9). His words of judgment are God’s words, and they are powerful like fire, contrasting with the useless words of the false prophets. Verses 15–17 once again announce judgment on the people in the form of an enemy (see 4:6, 16; 6:1, 22). The word ‘nation’ is used four times here (TNIV translates one instance as people and omits one occurrence altogether). The repetition sounds ominous: ‘I will bring a distant nation, an enduring nation (well-established and tough), an ancient nation, whose language you do not know/understand.’ Their quivers are like an open grave (v. 16): they are swallowing the dead (cf. Prov. 30:15–16). There is no opportunity for diplomacy, because the people do not understand their language. Babylon has not been mentioned so far, but it fits the picture and is referred to in other parts of Jeremiah. The Babylonians were indeed a strong, impressive military power, as can be seen in the archaeological findings.

In verse 17 the word devour (lit. ‘eat’) is used four times. The enemy is like a hungry wolf, swallowing everything he comes across. Nebuchadnezzar did indeed try to get rid of Judah as soon as possible, since the small kingdom was a barrier on the way to Egypt, and any rebellion would only hinder him in reaching his military and political targets. All this is very much part of what Deuteronomy 28:49–52 says in terms of curses with regard to breaking the covenant.

18–19. These prose verses reaffirm what was said in verse 10 and in 4:27. Verse 19 links the ‘serving of foreign gods’ to ‘serving a foreign nation in a foreign land’ (cf. Deut. 28:36). The punishment matches the crime (the so-called lex talionis; see Deut. 19:21). On the question and answer (v. 19), see also Deuteronomy 29:23–28 and 1 Kings 9:8–9.

20–25. The house of Jacob is usually a name for the northern kingdom, which was led into exile in 722. Here the words are spoken specifically to Judah, but the name Jacob reminds them of Israel’s history from the beginning, bringing home the fact that their relationship with God is a long-established one and should be taken seriously (see also 2:4). Announce and proclaim are plural imperatives (see 4:5). The people are foolish and senseless (lit. ‘without heart’). The heart was seen as the centre of feelings, thoughts and decision-making. The people’s foolishness is that they are missing out on good things by ignoring their God (see 4:22; 6:10). They are stubborn and rebellious and they are shutting themselves off from God’s message. The example from nature (vv. 22–25) is introduced by two rhetorical questions, which express the unheard-of matter of a people not respecting (fear in vv. 22, 24) the God of creation or living according to his will and decrees. The rest of creation is obedient to the boundaries he has set for them! The sea (v. 22), whose power was dreaded by the Israelites (other nations regarded it as a chaotic power and one of the gods), is controlled by God; he has set boundaries to its waves – and they ‘obey’! The implicit accusation that follows in verses 23–24 is that God’s people wilfully transgress the boundaries set for them in the commandments, even though they benefit from the good of creation. They do not acknowledge the Giver (Lalleman 2009). Verse 24 implies that God is the one who provides rain and fertility, not Baal (cf. Hos. 2:8). Verse 25 suggests that there has been judgment in the form of drought (see also 3:3; 14:1–6).

26–31. The wrongdoings mentioned in verse 25 refer to the sins of the rich who, like those who set traps for the birds, catch people and become rich at the expense of the poor (the Hebrew of v. 26 is difficult, but TNIV and NIV are followed here). The houses of those who rob others are full of deceit (i.e. they are full of treasures gained by deceit). This has happened despite the commandments to care for the powerless, the widow, the orphan and the weak in society (Exod. 22:22; 23:6; Jer. 7:6; 22:16; see also Isa. 1:15–17; Ezek. 22:12–13; Amos 2:6–7; 5:12). An example of someone gaining wealth by ‘setting a trap’ is King Ahab, who obtains Naboth’s vineyard by accusing him falsely (1 Kgs 21). In Jeremiah 22:13–17 King Jehoiakim is accused of exploiting the people by gaining wealth and a luxurious palace, in contrast with his father Josiah. Jeremiah 5:29 repeats verse 9 and focuses mainly on ‘social’ sins instead of idolatry, but both issues are equally important.

Finally, verses 30–31 address all groups of society: prophets, priests, people. The prophets prophesy lies (see vv. 12–13; 6:13–14; 23:9–40). They proclaim ‘peace’ when they should be announcing judgment because of the people’s unfaithfulness (v. 11). They do not speak God’s words, but their own. The priests rule by their own authority (lit. ‘at their hands’) can also be interpreted as the priests ruling in the way the prophets want them to (‘their’ as applied to ‘the prophets’). But what will they all do when judgment comes? The passage ends with this final question.

Meaning

The theme of God’s people ‘not listening’ (v. 21) and persistently going their own way instead of following God’s way is a frequent one in both the Old and New Testaments (see Isa. 6:9–10; Ezek. 2:5–7; 3:7–11; Matt. 13:13–15; Mark 4:12; Acts 28:25–27; Rom. 11:7–8). The prophet’s mission of searching for a righteous person (v. 1) is discouraging. Leaders and ordinary people, poor and rich alike, are all behaving in a way that contrasts with what is required of a covenant people. Even nature knows better!
