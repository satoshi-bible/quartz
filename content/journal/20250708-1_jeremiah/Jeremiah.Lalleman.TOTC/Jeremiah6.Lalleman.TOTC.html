E. Tested and rejected (6:1–30)

Context

Jeremiah 6 returns to the theme of chapter 4: the threat of the enemy from the north. Again, as in Jeremiah 4 and 5, the language is vivid, expressing the anxiety and horror of war. The people have been warned repeatedly by watchmen (6:17), the true prophets sent by God, but they did not listen (vv. 10, 16, 17, 28), a theme that also occurs in the previous chapter (see 5:21). In 5:1 the prophet is told to search for a righteous person; in 6:27 he is described as a tester of metals, assessing the people and their behaviour. Lundbom (1999: 447) points to the repetition of the word ‘reject’ in 6:30, 7:29 and 8:9.

Comment

6:1–5. Jerusalem is situated in the land of Benjamin, the tribe to which Jeremiah belongs. In Jeremiah 4:5–6 the people are urged to flee from the countryside to the fortified city for refuge, because the enemy is near. But here in 6:1, they are called to leave the city, even ‘the centre of the city’ (see NRSV), which is under siege and therefore unsafe. The trumpet (shofar) announces a warning of the coming attack (see 4:5, 19). The Hebrew words for Tekoa and sound are similar in sound and thus heighten the urgency of the call. The people are also commanded to send a signal of fire or smoke, so that everyone will know where to go. Two places of refuge are mentioned: Tekoa, south of Jerusalem near the Judean desert, is the birthplace of Amos (Amos 1:1). The exact position of Beth Hakkerem is unknown. It is somewhere south of Jerusalem and is also mentioned in Nehemiah 3:14. The disaster ‘looks down’ as it were (literal translation of the Hebrew; looms out, TNIV) on Jerusalem from the north (see 4:6). The word disaster can also mean ‘wickedness’, but that is not the meaning here, although obviously the two are related, since the coming disaster is a result of the people’s wickedness and they will have already heard of the connection. The terrible destruction is (lit.) ‘a great breach’ (see 4:6). The translation of verse 2 obviously differs in TNIV and NRSV, due to a different reading of the Hebrew. The NRSV reads beautiful as ‘pasture’ and uses a different Hebrew verb, translating ‘likening’ rather than destroy. The TNIV reading is well justified. The verb ‘to destroy’ can also mean ‘to silence’. The shepherds in verse 3 are the foreign rulers of the enemy. In the Ancient Near East a king was often described as a ‘shepherd’ (see also 23:1–4; Mic. 5:4; cf. Jer. 12:10). They approach Jerusalem and lay siege like shepherds who pitch their tents. The Hebrew word for pitch sounds like Tekoa and sound (v. 1), and the Hebrew words for shepherds and tending (v. 3) sound similar to disaster (v. 1). This use of assonance heightens the sense of tension as the enemy approaches. Verses 4–5 express the enemy’s eagerness to attack. The plan is to attack at noon, and there is disappointment (alas) when the daylight is fading. They are so keen to proceed that they even urge one another to attack at night. Prepare (for battle) means ‘sanctify’. War was regarded as ‘holy’; the gods were involved, and several rituals were performed before the attack (cf. 1 Sam. 13:8–9 for a similar situation in the Israelite army).

6–8. God himself is behind the enemy’s attack, so he is in fact attacking his own city, on account of her great wickedness and oppression. He encourages the enemy to cut down the trees and to build siege ramps, which are earthen mounds piled up against the city walls. Translations of verse 7 vary: TNIV has the verb pours (or ‘overflows with’), whereas NRSV has ‘keeps fresh’. Both readings point to the fact that evildoing is prevalent in Jerusalem. The pair of Hebrew words translated as violence and destruction recurs in 20:8, and can be understood as a cry of the innocent who are being abused by wicked people: ‘Violence and destruction!’ The sickness and wounds in Jerusalem are not caused by the enemy in this case, but refer to violence within the city. There is, however, still a chance for Jerusalem to change her behaviour and return to God. Otherwise, God will turn away (v. 8); the Hebrew used here sounds very much like Tekoa in verse 1 (see also comments above on v. 3). The desolate land where no-one can live is a regular theme in Jeremiah (see e.g. 4:7, 29; 9:11, 22; 12:10–11).

9–12. The first words of verse 9 read (lit.), ‘Glean, they will glean’, repeating the verb and thereby emphasizing the action required. TNIV has glean … thoroughly, and ‘they’ can be taken as an indication that the people as well as the prophet are commanded to glean. However, your hand is singular and refers to the prophet, so the NRSV reads the command ‘to glean’ as singular as well. Jeremiah 5:1 also has plural imperatives, so the form here is not unusual. Jeremiah (and others who are not specified) is commanded to glean once more the vine which is Israel (Isa. 5:1–7; see also 5:10). Lundbom (1999: 424) applies this verse to the enemies (as 5:10), who are to strip what is left of the people. The inference is not that some grapes could still be found, but rather that everything is to be stripped away. However, even if Jeremiah is the one to do the gleaning, the outcome will be negative (so also Holladay 1986: 213), for from the least to the greatest everyone has sinned (cf. v. 13). The prophet despairs (vv. 10–11): the people will not listen to his warnings which he speaks in God’s name. The people are said to have a (lit.) ‘foreskin’ on their ears; their ears are ‘uncircumcised’. They may belong to the covenant people by physical circumcision, but they do not listen to what that covenant requires of them (see also 4:4; 5:21; 9:26; Lev. 26:41; Acts 7:51). However, the prophet cannot stop speaking about God’s anger at their rebellious behaviour. He cannot hold it in (cf. 20:9). Verse 11b continues with an oracle of God, a command for Jeremiah to ‘pour out’ God’s wrath on everyone, young and old, husband and wife. The enemy will take control of their possessions and wives. God will stretch out his hand, not to show his power in rescuing his people from the Egyptians (Exod. 6:6; 15:12), but against them.

13–15. This oracle is repeated almost word for word in 8:10b–12, and 8:10a is similar to 6:12. Nobody is free of the guilt of unjust gain – neither the ordinary people (all of them, indicated by from the least to the greatest), nor the spiritual leaders, the prophets and priests. The term deceit is the Hebrew word for ‘lie’, ‘falsehood’, and is often used to describe prophets who prophesy peace instead of judgment (see Introduction; also 5:31; 23:16, 17, 25, 26). It indicates that both prophets and priests are acting in a dishonest way. The rebellious behaviour of the people and their neglect of the covenant will be fatal if repentance is not made. They are ‘wounded’, but their wounds are not treated seriously by the ‘doctors’ of the people, the prophets and priests, who just say everything is fine and apply soothing words, which has the effect of making things worse (cf. Jer. 30:12–15; also see Introduction for the use of ‘medical metaphors’, p. 42). Radical surgery is needed! Prophets were sometimes consulted in medical matters (see 1 Kgs 14:1–5; 2 Kgs 5:3, 11), and priests played an important role in diagnosing illnesses and assessing whether or not people were fully healed (Lev. 13 – 14).

16–20. Another warning and oracle (This is what the LORD says). The people are urged to stand at the crossroads where several roads meet. There they are to look for the ancient paths, which are the good way to the life set before them when the covenant was made (see also Deut. 30:15–20). Peace and rest (cf. v. 14) will be found there (cf. Matt. 11:29). The prophets are described as watchmen (v. 17) over the people, warning against imminent judgment by paying attention to the shofar announcing the approaching disaster (as in v. 1; for the prophet as watchman, see also Ezek. 3:16–21). Yet the people refused to take that path. The word for witnesses (v. 18) is translated as ‘congregation’ in the NRSV. Both are possible, but it makes good sense to see the nations here as witnesses, just as the heavens were in 2:12 (cf. Mic. 1:2). The earth is also summoned as a witness (v. 19). The disaster coming to the people is the fruit of their schemes, the result of their evil plans and actions. God will act as Judge and he will bring disaster (see v. 1; 4:6; 11:11; 19:3, 15; 23:12). The people have rejected my law, which may refer here to the written laws found in the book of Deuteronomy which was discovered under King Josiah. The law did not produce a lasting change of mind and attitude. Following God’s instructions would bring life and blessing. Verse 20 speaks of the precious gifts that the people thought would be a substitute for good behaviour. Sheba in south-west Arabia is mentioned as the source of incense (cf. Ezek. 27:22), and calamus, aromatic spice cane; was (probably) from India. This is also mentioned in Exodus 30:22–25. Neither burnt offerings sacrificed wholly to God, nor sacrifices in which the worshipper had a share as well, can put right what the people have spoiled by their behaviour (cf. Isa. 1:10–17; Amos 5:21–25).

21. Here is a new word from God which confirms what has been said earlier. It is shocking that God himself will make people stumble (see also Ezek. 3:20). The exact nature of the obstacles is not explained. Walking in the ways of the Lord results in a levelled path (see also v. 16 for the good way and rest for your souls; also 31:9, where God will lead his people on a level path where they will not stumble).

22–23. This oracle contains a lively account of the enemy from the north, similar to the vivid descriptions in 4:5–6, 13. The enemy is later identified as the Babylonians. Reliefs depicting warfare in the Ancient Near East, on display in the British Museum in London, match the description in these verses.

24–26. Anxiety, panic and distress are expressed in the same sort of language as in 4:31 (see comments there). The prophet voices the people’s terrifying experiences: We have heard … The phrase terror on every side (v. 25) recurs in 20:3 (referring to Pashhur), in 20:10 (about Jeremiah) and in the prophecies against the nations (46:5; 49:5, 29). The NRSV reads ‘my poor people’ (v. 26) as a translation of ‘daughter of my people’. TNIV has only my people, but the Hebrew indicates an element of sadness (see v. 23, Daughter Zion and comments on 4:11). Verse 26 opens with my (singular, which we take as the prophet’s words) and ends with us (the prophet speaking on behalf of his people; on v. 26, see also 4:8).

27–30. These words are spoken to the prophet. The Hebrew of verse 27 is complex (lit. ‘A tester I made you, among my people a fortress’, see KJV). The last word can be linked with Jeremiah 1:18 and the bronze and iron in 6:28. With a slight change, the second word (lit. ‘fortress’) means ‘a searcher’/‘a refiner’, namely of ore. The NRSV thus reads, ‘I have made you a tester and a refiner among my people’, which makes better sense than TNIV (and my people the ore). Jeremiah, like a tester of metals, is searching for what is valid amongst the people, what is honourable and noble in their behaviour. The rest will be defined as dross. The root of the noun, here translated as tester, is also used of God in 9:7; 11:20; 12:3; 17:10. God searches people’s hearts and tests their deepest motivations, thoughts and intentions. In 6:27 it is the prophet who tests on behalf of God. (See 9:3–9 for a description of the people similar to that in v. 28.) The people are like bronze and iron in their sins – strong-willed in their rebellion. They are hardened rebels; the verb is repeated in Hebrew for emphasis (‘They are all stubbornly rebellious’, NRSV). The prophet is compared to iron and bronze in his strength to resist his enemies (1:18), and he is positively distinguished from a bronze and iron people. As with verse 27, the Hebrew of verse 29 is not easy, but the meaning is that, despite the process of refining through fire, pure silver is not produced. Lead was added to silver to sort out the impure elements. Yet this process has not been effective. Despite the prophetic teaching, the people have not produced anything valuable in the eyes of God; nor have they changed their behaviour. The result is rejected silver, which cannot be used. That is the metaphorical name the people deserve. They have rejected God’s law (v. 19), and now they are rejected, for God has rejected them (same verb used in both cases). The Hebrew verb for [not] purged out (v. 29) sounds like the words sound and Tekoa (v. 1), thus forming an inclusio.

Meaning

Jeremiah had to search everywhere for a righteous person (5:1) but could not find one. In chapter 6 he is called to test the people to search for any good in them. The diagnosis is the same as in the previous chapter. Everyone – leaders, prophets, priests, young and old – all are living in a way that goes against the covenant relationship with God. In verse 19 the people are said to have rejected God’s law, with disastrous results. They are now the rejected ones, denying their covenant relationship with God by their words and deeds.
