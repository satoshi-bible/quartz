L. God and prophet versus people (18:1–23)

i. Object lesson at the potter’s (18:1–12)

Context

As in the previous chapter (vv. 19–27) and at the beginning of chapter 19, God commands Jeremiah to take action: Go and stand (17:19); Go down (18:2); Go and buy (19:1). In all these passages, the prophet is commanded to preach God’s word, and in chapters 18 and 19 this happens via an ‘object lesson’. The position of these chapters within the book means that they function as a repeated warning to the people, underlined by actions.

Comment

18:1–4. Verses 1–2 contain a commandment in the third person, and verse 3, changing to the first person, tells how the prophet obeyed. Since Jeremiah ‘went down’, the potter’s house was obviously at a lower level than the rest of the city, which was built on a mountain. People in the same profession used to live together, so there was probably an area where potters lived, just as there was a ‘bakers’ street’ (37:21). Jeremiah watches carefully what the potter is making at the wheel. This is a familiar picture even today: with his hands the potter moulds the clay into the intended shape, but the slightest wrong movement can make it fail, requiring the potter to start again from scratch. It is the potter who is in charge (as seemed best to him), yet the clay does not always end up as intended. The NRSV and TNIV readings suggest this is a one-off occasion, but the Hebrew can indicate that this is something that happens regularly, which is indeed the case in a potter’s workshop.

5–12. The interpretation of this object lesson starts with the house of Israel, a general name for the people of God, in this case specifically applicable to Judah in Jeremiah’s time. Moreover, in verses 11–12 it also ends with the inhabitants of Judah and Jerusalem, thus forming an inclusio, but in the intervening verses the reference is to any nation. The word for ‘nation’ (gôy) is used in its plural form for ‘the heathen’, the non-Israelite peoples. Israel looked down on ‘the nations’. Furthermore, Israel were required to be distinctive in their behaviour (Deut. 7:1–6). Yet in Jeremiah’s day, they behaved even worse than the others did (vv. 13, 15), and the message of judgment applies to them in the same way that it applies to ‘the nations’. The audience would have found it shocking to hear the word gôy alongside ‘Israel’ and ‘Jerusalem’: Israel is not as special she thought she was! It affirms that God is in charge of all nations, a recurrent theme in the book. Jeremiah was appointed as a prophet to the nations (1:5), and his message of ‘uprooting, tearing down, destroying, overthrowing, building and planting’ was meant for ‘nations and kingdoms’. Verses 7 and 8 pick up these very themes. The description of the potter’s work conveys the idea that God is in charge, and just as the potter has plans for his clay, so God has plans for the nations, including Israel. However, the behaviour of a nation, the ‘quality of the clay’, determines what God will do with it. God can use a nation for his purposes if the people obey, but if they persist in doing evil, the ‘pot’ is threatened with failure and is no longer fit for purpose. Because of Israel’s sinful behaviour, the ‘shape’ God has in mind for Judah is disaster. Verse 11 (‘I am a potter shaping evil’, NRSV) is from the same Hebrew stem as the ‘potter’ in verses 1–6. There is still an opportunity to change and become useful in God’s hands if the people repent, but the chances are very slim (v. 12). The Hebrew word for ‘repent, relent, change one’s mind’ occurs several times (relent, v. 8; reconsider, v. 10). This does not imply that God repents of something wrong, but that he changes his previous decision. He does so because of a nation repenting of its evil (v. 8: šûb meaning ‘return’, ‘repent’; see also Jon. 3:10). The people repent, so God ‘repents’.3 For the metaphor of God as potter, see also Isaiah 45:9 and 64:8.

Meaning

The object lesson of this passage is to bring home the shocking message that Israel cannot claim God’s support, regardless of their behaviour. A clear distinction is not even made between the people of God and other nations! The aim is to call Israel/Judah to change their ways, i.e. to repent and return to God. If they do so, final judgment can be prevented.

ii. How on earth! (18:13–17)

Context

This passage continues the theme of Judah’s stubbornness which began in the previous section. Israel is (again) placed alongside the nations (v. 13). The verb translated cease to flow in verse 14 sounds like ‘to uproot’ in verse 7. It may be that the consonants were mixed up and the order changed, giving the meaning ‘to dry up’, as is the case here. However, the use of this verb in its present form adds to the warning element of the text. The hearers or readers are alarmed by hearing these two words.

Comment

13–14. The perplexing situation of God’s people is emphasized in a series of questions. It is unheard of anywhere else in the world for a nation to forget its god. For Virgin Israel, see 14:17. Verse 14 is difficult to interpret, and the Bible versions offer a variety of translations. TNIV’s rocky slopes is an adaptation, and may also be read as ‘the mountain highland’. NRSV has ‘the crags of Sirion’, an old name for Mount Hermon. The rhetorical questions, expecting the answer, ‘No, of course not’, point to something in nature that is unchangeable, the natural order of things. The implication is that the people’s behaviour is unnatural, as previously highlighted in the examples from nature in Jeremiah 8:4–7 (cf. 2:11, 32). The second half of verse 14 also causes some problems. We follow Lundbom’s translation (1999: 822): ‘Can foreign waters dry up, the cool flowing streams?’ The ‘foreign’ (distant, TNIV) waters are those in Lebanon. Snow on the mountains of Lebanon and cool water running from them are natural events which can be taken for granted. However, the people’s behaviour is not natural at all.

15–17. The unheard-of behaviour of my people is set out in verse 15, followed by the consequent judgment. They have forgotten and ignored God (2:13, 32; 9:3, 6), and instead of, or as well as, worshipping God, they have served a ‘delusion’ (NRSV; worthless idols, TNIV), something of no substance, a ‘nothing’ (cf. Jer. 10). The comparison with cool rivers brings to mind the emptiness and barrenness of serving idols (see also 2:11–13). The land will become an object of horror, and those passing by will shake their heads in shock and disbelief (cf. 15:5; 19:8; 25:9; 29:18; 42:18; 49:17; 50:13). The wind from the east (v. 17) is the dry Sirocco from the desert, which can be scorching for human beings and all of nature. It is also a symbol of God’s judgment (see 4:11–12; 13:24). Just as the wind scatters, God will scatter his people into exile amongst other nations. In 2:27 the people turned their backs to God; now he is turning his back to them (note the contrast in Num. 6:25–26).

Meaning

The stubbornness of the people (see v. 12) is vividly portrayed in poetic form as something completely unnatural, unheard of amongst the ‘pagan’ nations. As a ‘natural’ result, judgment will follow.

iii. Threatening behaviour (18:18–23)

Context

This passage is one of the so-called ‘Confessions’ of Jeremiah, in which he utters his suffering as a prophet and cries out to God for help in a way similar to the psalms of lament (see Introduction, p. 51). There are links with the previous two passages, in that the adversaries refuse to listen to any of Jeremiah’s words (vv. 12, 18) because he prophesies disaster (vv. 13–17).

Comment

18. They are the opponents of Jeremiah. These people apparently quote a proverb in which they make clear that they do not need Jeremiah: they are content to obtain advice from the priest, the wise and the prophets. They plan to attack Jeremiah with their tongues to bring about his death; they want to accuse him falsely, probably even in court. The prophet mentions their intention to kill him in verse 23.

19–23. In this lament as in the other laments, Jeremiah addresses God urgently. He cries out to God to listen, to ‘pay attention’ to him. The same verb is used in the previous verse in a negative way: ‘let us not pay attention to him’. His prayer for revenge (11:20; 12:3; 15:15; 17:18; 20:12) is an urgent plea to God to bring justice. Jeremiah’s intentions were good – he even interceded on behalf of his people – so should good result in evil (v. 20)? The cause of his lament is clear: they have dug a pit to catch Jeremiah (vv. 20, 22); they have plotted to kill him. The prophet’s only refuge is his appeal for God’s just intervention. (For more on the prayer for revenge, see the Additional note, p. 138.)

Meaning

In this chapter judgment is pronounced on a sinful nation. Jeremiah’s adversaries are a vivid example of the stubbornness of people who do not want to listen to his (i.e. God’s) words. These enemies form a real threat to the prophet. They try to exclude him, and thereby God’s words, from their lives. However, this will result in even more disaster as a result of God’s anger. Their judgment has not yet come about, and Jeremiah has to leave their fate in God’s hands.
