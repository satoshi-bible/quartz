J. A time to mourn (16:1–21)

i. Loneliness as symbolical action (16:1–13)

Context

Verses 1–9 provide another example of how the prophet’s personal life is affected by the weight of his message of judgment, and verses 10–13 supply the explanation. Verses 1–9 can be divided into three, with each section opening with a word from God containing a prohibition, followed by an explanation: 1–4 (no marriage), 5–7 (no funerals), 8–9 (no feasts or weddings). Verses 10–13 contain a threefold question by the people about God’s judgment (v. 10), to which the reponse is one of condemnation and guilt for both past and present behaviour (11–12), followed by a further announcement of judgment (v. 13).

Comment

16:1–4. It was unusual for Jewish men not to marry. The prohibition to marry is a general one: it does not apply to just one particular period in Jeremiah’s life, but implies permanent celibacy. Although the rest of the passage indicates that judgment is inescapable (which this commentary considers to be the case after Jehoiakim’s burning of the scroll; see Introduction, p. 24), the prohibition to marry must have been given earlier, otherwise the prophet would certainly have been married already. Jeremiah may have been asked many times why he had not found himself a wife. Having no children meant that one was not included in the promises concerning the coming Messiah, and one’s life story would come to a definite end. The prohibition is given in this place, i.e. in Jerusalem or the whole of Judah (see also vv. 3, 9). Jeremiah’s ‘barrenness’ symbolizes the end of life for those in Judah who do have children. They and their offspring will succumb to diseases that will kill them, such as the pestilence. Or they will be killed in war and suffer from famine resulting from war (cf. also 14:12, 16, 18; 15:2–3).Their bodies will lie in the open air to be devoured by birds and beasts, a sign of severe judgment (cf. 7:33; 8:2; 14:16; 15:3; see 22:18–19 for King Jehoiakim’s fate).

5–7. Jeremiah is commanded not to attend funerals or mourn the dead. Several customs are mentioned. First there is the ‘wailing and mourning’ (v. 5). Going to a ‘house of mourning’ (NRSV) probably included a meal as well (see TNIV). Then there is the ‘bemoaning or showing sympathy’ (the Hebrew actually means to move backwards and forwards as a sign of grief) and the ‘cutting in the skin and shaving of the head’, practices actually forbidden in Israel because of their pagan origins (Lev. 19:28; Deut. 14:1–2; yet Jer. 41:5 mentions it as a custom in those days). Finally, there is the custom of eating and drinking as a form of consolation (v. 7). The beginning of verse 7 reads (lit.) ‘and they shall not break for them’, though it may be assumed that the reference is to bread being broken. The Hebrew word ‘for them’ is quite similar to the word for ‘bread’ and is often read as such. However, ‘[for] them’ is repeated in verses 5–6 (in Hebrew) and suits the context well. ‘Break’ can still refer to the breaking of bread, even if this is not explicit. The motivation for the prohibition in verse 5 is given at the end of the verse by emphasizing the words of the covenant: God has taken away his peace (blessing, TNIV), his faithful love (ḥesed) and his merciful pity. These words clearly belong to the covenant relationship between God and his people, and their withdrawal is disastrous. The people are now called this people instead of my people, the usual description in the context of the covenant (see Exod. 6:7). This breach in the covenant is caused by Israel’s sin (vv. 11–12).

8–9. The third prohibition concerns participation in festive events, such as weddings. Joy and gladness will come to an end (cf. 7:34; 25:10; also 33:10–11 where weddings will be part of life again as a sign of restoration and hope).

10–11. God is anticipating the people questioning the disaster that will fall upon them (cf. 5:19 and 13:12–14). Their questions suggest that they have no idea why judgment will come, as if no sins have been committed. Verses 10–13 use language similar to that found in Deuteronomy (cf. Deut. 29:24–28) and Jeremiah (‘serving other gods’; see 7:9; 8:2; 11:10; 13:10; 19:4; 25:6).

12–13. The placing of you at the beginning of the verse adds emphasis, and similarly the second you (lit. ‘Look!’ [you, plural] … following the stubbornness …). No-one can claim to be innocent (cf. Ezek. 18). They all cling to their sinful ways and disobedience towards God. He will throw the people away like someone discarding something useless (see also 22:26 for a similar description applied to Jehoiachin). In exile, the people will be confronted with other gods as a punishment for serving them in the Promised Land, thus recalling the so-called lex talionis (Exod. 21:23–25; Deut. 19:21): the punishment matches the iniquity.

Meaning

The nation’s hopeless situation, symbolized in Jeremiah’s own life, will end in destruction and exile. Jeremiah is instructed to avoid normal life situations: marriage, funerals and weddings. The impact of this is much greater than for those living in a Western European country. It means isolation from a social life with family and friends, which is so important in most non-Western cultures (see also 15:17). Moreover, being childless meant the prophet would be excluded from the line of generations from which the Messiah might be born, and marks an end of hope and a future.

ii. Mixed hope and judgment (16:14–21)

Context

Verses 14–21 can be divided as follows: hope (vv. 14–15); sins and sinners will not go unpunished (vv. 16–18); psalm about future hope (vv. 19–20), followed by an oracle of hope from the Lord. Although these texts may have been spoken at different times, in their present context there are some links with verses 1–13, such as the theme of idolatry (vv. 11, 13, 18, 19, 20) and the theme of the land (v. 13 – exclusion from the land; v. 15 – restoration to the land; v. 18 – defilement of the land).

Comment

14–15. These verses contrast with the previous ones: God will gather his people from the exile, which he brought on them as a judgment. The area of the exile is referred to as the land of the north, as in previous chapters (see 1:14; 3:18; 4:6). God will bring them back (restore, TNIV) to the land he had given to their ancestors, indicating the promise to Abraham (Gen. 12:1–3). The phrase As surely as the LORD lives constitutes an oath. God will be referred to as the one who led his people out of the Babylonian exile, an act that surpasses even the exodus, which was the major event in Israel’s history (see also Isa. 43:16–21). All this emphasizes the great thing God is going to do in restoring his people (see also 23:7–8).

16–18. The content of verses 16–18 can be linked to verses 10–13: the iniquities of the people will not go unpunished. The enemy, here referred to as fishermen and hunters, will come, and no-one will escape them or the coming judgment (similar imagery is used in Ezek. 12:13; 19:1–9; Amos 4:2). The Hebrew text of verse 18 reads: ‘And I will repay first double their sins.’ TNIV and NRSV omit ‘first’, but it is retained in NKJV. It implies that God will have to punish first, before the promises of the previous verses can come true. God will repay them double, which can also mean ‘fully’ or ‘more than enough’ (for a similar expression, see Isa. 40:2). It does not mean that God is measuring their sins exactly or that he punishes more than is just. Judgment is the result of serving idols, lifeless forms of their vile images and detestable idols (‘abominations’, NRSV). Idols are abhorrent ‘things’ (cf. Ezek. 11:21 for the same Hebrew words). The land is God’s inheritance (3:18–19; 17:4; Deut. 4:38), and it is defiled by idolatry (see 2:7; 3:1–2, 9).

19–20. Verse 19 is reminiscent of many of the psalms (cf. Pss 27:1; 37:39; 46:1). Jeremiah speaks here about the future, when the nations will acknowledge that they and their ancestors have worshipped what is a lie (‘nothing but lies, worthless things in which there is no profit’, NRSV). The ‘worthless things’ are hebel (Heb.), ‘nothing’, ‘a vapour’. This word is frequently used in Ecclesiastes. Idols may look impressive, but they have no substance and cannot help in times of need. They are not what they appear to be; they are ‘false’, a lie (see 2:5, 8, 11; 10:1–16). The idea that the nations will come to the God of Israel is expressed frequently in the prophets (see Isa. 2:2–4; Jer. 3:17; 12:14–16). The rhetorical question in verse 20 emphasizes that gods made by humans are no gods at all (see 2:11; 5:7; 10:1–16).

21. God will make himself known among the nations. They will acknowledge his power (lit. ‘my hand and my power’), as demonstrated at the time of the exodus (Exod. 6:6). God will (lit.) ‘cause them to know’ (will teach them, TNIV): the phrase is repeated. He will show his power ‘so that they will know’ that he is the one and only true God. God and his name are one and the same.

Meaning

The alternating themes of hope and judgment are part of Jeremiah’s message from the beginning (see 1:10), as is the fact that he speaks words concerning the nations. The message of hope in verses 14–15 does not contradict the message of judgment in verses 1–13. Restoration will come, but only after judgment on sin has taken place (16–18). The detestable idols in the land must be removed before restoration can take place. The land is defiled and will be more or less ‘evacuated’ and ‘emptied of sinners’ in order to make way for a restored future. In those days even the nations will recognize that idolatry leads to nothing.
