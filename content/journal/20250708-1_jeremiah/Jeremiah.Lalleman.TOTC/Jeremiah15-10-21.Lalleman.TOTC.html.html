I. A lamenting prophet reproached (15:10–21)

Context

Jeremiah 15:10–21 is one of the so-called ‘Confessions’ of Jeremiah, one of the prayers in which he addresses God in a very personal way, yet within the context of his prophetic ministry. It is obvious in verses 15–16 that the prophetic task and message are causing him to suffer at the hands of his own people, who oppose his message and do not want to listen to him (cf. 11:18–23; 12:1–6). A link between verse 10 and the previous passage may be the word mother (vv. 9, 10), although in verse 10 mother probably refers to the prophet’s own mother (see also 20:14–18). However, the word may be used ambivalently, and the reader should bear in mind the ‘mothers’ of the previous verses who represent the nation. Similarly, there is a link between the announcement of judgment and the prophet’s lament, which may therefore be a reaction both to the preaching of judgment in the former passage and to the prohibition to intercede in 15:1.

There is some discussion about verses 11–14, where the Hebrew is difficult and the verses seem to interrupt the flow of Jeremiah’s lament. Verse 10 can be seen as the prophet’s lament, verses 11–14 as God’s answer, verses 15–18 as the prophet’s lament and 19–21 again as God’s answer.

Comment

10. Alas introduces a lament (Woe to me, see 10:19).The prophet addresses his mother, as it were (see above), complaining that he has been born (see also 20:14–18). Presumably he also regrets his call as a prophet (1:5). The whole land seems to be fighting against him. His message of judgment brings terror. Yet he knows he is innocent and has never caused trouble by being involved in commercial business (i.e. lending or borrowing; see Ps. 15:5).

11–14. God answers with words that match the story of Jeremiah’s call (v. 11; see 1:8, 17–19). God will be with the prophet in his encounters with the enemy. Verse 12 has various possible translations and interpretations. TNIV helpfully interprets the iron and bronze as a symbol of Jeremiah’s steadfastness (see 1:18). The iron from the north may also be interpreted as the power of the (Babylonian) enemy coming from the north, in which case verses 11–14 affirm Jeremiah’s message of doom and in this way God defends the trustworthiness of the prophet against his enemies who say disaster will not come (17:15). Lundbom (1999: 735) applies the verse to the prophet’s enemies, who are like ‘iron’, but, unlike the prophet, not the high-quality iron that comes from present-day Turkey (the north). Lundbom paraphrases: ‘“Can iron-clad enemies break an even stronger iron and bronze Jeremiah?” Answer: “Of course not!”’ Verses 13 and 14 continue the announcement of judgment in verse 9, but, as mentioned above, they also function as an affirmation of Jeremiah’s prophetic message. I will enslave you or ‘I will make you serve’ (NRSV). The Hebrew word for ‘serve’ here is an alteration of the word ‘pass over’, which is very similar but less probable (see also Jer. 17:4; NKJV sticks to ‘cross over’). On the subject of God’s anger as a fire, see 4:4; 7:20; 17:4; 21:12. The words [burn] against you are rendered as ‘for ever’ in NRSV, a slightly different reading of the Hebrew and in accordance with 17:4.

15–18. These verses are similar to a psalm of lament (e.g. Pss 4; 6; 13). When God ‘knows’ (understand, TNIV) and ‘remembers’, it will involve action (see Exod. 2:25 where the verb ‘know’ is translated was concerned in TNIV). The verb remember has the sense of ‘thinking of’ in a way that results in action (cf. 14:21). The verb care for [me] can mean ‘visit’ with a positive purpose, or ‘punish’ (i.e. visit with a negative purpose). Here it is positive. The prophet cries out for help. He knows God is long-suffering and slow to anger, but he pleads with God to put a stop to his enemies in time, and rescue him from death. After all, it is for God’s sake that he is suffering. Part of his suffering, according to verse 17, stems from his loneliness. He did not join in the festivities (Jer. 16). The prophet belongs to God, and he ‘bears his name’ (v. 16). This is also said of the people (14:9). So it seems that the prophet represents the people, yet suffers at their hands. When God’s words were given to Jeremiah, they were like good food (see Ezek. 3:1–3). Yet the ‘food’ (i.e. Jeremiah’s message) brought indignation, judgment and God’s wrath. The prophet cannot hold it in (6:11; 20:9). Why (v. 18) is a familiar word in the psalms of lament (see comments on 14:7–9; also 14:19). The wound of the people cannot be healed (8:21–22; 30:12–13), but neither can Jeremiah’s wound (on medical metaphors, see also Introduction, p. 42). He suffers the pain of a prophet under attack, but also as someone who is part of his people and is watching disaster about to unfold, without any possibility of preventing it (15:1). Jeremiah even reproaches God as someone he cannot trust any longer: a deceptive brook, not providing the water one expects. This is a stark contrast with the imagery in Jeremiah 2:13! Jeremiah’s reproach is taken very seriously by God (v. 19). You are to me like … (v. 18) is expressed in Hebrew using two forms of the verb ‘to be’, for additional emphasis: ‘Truly, you are to me [like a deceitful brook]’ (NRSV). It may also be translated as a question: ‘Will you surely be to me like …?’ (see NKJV).

19–21. God does not accept this kind of language. The prophet is urged to repent and then God will restore him (lit. ‘If you return, I will let you return’). The word ‘return’ is repeated twice at the end of verse 19: ‘they shall return to you, but you should not return to them’ (note the important role of the word šûb here, as elsewhere in Jeremiah). Jeremiah is challenged again to be ‘God’s mouth’, his spokesperson, speaking God’s words, rather than ‘worthless’ nonsense (v. 18b). If he repents of that, God can use him again. Verse 20 repeats words from Jeremiah’s calling (see 1:18–19), and we notice the wonderful fact that, despite the prophet’s ‘worthless words’ of verse 18, God will take Jeremiah again into service as his prophet. At the beginning of his lament, the prophet implicitly considers his call (v. 10); at the end of it, God refers to his call as a way of challenging the prophet to return to his first call and continue the battle of being God’s prophet.

Meaning

The prophetic task was not to be chosen lightly. In fact, Jeremiah did not choose it at all – God chose Jeremiah. As in other passages, Jeremiah runs to God in his misery. He is being threatened by enemies and he suffers from loneliness. God brings comfort, not by solving the problems, but by renewing his call (even after Jeremiah’s reproach in v. 18) and assuring him of his presence and the preservation of his life.
