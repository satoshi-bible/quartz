E. Divine disappointment (11:1–17)

Context

Jeremiah 11 can be divided into three major parts: 1–13, 14–17, 18–23. The passages are thematically linked by the idea of ‘listening’ (vv. 1–13, 14) and ‘conspiracy’ (v. 9, against God and his words; v. 18, against the prophet). The final passage of chapter 11 (vv. 18–23) cannot be separated from what follows in Jeremiah 12, and the verses are therefore treated together. We assume a later date for 11:1–17 than for verses 18–23, which can be placed at the beginning of Jeremiah’s ministry (see below).

There is a link with the previous chapter in the introductory command to listen/hear (10:1; 11:2). Also, the themes of idolatry and judgment in Jeremiah 10 (cf. 11:10–13, 17) are resumed.

i. Broken covenant (11:1–13)

Context

These verses (1–13) can be taken as one unit, although verse 14 fits the context as well (see below). They are written in prose and are regarded by those who assume a Deuteronomistic redaction of Jeremiah as a classic example of that theory, just like Jeremiah 7. However, although there are many similarities with the prose of Deuteronomy, there are also differences. For a list of comparisons and distinctions between Deuteronomy and Jeremiah 11:1–17, see Craigie, Kelley and Drinkard (1991: 168–169). They assume a common literary milieu for the two, which is plausible if we take into account that the prophet came from a priestly background in which the knowledge of the religious traditions of his people was kept alive.

Key words in verses 1–13 (and 14) are ‘the words [terms, TNIV] of this covenant’, ‘the covenant’ and ‘hear’. The latter occurs in verses 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 14, but is translated in various ways in TNIV and NRSV: hear, listen, obey, heed. To ‘hear God’s voice’ means to obey him and live in accordance with his will in everyday life, not just to pay lip service. There are many correspondences between 11:1–14, 17 and chapter 7. A major difference between 7:1–15 and 11:1–17 is that there is still a call to repentance in the former passage (7:5), whereas in the latter the people have not listened to God and he will not listen to them. So we can date 11:1–17 to the time after King Jehoiakim burned the scroll (see Introduction, p. 24).

Comment

11.1. Without any introduction, the word of the Lord comes to Jeremiah. It is obvious that the prophet is the messenger of God. What follows is a serious reminder of the words of this covenant, the covenant given at Sinai. Like Moses, the prophet par excellence in the Old Testament, Jeremiah plays an important role in reminding the people of the covenant.

2. In the Hebrew, ‘hear’ is plural and ‘speak’ is singular, which has given rise to some variations: following the Hebrew, we may conclude that the people are addressed through Jeremiah, but he is the only one to ‘speak’ these words.

3–5. These verses are reminiscent of Deuteronomy 27:15–26, where Moses confronts the people with curses if they do not follow the commandments of the covenant. Blessings and curses are a well-known aspect of Old Testament (and other ANE) treaties between a superior and his vassal. Two events from the past are recalled in order to contrast the unthankful behaviour of a people chosen, loved and blessed by God. First, they were led out of Egypt, the land of slavery, and were almost ‘melted’ in the ‘iron furnace’ (cf. Deut. 4:20; 1 Kgs 8:51). God led them out of Egypt and up to the Promised Land, from a lower area in the geographical sense of the word, but also spiritually, to a ‘higher’ land. The Promised Land was full of life and abundance, flowing with milk and honey (v. 5; cf. Exod. 3:8, 17 amongst many others). Secondly, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob are referred to as the forefathers to whom God promised the land, beginning with Genesis 12:1–3 (also Exod. 6:8). The classical covenant formula, ‘you will be my people and I will be your God’ (v. 4, Exod. 6:7), stresses that the main focus of God’s deeds towards Israel is to establish a strong, intimate relationship. At the end of verse 5, Jeremiah affirms God’s words in his Amen, so be it Lord.

6–8. Again God speaks to Jeremiah; in content these words echo the previous message. There is no need to regard these as two different occasions on the grounds of the use of me instead of to Jeremiah (v. 1), since this often occurs (18:1, 3, 5; 35:1, 3). God has warned his people repeatedly, which is emphasized in the verbs, although this is less obvious in TNIV than in NRSV. NKJV is closest to the Hebrew: ‘I earnestly exhorted … until this day, rising early and exhorting …’ The word ‘earnestly’ is absent in Hebrew, but the idea of earnestly and strongly exhorting is expressed by the verbal forms. The ‘rising early and exhorting’ is a literal translation of the two infinitives in Hebrew. Several examples of this device are to be found in Jeremiah (e.g. 7:13, 25), to emphasize the action. Lundbom (1999: 469) says this idiom may be a ‘signature phrase of the prophet’. The word But (TNIV) or ‘Yet’ (NRSV, NKJV) expresses the unexpectedness and shock of the people’s reaction to so many exhortations. It is their stubbornness (cf. 7:24), deeply rooted in their hearts, which has caused them to disobey the Lord by following other gods. These verses are concerned with the previous generations, from the Exodus generation through to the time of the judges and kings. God ‘brought upon them all the words of this covenant’ (NRSV), namely the curses (TNIV) connected with the covenant at Sinai, such as war and famine (Deut. 27).

9–10. The conspiracy found in Judah and Jerusalem is against God and his words, as if everyone has agreed to disobey him. Jeremiah’s generation is no better than the previous ones, for their sins are the same, i.e. worshipping other gods and thereby ignoring and provoking the one true God (cf. Exod. 20:3–5). This implied breach of the covenant was not God’s doing, but has been brought about by the people who have not kept the stipulations of the covenant-treaty (see vv. 3–5; also 31:32).

11–13. The word Therefore introduces the verdict, which will be a disaster from which they cannot escape. The people have not listened to God; now he will not listen to them. People will search for help from the gods they worshipped, but of course these dead objects are unable to save (in contrast to God who saved them out of Egypt, but whom they ignored; see the end of 10:5). The word ‘shame’ (NRSV) is another name for Baal (also 3:24) and is combined with Baal in TNIV (that shameful god Baal).

Meaning

In this passage and in previous chapters, the prophetic message is hammering home the disillusionment after the wonderful expectations of the past when God brought his beloved people out of Egypt. No-one who receives judgment can say they had no idea it was coming or why it is happening. There is an intensity and urgency in the message, which is delivered variously as prose, poetry, symbolism, and through different speakers (God/the prophet/the prophet speaking for the people). Listen … obey … but they do not listen or obey: that is the message of verses 1–13. The covenant of Sinai is broken, not by God, but by his people (cf. 31:32).

ii. Stop interceding! (11:14–17)

Context

Verses 14–17 appear to be an independent unit, but in its final form the content matches that of the previous passage: because God’s people do not listen to him, he will not listen to them any more. Two poetic verses (15–16, not visible in the NRSV) are enclosed by two prose verses (14, 17).

As with the previous passage, a date after the burning of the scroll is assumed, because of the inevitability of judgment.

Comment

14. As in 7:16, the prophet is forbidden to intercede on behalf of his people, because judgment is now irreversible. Also in common with 7:16, this follows a speech on not keeping God’s commandments (see commentary there on the prophetic role of intercession, p. 109).

15. This is a complex verse in Hebrew, which has given rise to much discussion. The literal reading of the sentence is: ‘What to my beloved in my house her doing the wicked plan the many and the holy meat/flesh pass away from her because of my disaster then you rejoice.’ The first part is the easiest and can be translated as ‘What about my beloved [Israel] in my house?’ For the next part, some take ‘the many’ together with ‘wicked plan’ and translate, ‘Having done lewd deeds with many …’ (NKJV). Others, like NRSV, change the Hebrew for ‘many’ into a word which means ‘vows’, following the LXX. In Hebrew this is a slight change. However, it is possible to retain ‘many’ and apply it to priests and other people in the temple: they are ‘many’, and their end will come, just like the sacrificial meat. Would the people then rejoice? This is an ironical question (in accordance with Lundbom 1999: 631). The phrase ‘because of my disaster’ (see above) can also be read as ‘because of your wickedness’, by taking the Hebrew ending, an archaic, second-person, masculine suffix, to mean ‘your’ rather than ‘my’. Lundbom suggests: ‘What about my beloved in my house her doing this intrigue! The many [priests and others] – also sacrificial flesh – will cease from you because of your evil then you will exult!’ (Lundbom 1999: 627). God’s people are his beloved, his bride. He chose them out of love (Deut. 7:8). Hosea 3:1 emphasizes that God will still love his people, just as the prophet Hosea takes his wife back again after her unfaithfulness.

16. God’s chosen people were like an olive tree, bearing good and beautiful fruit (cf. Isa. 5, and Hos. 14:6 for future restoration). Yet God will set fire to it and bring judgment by destroying its beauty. An enemy will bring destruction to Judah. Think of what happened to the beautiful temple and palace of Jerusalem when it fell into the hands of the Babylonians (chs. 39 and 52).

17. The often-repeated reason for judgment is idolatry, the worship of the Canaanite god Baal. God has been deliberately provoked and vexed by his beloved people, ‘his own’ (see also comment on 7:18).

Meaning

Israel is called my beloved (v. 15), but God’s beloved have forgotten him. There is great disappointment and pain in verses 15–16. Judgment and punishment come from a God who loves his people deeply and constantly tries to bring them close to him. In the book of Revelation, Jesus Christ addresses the church in Ephesus, saying, Yet I hold this against you: you have forsaken the love you had at first (Rev. 2:4). The relationship between Christ and his church is compared with the marriage relationship in Ephesians 5:25–27. The bride, however, can be so remote from her ‘husband’ that there is hardly anything left of the relationship.
