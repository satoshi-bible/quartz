H. Drought and disaster (14:1 – 15:9)

Context

Jeremiah 14 is a lament about drought (vv. 2–6) and war (vv. 17–18). The sinful behaviour of the people has caused this disaster (see also ch. 13). Jeremiah 14 and 15 deal with a variety of themes which are familiar elsewhere in the book, such as false prophets (14:14–15), destruction by sword, famine and plague (14:12; 15:2), the prohibition of intercession (14:11; 15:1), the people as a sick and wounded nation (14:19). It is somewhat artificial to separate 14:22 from 15:1. So it is preferable to run the chapters together, and deal with Jeremiah’s lament in 15:10–21 separately.

Since judgment is inevitable, the whole of 14:1 – 15:9 in its present form can be dated to a time after the burning of the scroll by King Jehoiakim (see Introduction, p. 24). No dates are given for the drought (14:1) or for the time of war (14:17–18). The latter may refer to a current situation, for instance the first deportation in 597, or to an event in the future which is presented as if it has already happened.

Comment

14:1. This is the word of the LORD is usually followed by a message from God. However, here there is a lament (vv. 2–6), containing the message of God, so it may also be called a ‘prophetic lament’. The form of a lament renders the prophetic message more poignant and dramatic. Judgment is not proclaimed without emotions (see also vv. 17–18).

2–6. Judah, Jerusalem and the cities are personified: they mourn and lament (see also Jer. 4:28). The word cities is literally ‘gates’, the place where business was conducted and matters of justice were settled. They are empty now, with no sign of activity. The nobles send their servants to get water, but there is none, and the drought is severe. Covering one’s head is a sign of mourning or dismay (cf. 2 Sam. 15:30). Servants and farmers alike cover their heads, and even the animals suffer and cannot care for their young any longer. There is a connection between the drought and the people’s sins, drought being one of the curses mentioned in Deuteronomy 28:23–24 (see also 3:1–5; 23:10).

7–9. Jeremiah finds words to lament on behalf of the people. If it is a genuine confession of sins, it did not last long, for God’s answer is still one of judgment (cf. 2:27; 3:10, 22–25). Verse 7 appeals to God’s reputation (your name) as the grounds for delivering his people. Such an appeal is well known from the psalms (Pss 25:11; 31:3; 109:21). Verses 8–9 have a chiastic structure (see Introduction, p. 47) as follows:

A – Hope of Israel, Saviour in times of distress

B – Why are you like a stranger … like a traveller?

B’ – Why are you like someone … like a warrior?

A’ – You are among us, LORD, we bear your name

The ‘why-questions’ play an important role in the psalms of lament (Pss 10:1; 22:1; 74:11; Jer. 15:18; 20:18). It seems as if God is just a stranger travelling through the (promised?) land for a short time and leaving the people behind (cf. 9:2), as if he does not have the power to do anything; he is like someone taken by surprise. The Hebrew word for ‘surprised’ occurs only here in the Old Testament, and a better translation is ‘helpless’. God is also likened to a warrior powerless to save (v. 9). How can God seem powerless to save when he is Israel’s Saviour (v. 8)? The rest of verse 9 recalls God’s special relationship with Israel, the reason for saving them: we bear your name (cf. v. 7: for the sake of your name).

10–12. The answer to the questions in verses 8 and 9 comes in God’s response in verse 10: God is not the one who is wandering – they are! ‘My people’ has therefore become this people (in a pejorative sense). God and his people are indeed estranged, but God is not the cause of this situation. Their sins alienate them from their special position as the chosen people. God has not forgotten them, but what he mainly remembers now is their sins, which require judgment. He is still present, but through judgment, which is inevitable. God now even forbids the prophet to intercede (v. 11; see 7:16; 11:14; 15:1). In Jeremiah, sword, famine and plague (v. 12) are often mentioned together (e.g. 21:9; 24:10; 29:18; 42:17, 22). Famine and plague are the results of war. The three together signify total destruction (see also 15:2). Sacrifices can no longer avert judgment; neither can fasting or praying – God cannot be manipulated.

13. As a counter-argument, Jeremiah quotes the predictions of salvation by the false prophets (indicated by Ah; see also 1:6; 4:10). These crop up regularly in Jeremiah (see 6:14; 8:11; 23:17). It is as if Jeremiah is pleading for the people because they, as it seemed, could not help going astray, for the prophets had predicted peace.

14–16. God’s answer is that these prophets are not sent by him and, unlike true prophets, they do not speak his words (1:7, 9; see also 23:16, 21, 32). They tell of false visions, lies by which they deceive the people (see 23:25–26; also the story of Hananiah in ch. 28, and 29:31 where the false prophet Shemaiah is accused of telling a lie). The words divinations and idolatries can be taken together and translated as ‘worthless divination’ (so NRSV), since the word for ‘worthless’ can also mean ‘idolatry’ (see TNIV footnote). Divination was clearly forbidden in Israel (Deut. 18:10). The verdict on the false prophets follows (Therefore … v. 15). Those false prophets will suffer the same fate as the people (v. 12) – a fate they have been denying (v. 13). Both prophets and people will suffer from famine and the sword. There will be no-one to bury them, i.e. the people in general, not just the prophets (see 7:30 – 8:3). God will pour out on the people the calamity they deserve (v. 16; ‘and I will pour out upon them their wickedness’, NRSV). The word for ‘wickedness’ can also mean disaster, and both elements are present here: God will make Judah’s sins (‘wickedness’) fall upon their heads.

17–18. God urges the prophet to utter a prophetic lament that dramatically expresses the disaster of the people struck by famine and war (vv. 17–18; see also vv. 1–6). The lament for the virgin Daughter – my people demonstrates the love God still has for his people (cf. v. 10, this people). In his anger he seems distant, but it is with pain that he punishes his beloved children. The people are deadly wounded (see Introduction, p. 42, for medical metaphors in Jeremiah). Prophets and priests are usually there to offer comfort and help in cases of illness (see comments on 6:13–15). However, they have been exiled to a land they know not (rather than ‘ply their trade throughout the land, and have no knowledge’, NRSV; see also 15:14; 16:13; 17:4).

19–22. These verses can be interpreted as a communal lament by Jeremiah on behalf of the people (cf. vv. 7–9). Two rhetorical questions, followed by ‘why (then)?’, express something unthinkable (for use of this linguistic device, see also 2:14, 31; 8:4–5, 19, 22). The intended meaning is: ‘We know you have not rejected Judah or despised Zion, but why then are we so wounded [similar language to v. 17]?’ The answer comes later, in 15:6: they have rejected God. We hoped for peace, which was predicted by the false prophets.

Verses 20–22 repeat several themes already mentioned in verses 7–8: a confession of guilt, this time even the guilt of their forefathers (cf. 11:7–8); a plea invoking God’s name and this time the covenant as well; a recognition that God is the only one who can save and bring hope (v. 8; see also 17:13). God’s glorious throne (v. 21) is the place where God shows his glory (also the ark in the temple; cf. 3:16; 17:12). If God does not intervene, other nations might think he is not capable of rescuing them or keeping his promises. Ezekiel frequently uses this motive (Ezek. 20:9, 14, 22; 36:20–23). When God remembers his covenant (v. 21), it means that he is going to act in order to save his people, as in Exodus 2:24. It is not God who is breaking the covenant, but the people are disobeying his commandments with devastating consequences (11:10; 31:32; Deut. 27 – 28). There is even recognition that it is God who sends the rain, rather than the gods of the nations, the worthless idols or ‘nothings’ (see comments on 10:1–16). The lament ends with the repetition that the people’s hope is in God alone (v. 8) who does all this (i.e. sends the rain and showers).

15:1–4. Jeremiah is forbidden to intercede, and even if the great interceding prophets Moses (Exod. 32:11–14, 30–32; Num. 14:13–20; Deut. 9:13–29) and Samuel (1 Sam. 7:8–9; 12:19–25) were to intercede, God would not hear them. This (rather than ‘my’) people should be sent away. But where? The answer is ironic: to death, by whatever means (see also 14:12). Verse 2 is poignant: the word destined does not appear in Hebrew (‘those to death to death, and those to the sword, to the sword’, etc.). Four things will destroy the people, and destruction will be complete. Again, verse 3 mentions four things that will destroy the people: the sword and, after death, dogs, birds of prey and other animals will devour the corpses (see 7:33; 14:16; 34:20). Other nations will shake their heads over what has become of Judah and Jerusalem (25:9; 29:18; 34:17). Even Josiah’s reform could not avert judgment, because of the great evil carried out by his predecessor Manasseh (2 Kgs 23:26).

5–9. The prophetic message of verses 5–9 (declares the LORD; see vv. 6, 9) opens with three rhetorical questions to which the answer is: nobody will be interested in you any more, Jerusalem. The Hebrew word for mourn is to ‘move and shake one’s head in sadness’. Verse 6 answers the questions of 14:19: God has not rejected his people, but they have rejected him! You keep on blacksliding can also be translated as ‘backwards you went’, which means that they have turned their back on God. God is tired, ‘weary’ (NRSV) of showing compassion and withholding punishment (see 4:28; Jon. 3:9–10; 4:2). NRSV translates as if judgment has already taken place, but the verbs can also be translated in the future tense (see TNIV). The image in verse 7 of someone winnowing with a winnowing fork suggests that the corn and chaff will be thrown into the air and the wind will carry away the chaff – a picture of the people being carried away into exile (see 13:24). The city gates are where justice was done (see comments on 14:2) – now is the time of God’s judgment. The bereavement (v. 7) is literally ‘make childless’, through the sword (Ezek. 5:17). The prophetic call to repent and return (Jer. 3; 7:3) has been ignored; the people have not changed their ways. The judgment in verse 8 is very different from the promise in Genesis 22:17! Enemies will suddenly and unexpectedly attack at noon (see 6:4). A mother who loses seven children (v. 9, seven being an ideal number; cf. 1 Sam. 2:5) will … breathe her last. The literal meaning is ‘breathe out her life/soul’, so that she faints or dies, or perhaps loses courage. The sun goes down in her life; her life seems to be over. For disgraced because of childlessness, see Genesis 30:23.

Meaning

The message of judgment is brought home in various ways. A disastrous period of drought is followed by war, famine and pestilence. Lamenting seems to be the appropriate response. Prophet, God and nation are all involved in the laments, which vividly and poignantly express the hopeless situation of the people. The false prophets have given false hope by proclaiming peace and a message contrary to Jeremiah’s (v. 13). The prophet also speaks of hope (14:8, 22), but only in the context of recognizing guilt and sin (vv. 7, 20).
