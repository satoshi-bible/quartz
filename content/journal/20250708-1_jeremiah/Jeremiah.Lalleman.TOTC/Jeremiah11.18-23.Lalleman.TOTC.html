F. God and prophet suffer (11:18 – 12:17)

i. Plot against Jeremiah’s life (11:18–23)

Context

Jeremiah 11:18 – 12:6 is one of the so-called ‘Confessions’, passages which contain a personal address from the prophet to God concerning Jeremiah’s suffering as a prophet (see Introduction for connections between the ‘Confessions’ and psalms of lament, p. 51). Several characteristics of these psalms are apparent in 11:18–23 and 12:1–6, which according to Craigie, Kelley and Drinkard (1991: 177) are in fact two laments in parallel, containing the following elements:

  • Invocation/address to God (11:18; 12:1a)
  • Complaint (11:19; 12:1b, 2)
  • Prayer (11:20; 12:3–4)
  • Divine response (11:21–23; 12:5–6)

In this first ‘Confession’, we read about Jeremiah’s suffering at the hands of the people from his home town, even his own family. Whereas Jeremiah 11:9 refers to a conspiracy against God and his words, verse 18 speaks of a plot against his prophet Jeremiah.

Comment

18–19. God revealed to the prophet how the people had been plotting against him, which he had not noticed. The gentle lamb led to the slaughter is similar to Isaiah 53:7, although the emphasis there is on the willingness of the lamb to suffer. In Jeremiah 11 the prophet is unaware of the people’s intentions to kill him in order to stop him proclaiming the truth. They do not want to hear, since their sins are laid bare. The people conspired against God (v. 9), but here they are plotting against his prophet, God’s mouthpiece. Jeremiah’s life is compared to a tree (cf. 17:7–8; Pss 1:3; 92:12–15). God had ‘planted’ him, as he had Israel (v. 16), but the people reject both God and his messenger.

20. The prophet commends his case to God, asking him to judge those who are plotting evil. God is the only one who can judge with complete righteousness; he knows what is in the hearts of human beings. God ‘tries’, ‘tests’, examines the inner motives, will, mind and feelings of people (see also Ps. 7:9). The prophet is appointed as the tester of the people (6:27), but in 9:7 (Heb. 9:6) God tests his people. In ancient Hebrew thought, the kidneys were believed to be the seat of emotions. The word is translated as mind in TNIV, NRSV and NKJV.

21–23. Only now do we discover the identity of Jeremiah’s opponents: the people of Anathoth, his home town (see 1:1) where he grew up and everybody knew him. They are motivated to kill him in order to silence his message of identifying their sins and proclaiming God’s imminent judgment. God will not let the people of Anathoth go free. Therefore (vv. 21, 22) introduces judgment on the basis of their behaviour towards the prophet. The combination of sword and famine often occurs in the book of Jeremiah (cf. 14:12, 15, 16; 15:2) and implies total destruction.

Meaning

The prophet Jeremiah carries in his body the suffering which is the result of the disobedience and godless behaviour of the covenant people. He is not suffering at the hands of pagans!

It is obvious elsewhere in Scripture that God’s messengers are not always popular, to say the least. Jesus himself was not honoured in his own home town (Luke 4:24; cf. also the parable in Mark 12:1–8). Even Jesus’ friends left him and Judas betrayed him. In John 7:5 we read that Jesus’ own brothers did not believe in him. In this respect, Jeremiah can be seen as a forerunner of Jesus, the Lamb slaughtered for our sins (John 1:29, 36; Rev. 5:6), although Jeremiah himself needed forgiveness.
