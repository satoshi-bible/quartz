3. THE COVENANT BROKEN: JEREMIAH’S LAMENTS (7 – 20)

A. The loss of a covenant people (7:1 – 8:3)

Context

This section (7:1 – 8:3) can be subdivided into four more or less loosely connected units, although there are several words and themes linking them together (see below). Jeremiah 7:1 marks the beginning of a prose section which runs through to 8:4. Also thematically, chapter 7 continues into 8:1–3. Therefore it makes sense to read and interpret them together.

i. The temple is no talisman! (7:1–15)

Context

This passage is assumed to be a longer version of the sermon summarized in Jeremiah 26, which dates the events to the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim, who became king in 609 BC. Although the poetic style of the previous chapters differs from the prose in 7:1–15, there is no reason to deny the prophet’s authorship or ascribe it to a Deuteronomistic redaction (see Introduction, p. 29). Jeremiah’s ministry took place over a long period, and the different times and venues may account for the variety in style. After the introduction in verses 1–2a, the content of the sermon can be divided into sections (e.g. vv. 3–7, 8–11, 12–15), as indicated by Lundbom (1999: 455). However, the recurrence of the word house creates a coherent theme throughout. It therefore makes sense to regard verses 1–15 as one sermon, at least in its present form.

Comment

7:1–2. Verses 1–2a introduce the words of Jeremiah, and are followed by the content of his message in verses 2b–15. The prophet receives the command to stand at the gate of the LORD’s house, which is the temple. The particular gate is not mentioned, but it apparently gave access to the inner court of the temple (26:2 reads courtyard), so everyone coming to worship (lit. ‘bow down’) would meet Jeremiah first. He may have spoken at a particular festival when many people came into the temple.

3–8. There is a clear call to repentance here: the people need to reform their ways (lit. ‘make good’, i.e. change their whole way of living). This change will then result in God allowing them to continue living in the Promised Land. The words in this place occur in verses 3, 6 and 7, and verse 14 also repeats the word place. There is some ambivalence in the meaning, because the words are spoken in the context of the temple, the land and Jerusalem. All of these play a role in the sermon. Verses 7 and 14 link the place to the one promised to their forefathers, namely the land.

Verse 4 contains one of the key words in the book of Jeremiah: šeqer, meaning deception, lie (the word is also mentioned in one of the Ten Commandments; see Exod. 20:16, part of the background to this passage; see below). The people have put their trust in a false concept: i.e. that nothing can happen to God’s temple (and the Promised Land). The threefold saying in verse 4 sounds like a mantra, as if it could protect the people from harm and destruction, but this is a false, deceptive concept. Worshipping God and serving him in the temple according to the rules can never be an excuse for ignoring his commandments in the rest of life. A list of these commands in verses 5–6 and 9 closely follows the Torah given to Moses (see Exod. 20; 22:21–24; 23:6–9). The biblical concept of faith includes loving God and one’s neighbour. Verse 8 repeats the theme of verse 4: trusting in God should be the only foundation of life. He is trustworthy and gives assurance of life in the Promised Land, whereas trusting in a lie causes a major breakdown and chaos in society, even resulting in exile.

9–11. Verse 10 contrasts the temple, God’s holy house which bears my Name (cf. 11, 14, 30), with the people’s unholy behaviour, which strongly denies who God is. In fact, it defiles his Name. An appropriate description for the temple is now den of robbers, a place where robbers think they are safe (in Hebrew the words are at the beginning of the sentence for emphasis). Yet God sees what is happening in secret. The verse is also quoted in Mark 11:17. The detestable things are those things which are abominable, as mentioned in verse 9.

12–15. These verses present a shocking message to the people, who probably looked down on the Northern Kingdom, Israel or Ephraim, named after one of the sons of Joseph. Yet their fate will be the same: Judah will be exiled, just as Israel was in 722 BC. Shiloh was where the ark was kept in Samuel’s day (1 Sam. 3:3). The fact that it was a ‘holy place’ did not prevent its destruction. In the same way, the temple and Jerusalem may be destroyed. The people had been warned again and again (Hebrew is lit. ‘rising up early and speaking’, v. 13; so NKJV). For a similar expression, see verse 25, which reads (lit.), ‘daily rising up early and sending’. Lundbom (1999: 469) comments, ‘This idiom in the Hebrew, where a verb is repeated together with škm (“to rise early”) … may be a signature phrase of the prophet. The idiom intends to emphasize the verb (RSV: “I spoke to you persistently”).’ For other instances of this idiom, see 7:25; 11:7; 25:3, 4; 26:5; 29:19; 32:33; 35:14; 44:4. Nobody could say they did not know what God expected of them. God’s servants, the prophets, had called the people again and again to repent and return.

Meaning

The people in Jeremiah’s time seem to be very ‘religious’, but are not living according to God’s commandments. However, religious behaviour can never be an excuse for sinning. The temple is no talisman! Who can enter God’s temple? Psalms 15 and 24 offer a picture of life which is the complete opposite of the lives of the people here.

ii. Intercession prohibited (7:16–20)

Context

In Jeremiah 7:1–15 the people are urged to repent while there is still time. Verses 16–20 and the rest of the chapter are of an even more urgent character. Jeremiah is forbidden to intercede, because judgment will be inevitable. This suggests a different date from the previous passage (i.e. after Jehoiakim burned the scroll; see Introduction, p. 24). There are several connections with regard to content between verses 1–15 and 16–20, such as this place in verses 3, 6, 7, 20 and listen in verses 13 and 16.

Comment

16. The Hebrew sentence starts by emphasizing ‘you’, as in the NRSV: ‘As for you …’ The prohibition to pray for the people (i.e. to intercede on their behalf) is not a general prohibition for all times, but applies to this particular period. The Hebrew word for ‘not’ here differs from the one used in the case of a permanent prohibition, such as in the Ten Commandments. Jeremiah receives this prohibition several times (see 11:14; 14:11; 15:1). Old Testament prophets often engaged in intercessory prayer. Examples are Abraham, who is called a prophet in the context of intercession (Gen. 20:7, cf. Gen. 18:23–32), Moses (Exod. 32:11–14) and Samuel (1 Sam. 7:5; 12:19, 23). Amos intercedes to prevent God’s judgment, but there is a time when intercession is no longer effective (Amos 7:1–9). The words do not plead with me are also spoken by Ruth to her mother-in-law Naomi in Ruth 1:16, where they are translated, Don’t urge me, which expresses well the idea of clinging to someone to put pressure on them. Later in Jeremiah’s ministry, there is a time when he intercedes again (21:2; 37:3; 42:1–4), but this is no longer to prevent judgment by exile, which by then is an inescapable fact.

17–19. God does not heed Jeremiah’s intercession, because the people’s ongoing sins have blocked its effect. Judgment is inevitable: has Jeremiah not seen how people offend God by offering to other gods? He must have noticed! Whole families throughout Judah are involved in the worship of false gods. It has become a habit (expressed in Hebrew by using active participles). Worship of the Queen of Heaven is particularly mentioned. In Jeremiah 44:19 the cakes are related to her image (i.e. they may have been shaped in the form of a star, or her image may have been pressed on the surface of the cake). It is possible that the Queen of Heaven was the Assyrian-Babylonian fertility goddess Ishtar. There was a long tradition of worshipping female gods, such as Astarte or Asherah, the companion of Baal (see 2 Kgs 21:3, 5, 7; and 23:4 where Josiah tried to destroy this cult). In view of Jeremiah’s repeated warnings, apparently Josiah did not have the success he sought. To arouse God’s anger or provoke God to anger (the same verb in Hebrew) is to offend him deeply. This is repeated in several contexts in Jeremiah (see v. 19; 8:19; 11:17; 25:6–7; 32:29, 30, 32; 44:3, 8; cf. Deut. 32:16, 21).

Sin is harmful for those who commit it, and the people are bringing shame upon themselves. The word for shame is also used of Baal (see 11:13; Hos. 9:10). In the end, worshippers of the ‘gods of shame’ will become what they are: shame (see also 2:5).

20. Therefore introduces the judgment following the accusations in the previous verses. For the outpouring of God’s anger, see also 4:4; 15:14; 44:6. Nature (i.e. animals, trees and crops) will be included in the judgment (see also 4:23–28; 9:10; 12:4; Hos. 4:1–3).

Meaning

To abandon the practice of intercession must have been one of the hardest things for the prophet, since it was so much a part of his ministry. The passage emphasizes the sense of hopelessness of the situation. Due to their own stubborn refusal to obey God’s commandments, the people have gone far away from the ideal God had in mind at the time of Moses.

iii. Obedience better than sacrifices (7:21–28)

Context

The theme of sacrifices is taken up in verses 21–28, but in this case they are to God and not to idols. However, even those sacrifices have become worthless, because the people’s lives do not reflect what is expressed by them. The instruction to hear or listen crops up repeatedly throughout Jeremiah 7 (vv. 2, 13, 16, 24, 26, 27; see also vv. 23, 28, where obey is lit. ‘listen to my voice’).

Comment

21. The burnt offerings were burned as a whole, whereas a part of the sacrifices mentioned here was to be eaten by the one bringing the sacrifice. God now says that the worshippers can keep it all for themselves; he does not want to receive anything, because their evil conduct contradicts their act of worship. The verse is ironic and implies that people should stop bringing sacrifices at all. Lundbom (1999: 481) mentions other examples of this rhetorical device (known as an epitrope) in 15:2; 22:20; 44:25; 46:11 (cf. also Amos 4:4–5).

22–23. Verses 22 and 23 should be read together. Prophets were not opposed to sacrifices and other forms of official worship, but they protested against abuse of the practice, as if idolatry and injustice could be covered up by being ‘pious’. Other passages are also clear about this issue (see 1 Sam. 15:22; Ps. 51:16–19; Isa. 1:10–20; Amos 5:21–27; Mic. 6:6–8). Sacrifices are not mentioned in the Ten Commandments, but clear instructions are given on the subject elsewhere, particularly in Leviticus. However, sacrifices do not work ‘magically’. God can still refuse to forgive if people persist in wrongdoing. Being a people who have entered into a covenant relationship with God, expressed in the so-called ‘covenant formula’, I will be your God and you will be my people (cf. Exod. 6:7), means living as the people of God according to his will. As with the temple (see 7:1–15), sacrifices are no guarantee that God will spare his people if they continue to be stubborn and unwilling to change their way of life.

24–26. The people’s stubbornness is expressed in the repeated phrase, they did not listen. They are like a stubborn animal going backwards instead of forwards. For God’s continuous (‘persistently’, NRSV) warnings, see commentary on 7:13.

27–28. These verses are addressed to the prophet, who is to convey the words to the people. However, they will not listen (see vv. 24, 26), and therefore their nickname will be ‘the people who don’t listen’. The word for ‘people’ is the one used of pagans! Jeremiah 9 also emphasizes that truth and trustworthiness have disappeared.

Meaning

As in verses 1–15, religious behaviour, even sacrifices, cannot cover disobedience to God’s words, which harms one’s relationship with God. This passage also emphasizes how, like a father, God urges his people again and again to come back, through the words of his prophets.

iv. Horrible practices lead to a horrible end (7:29 – 8:3)

Context

This passage is linked with the preceding prophecies through the theme of idolatry (7:6, 9, 18) and the words the house that bears my Name (7:10, 11, 14).

Comment

29. This verse stands on its own somewhat, as it is written in poetry (see NRSV) and uses a different metaphor. In the Hebrew text, it is followed in verse 30 by kî, meaning ‘for’ or ‘because’, which links the two verses. Judah (or Jerusalem) is addressed as a woman who is specifically consecrated to God by a vow. This was visible by her long hair which marks her out as a ‘Nazirite’ (see Num. 6:1–8). However, she has not behaved like someone committed to God, so why not have her hair cut off? Normally this would happen when the vows had been completed, but she had never fulfilled her vows. Shaving the head was also a sign of mourning (see Jer. 16:6), although certain types of shaving were forbidden in Israel because of practices amongst her neighbours (Lev. 19:27; Deut. 14:1). The only thing to do is lament, and the designated place for this is, ironically, where idolatrous practices were carried out (i.e. the barren heights).

30–31. The detestable idols in the house that bears my Name are also mentioned in Ezekiel 8. Josiah removed the Asherah pole and other objects of idolatry from the temple, which had been placed there by Manasseh (2 Kgs 23:4–7). People even burned their children on the high places or pagan cult centres, as a sacrifice to idols (to the god Molek: Jer. 32:35). Manasseh is said to have sacrificed his own son in the fire (2 Kgs 21:6). Josiah desecrated the place to prevent further child sacrifices (2 Kgs 23:10). Child sacrifice was well known in the ancient world, but is absolutely forbidden by God (cf. Lev. 18:21; 20:1–5; Deut. 12:31; 18:10).

32–33. Judgment will follow because of the practices in Topheth and the Ben Hinnom Valley: the place will be full of slaughtered bodies. They will be so numerous that they will just lie in the open air, a prey for birds and beasts, and there will be no survivors to chase the predators away. Not having a proper funeral was seen as shameful and a token of punishment (cf. 8:1–3; 9:22; 14:16; 16:4, 6; also 1 Kgs 21:23–24).

34. All joy will be gone, echoing Jeremiah’s own experiences and symbolizing the nation’s despair and misery (cf. Jer. 16).

8.1. At that time may introduce an oracle of salvation (see 3:17), but here it announces judgment. The removal of bones from the graves (note the repetition, emphasizing the horror of it) is a terrible thing (see also 7:33). Even after a bomb attack in Israel today, the remains of human beings are treated with the highest respect by rabbis. No-one involved in such idolatrous practices and injustice will be spared – from the leaders to the ordinary people in the street.

2–3. Ironically, their bones will be exposed to the very things they have worshipped – as a post-mortem sacrifice. The idolatrous behaviour is emphasized, using a variety of verbs: they loved the sun, the moon and all the stars (which were worshipped in the Assyrian religion; cf. 7:18; 19:13; 2 Kgs 21:3, 5; see 23:5 for the removal of all foreign artefacts from the temple); they served them and went after them; they consulted them and bowed down to them. These verbs also feature in the book of Deuteronomy, with God as the object and only him! When the enemy comes to destroy and take the people into exile, even death will be preferable. These are the words of the ‘LORD of hosts’ (Almighty, TNIV), who is the only true God, not the ‘host of heaven’ worshipped by the people (see v. 2).

Meaning

Mixing foreign religious practices with the worship of God is not an innocent form of behaviour. In the end, it leads to destruction and death, and the consequences of neglecting the covenant on Sinai become a reality (Deut. 27 – 28).
