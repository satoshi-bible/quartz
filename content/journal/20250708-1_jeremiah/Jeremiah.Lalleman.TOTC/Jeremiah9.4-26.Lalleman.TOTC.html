C. Analysis of a broken society (9:4–26)

i. Lies and laments (9:4–22)

Context

As we saw in the previous chapter, in the Hebrew text, Jeremiah 9:1 is actually verse 23 of chapter 8, in which case 9:4 in the English version becomes 9:3 when the Hebrew numbering is followed. However, Jeremiah 9:4–22 cannot be separated from the previous passage (8:4 – 9:3). The complaints about the people’s behaviour continue in 9:4–6, and then the verdict is announced (Therefore in v. 7). This is followed by a lament, which is also the theme of verses 17–22. Various parts of 9:4–22 deal with the broken society and the coming judgment of the covenant people. The theme of falsehood, deception and lies runs throughout chapters 8 and 9 (the Hebrew word for ‘falsehood’ can be found in 8:8, 10; 9:3, 5; the theme of ‘lying’ is even more widespread; see e.g. 9:4, 6, 8).

Comment

4–6. These verses continue the theme of 9:1–3, highlighting that all are guilty (v. 2) and are accustomed to telling lies (v. 3). Even those one would expect to be trustworthy turn out to be deceivers. Verse 4 sums them up: your friends (lit. ‘your neighbours’, as in the parable of the Good Samaritan), any of your people and every one of them (lit. ‘any brother’ and ‘every brother’, a wider reference than sons of the same parents; ‘all your kin’, NRSV). In Israel each member of the covenant community was a ‘brother’ (Deut. 15:1–11), but here it may refer to one’s close family (just as Jeremiah discovered that his own brothers had betrayed him, 12:6). The pattern of verse 4 has a chiastic structure: friend – brother – brother – friend. Furthermore, the Hebrew for friend or ‘neighbour’ (NRSV) at the beginning and end of verse 4 sounds like the word for sin or ‘evil’ at the end of verse 3 (‘for they proceed from evil to evil’, NRSV). The use of similar consonants and sounds strengthens the effect of the message: those close to you are causing you harm – don’t trust anybody! For every one of them is a deceiver – this line contains two verbs with the same stem (‘deceive’). The name Jacob, the patriarch known for his deceit, is also derived from it (Gen. 27), so we could also translate as ‘a deceiving Jacob’. The words link Israel’s present with its past, as if to say, ‘You are standing in a tradition, but in this case not a positive one.’ The NRSV translation of the end of verse 4 is more literal than that of TNIV: ‘and every neighbour goes around like a slanderer’. People who gossip and slander like to go around picking up news and spreading it (see also Jer. 6:28, Lev. 19:16; and also Exod. 20:16; 23:1–2, 6–8; see 1 Kgs 21 for the dangers of a false witness in court). Verse 5 continues the message of verse 4: the habit of lying and deception is widespread. The NRSV follows the LXX here, but the TNIV translation seems to be a good one. Interpretations of verse 6 also vary, as the Hebrew is rather complicated (lit. ‘Your [singular, meaning Jeremiah] dwelling is in the midst of deception upon deception; they refuse to know me …’). The prophet is surrounded by deceit on all sides, because the people refuse to know God and his will. They do not acknowledge [‘know’] me (v. 3) speaks of their persistent disobedience, visible throughout the whole of society.

7–9. Therefore introduces the action God will take in response to the people’s sin. In 6:27–30 the prophet was told to be a tester of the people; here it is God himself who will refine and smelt them like metal in a fire to search for the good and the bad (see also 11:20; 12:3; 17:10). What else can I do (v. 7) sounds like a cry of despair because of the apparent hopelessness of the people’s attitudes and behaviour, which are hurtful to God. The Hebrew does not read the sin of my people; ‘the sin’ is added by the LXX. The words my people are actually ‘the daughter of my people’, an expression evoking a certain tenderness and closeness between God and his people which is used elsewhere in the context of judgment and lament (NRSV footnote: ‘my poor people’; see on 4:11; also 8:11, 19, 21, 22; 9:1; 14:17). Verse 8 is an extension of verses 3–5 and corresponds with Jeremiah’s own experience (see 12:6). The rhetorical questions in 9:9 express God’s judgment as a result of the previous analysis of the people’s behaviour.

10–11. The context of the lament is the reaction to the evil observed amongst the people and the resulting judgment. The same sort of reaction appears in 4:19–21. Is it God or Jeremiah who is speaking in verse 10? The NRSV follows the LXX by reading an imperative addressed to the people: ‘Take up weeping …’ In keeping with the MT, I will weep (TNIV) is a correct translation, but who does I refer to? The speaker in verse 11 is obviously God, and this may be the case in verse 10 as well. However, a certain ambiguity is possible here: the prophet speaks, but in his lament we hear God’s lament over his people (see comment on 9:1–2). They are desolate is not just a description of the state of affairs; the enemy has contributed to this (cf. NRSV: ‘they are laid waste …’ [by the enemy]). Often in Jeremiah and elsewhere in the Old Testament, there is a clear relationship between the people’s behaviour and the impact this has on the people, the land, the city and creation (cf. also v. 12; 4:23–28; Hos. 4:1–3). There are several instances of the word no-one (v. 10, untravelled [TNIV]; NRSV is preferable: ‘so that no one passes through’; also vv. 11, 12). The lowing of cattle is not heard. The Hebrew for cattle contains the same consonants (q, n, h) as the word for lament at the beginning of verse 10. Lamentation has replaced the sound of the cattle, because the land has been destroyed and the animals are gone.

12–16. This passage, written (mainly) in prose, continues the theme of the land being ruined and therefore inaccessible for travellers. Several scholars regard the passage as ‘Deuteronomistic’ (see Introduction, p. 29). However, the first question of verse 12 is remin-iscent of wisdom literature such as Proverbs and Ecclesiastes (see Eccl. 8:1 and questions in Prov. 23:29; Eccl. 2:22; 3:21–22; also Jer. 17:9). Such questions urge the hearers to reflect. In this context, the answer to Who is wise? may be Jeremiah and ‘those who listen to God’s words and do them’ (see vv. 23–24). The reason why the land has been ruined (v. 12) is to be found in verses 13–14, and is familiar to the hearer/reader (see e.g. 7:13, 24–26; 11:7–8, 10; 13:10; 16:10–12; 19:4–5). Therefore (v. 15) strengthens the verdict of verse 12, this time expressed in terms of making the people eat and drink deadly things. The bitter food is an acrid desert shrub (for the poisoned water see 8:14). Both are metaphors for bitter suffering, disaster and death. The expulsion of the people from the Promised Land was a consequence of neglecting the stipulations of the covenant (v. 13; see Deut. 28:64). Verse 13, which speaks of God’s law and walking in the ways of the Lord, contrasts sharply with the description of the people who are stubbornly following their own hearts and worshipping the Baals (v. 14), a problem which began with former generations. Because they have not ‘known’ the Lord (v. 3), they will end up in a land they do not ‘know’ (v. 16), and the Promised Land will be exchanged for a foreign land.

17–22. These verses continue the theme of lamenting and mourning (9:1–2, 10). Imperatives are given to lament (vv. 17, 20). Professional mourners who are especially skilled women are urged to sing their laments as over the dead. In this case, the mourning is not just over one dead person, and their laments over so many can hardly express the grief caused by the enemy. The actual words of the laments are given in verses 19, 21 and 22, using the typical Hebrew metre for a lament. God urgently commands the professional mourners to be summoned quickly (Eccl. 12:5; Amos 5:16) so they can help the people to grieve (v. 18). The most skilful of them are needed because of the huge loss. The reason for so much sadness is that the land lies in ruins (v. 19). The Hebrew reads, ‘They have thrown down our dwellings.’ This deliberate act by the enemies is part of God’s judgment. The professional mourners are addressed directly (vv. 20–21). The laments are to be passed on to the next generation and taught to their daughters, because the mourning will last for some time. Death is personalized, entering people’s homes like a burglar and bringing about the unexpected deaths of young people and children (cf. Isa. 65:20) through disease or war. Even fortified places such as palaces are not safe, for death will be everywhere. Added to all this is the shame and horror of dead bodies lying in the field, without anybody left to bury them (v. 22). No-one lives in the empty and ruined land any more (vv. 11–12), so there are no people to perform burials. (On the horror of corpses lying in the land without being buried, see also 7:33; 8:2; 14:16; 16:4, 6; 1 Kgs 21:23–24.)

Meaning

Judgment is pronounced, but not without reason. After the analysis of a broken society, the logical consequence is introduced (therefore). There is a clear connection between not serving and obeying God and relationships with others: when God is exchanged for other gods and his commandments are ignored, the whole framework of the covenant community falls down, and no-one can be trusted. A major theme in 9:4–9 (and in vv. 2–3) is that of deceit and falsehood. Throughout the book there is the constant struggle between Jeremiah and the ‘false’ prophets who claim to be true messengers of God (e.g. ch. 28). However, falsehood and deceit are present throughout society, and even neighbours cannot be trusted. God’s judgment on his people and nation is not communicated from a distance, but with tears. The vivid expressions in verses 17–22 add a further dimension to the prophetic words of judgment, and bring home the announcement of disaster in a poignant way. To act as if people have actually died is as shocking as Ezekiel enacting the siege of Jerusalem (Ezek. 4).

ii. Boasting – in what? (9:23–26)

Context

The virtues mentioned in verse 24 which belong to God and those who follow him (kindness or steadfast love, justice and righteousness) contrast sharply with the analysis of the people in 9:2–9: ‘knowing God’ (v. 24) as opposed to ‘not knowing him’ (vv. 3, 6). The theme of wisdom (v. 12) recurs in verse 23. So, although verses 23–24 seem to be only loosely related to the previous parts of the chapter, there are connections in thought and theme.1

Comment

23–24. People should not boast/praise themselves because of their wisdom, power or richness. Such things are highly valued in society, but God sees people differently. Faithfulness or ‘loyal love’ (ḥesed), justice and righteousness are part of God’s character (cf. Exod. 34:6–7) and are reflected in those who know him. There is criticism of the ‘wise men’ in 8:8–9, although wisdom itself is not wrong (9:12). The focus of people’s praise should be God and not themselves or their abilities.

25–26. God’s time of punishment will come for other nations in the future, and the shocking thing is that this includes Judah. The nations are circumcised just as Judah was. Circumcision was a widely known practice, although, in the case of Abraham, a whole new meaning was attached to it, and it became a sign of God’s special covenant with his chosen people. In the second half of verse 25, the TNIV refers to nations who live in the desert in distant places. However, the Hebrew can also mean that they are nations in the desert who cut their hair at the edges, which was probably a religious rite forbidden in Israel (see alternative reading in TNIV footnote here, and in Jer. 25:23; 49:32; see also Lev. 19:27; Deut. 14:1). It is problematic that all the nations are called uncircumcised in verse 26, but circumcised in verse 25. TNIV tries to resolve this by adding the word really. This difficult verse may be interpreted thus: all nations, though circumcised in the flesh, count as uncircumcised because of their behaviour, especially Israel, who should have known better!

Meaning

People should serve God according to who he is (v. 24), which is not the reality in Jeremiah’s day. Verse 24 contrasts with 9:3 and 6: the people do not acknowledge God or live according to his commandments. God’s wisdom differs greatly from that of human beings, who often tend to focus on outward appearances (power and wealth) and are impressed by eloquent speech.

The other nations are included in the coming judgment. Judah has not behaved like the covenant people, so they too will be judged as one of the nations. Circumcision does not guarantee escape from judgment that is deserved, just as the temple is not a talisman (Jer. 7:1–15). The apostle Paul reiterates the admonition of verses 23 and 24 in 1 Corinthians 1:31 and 2 Corinthians 10:17.
