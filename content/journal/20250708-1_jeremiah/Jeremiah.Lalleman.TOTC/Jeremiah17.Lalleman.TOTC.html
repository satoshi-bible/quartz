K. Hearts and lives investigated (17:1–27)

i. The way of life and the way of death (17:1–11)

Context

Jeremiah 17 continues several themes from chapter 16, addressing Judah’s sin (v. 1), their idolatry (cf. 16:11–13, 18; 17:2–3) and the ensuing judgment (16:13, 16; 17:3–4). There are further connections in the theme of the stubborn ‘heart’, the seat of people’s sins, and God’s knowledge of our ‘hidden motives’ (16:12, 17; 17:1, 5, 9, 10). The land as God’s inheritance is mentioned in 16:18 and 17:4. For links with verses 12–18, see below.

Comment

17:1–4. An iron stylus, a tool used to engrave stone or metal, with a point made of hard material (some translate ‘diamond’), has been used to engrave sin on the hearts of the people of Judah. Sin is deeply embedded in their hearts. The Ten Commandments were written on tablets of stone, but instead of keeping them in their hearts (Deut. 6:6), sin has settled deep within. (For the image of ‘tablet of the heart’, see also Prov. 3:3; 7:3; on God writing his laws in their hearts, see Jer. 31:33.) The horns of an altar were four high points, one at each corner (see Exod. 27:2). Altars like this have been found by archaeologists. The word for their altars is a slight alteration from the Hebrew, which reads ‘your altars’ (so NKJV). Even in worship, sin has become normal. The people have set up illegitimate altars used for illicit worship, but they have also used legitimate worship in a way that conflicts with its real meaning: they have been sinning persistently, rendering their use of sacrifices hypocritical.

Even the children know about the worship on the high hills and near green trees (cf. Jer. 2) of the female goddess Asherah (com-panion of Baal, but sometimes even considered to be the companion of God). Asherah poles (v. 2) can also mean ‘sacred poles’ (so NRSV). They represented the goddess. Syncretistic worship is passed on within the family. The Hebrew of verse 3 is difficult, with several options, and translations may differ considerably. The TNIV reading (My mountain) must refer to Mount Zion, which will be captured and plundered by the enemy, as will the high places where idol worship took place. The rest of the verse resembles 15:13, and verse 4 resembles 15:14 (see comments there). For the land as Israel’s inheritance, see 3:18–19; 16:18.

5–8. These verses remind us on the one hand of the curses and blessings in Deuteronomy 27 – 28, and on the other hand of wisdom literature and wisdom psalms such as Psalm 1. If people rely only on mortals (flesh) and turn away from God, their lives will be like a shrub in a desert area. They will not see prosperity (v. 6; lit. ‘when/if good comes’). Even when rain comes, the shrub in the desert will not flourish. The salt land where no-one lives is reminiscent of the area around the Dead Sea and Sodom and Gomorrah, a place of barrenness and judgment (see also 4:26; 6:8). These people are the complete antithesis of those who trust not in mortals but in God (vv. 7–8). They do not turn away, but turn towards him in all matters of life. Difficult times may come (heat, v. 8), but their lives are rooted in the Source of life, always bearing fruit. God is the spring of living water (v. 13). These verses can be applied both to individuals and to the people and leaders who trusted in mortals and sought security from various allies (2:13–19, 36), instead of trusting in the Lord and his strength.

9–10. Again this is a ‘wisdom saying’, using a rhetorical question to describe the general nature of human beings. However, it fits in a prophetic context, since it emphasizes that no-one is without sin, and this applies to the prophet’s own time. This prophetic message of warning is in the context of analysing the nation’s situation. The people are ‘incurably ill’ (see also 15:18). Only God knows what is really in people’s hearts, minds and thoughts, what are their deepest emotions. He gives them what they deserve. Judgment was deserved in Jeremiah’s day, and God also knew what his enemies had plotted against him (17:14–18). The word for [examine the] mind is literally ‘kidneys’ (see comment on 11:20).

11. Again, this is a ‘wisdom saying’ (‘like this … is’, cf. Prov. 25:11–14). Those who have become rich by treating others unjustly, like King Jehoiakim (22:13–17), are compared to a partridge, a bird that was thought to hatch eggs it had not laid; the chicks would later leave the nest to return to their own mother. This explanation fits the TNIV translation; those who have gained riches unjustly will also have their illegitimate possessions taken away in the end. There is some uncertainty about how to translate the Hebrew, since the word for ‘hatch’ occurs only here and in Isaiah 34:15, and can also mean ‘to gather (as a brood)’. Craigie, Kelley and Drinkard (1991: 228–229) translate: ‘A partridge broods but he will not hatch’, so the partridge loses its eggs, rather than steals them (as in TNIV). But the main point of comparison remains the same: in the end, the rich will lose what they thought was theirs. Craigie, Kelley and Drinkard (1991: 229) also point to the fact that in Hebrew the word for ‘fool’ at the end of the verse sounds very much like ‘carcass’: death will be the end of all earthly wealth. To die when life is half gone means to die prematurely, which is what happened to King Jehoiakim who died at the age of thirty-six (2 Kgs 23:36). To have a long and fulfilled life was seen as a blessing. This comment about the rich reminds us of the parable of Jesus in Luke 12:16–21.

Meaning

The sinful ways of individuals and the people as a whole will lead to disaster, because they have forgotten the Lord. Whatever is hidden in people’s hearts is not hidden from God. He will reveal the truth about every human being. This is true of the nation and also of individuals. The choice between God and the idols, truth and deceit, is ultimately a choice which will result in blessing or curse.

ii. A prophet’s plea (17:12–18)

Context

Jeremiah 17:12–18 is another ‘Confession’ of Jeremiah, the third one (see Introduction, p. 51). Although verses 12–13 are often taken with the previous passage, there is good reason to understand them as part of the lament of verses 14–18. Verses 12–13 form an introduction to the lament, in which God is addressed. The verb ‘to put to shame’ (v. 13) recurs in verse 18. This time the cause of the prophet’s lament is that his hearers are saying that the predicted judgment will not come true. His enemies ridicule him. However, Jeremiah has not chosen to speak about the matter of judgment himself (v. 16); God has commanded him to do so. It is not something Jeremiah delights in.

Several themes link verses 12–18 with the previous verses, such as the people who turn away from the Lord (vv. 5, 13), the metaphor of drought (v. 6) and turning away from the spring of living water (v. 13), and God who ‘knows’ what is in people’s hearts, both good and bad (vv. 10, 16). The contrast in verses 5–11 between those who trust in the Lord and those who forsake him is highlighted in verses 12–18 in the faithful and true prophet and his adversaries who have turned away from God and planned bad things. Thus, those who sin against the Lord and those who offend the prophet are linked in this chapter.

Comment

12–13. As in the psalms of lament (e.g. Pss 4, 6, 13, 28, 80), Jeremiah starts his prayer by addressing God and, in this case, his glorious throne (see also 14:21). The place of our sanctuary is also addressed (rather than TNIV reading: A glorious throne … is the place of our sanctuary). The throne of God is in heaven (Ps. 11:4; Isa. 66:1) and his dwelling is ‘on high’ (exalted, v. 12), but on the other hand, God dwells in the temple on Mount Zion (Ps. 26:8). This tension is expressed in 1 Kings 8:27. In his lament, Jeremiah focuses first on God and his glory (v. 12). Then in verse 13 he turns his attention to those who turn away from God, using a concentric structure:

A – God is the hope of Israel

B – Those who forsake him will be ashamed

C – Those turning away from him will be written in the dust (either they will go to the ‘underworld’ or they will die early as a punishment)

B’ – Because they have forsaken God

A’ – God who is the spring of living water

Thus, the central idea is the fate of those who turn away from you. This fate which awaits the enemies of the prophet is linked to the following verses. There is a link too with the previous passage: the people whose ‘written sin’ is engraved on their hearts (v. 1) will eventually be written in the dust. For forsaking the spring of living water, see also 2:13. The Hebrew words for place (v. 12), hope and spring (v. 13) share several consonants. Such repetitions add emphasis to the message. In 15:18 the prophet accused God of being like a spring that fails, but here the expression is a positive one, in the context of the prophet crying out for God’s help.

14–18. Most of the verbs in these verses express an urgent request, which is reminiscent of the psalms of lament asking for healing (e.g. Pss 6:4; 41:4). Jeremiah is ‘ill’, just as the people are ‘ill’ (8:22; 14:19), because he is suffering as a result of his message. God is the subject of the verbs in verse 14, followed by the prophet as subject. Van Selms (1980: 180) says that this is a way of expressing that if God does something, he does it thoroughly. Verse 15 quotes the prophet’s opponents: they do not believe disaster will actually come (cf. 2 Pet. 3:4). Jeremiah does not enjoy bringing such a message of doom, but he has not run away from his task. The beginning of verse 16 could mean that he did not insist on being a shepherd, as is clear from Jeremiah 1. Jones (1992: 247) changes the text slightly, since the Hebrew for from being your shepherd is somewhat unclear and looks very similar to the word for ‘evil’, meaning that the prophet has not desired judgment, the ‘evil day’. The day of despair (v. 16), judgment day (lit. ‘the incurable’ day), is the same word as in verse 9. It is the ‘day of YHWH’ in Amos (see Amos 5:18). Jeremiah pleads that God will be for him, not against him, that he will be vindicated, and not harmed. (For the ‘prayer of revenge’, see the Additional note, p. 138.)

Meaning

This passage is a very personal cry for help by a prophet in despair. The only one who can vindicate him is God who called him to be his prophet. Jeremiah has been faithful (v. 16), even when his message was hard and disaster had to be announced. Jeremiah is on the right side and he clings in despair to God who is his refuge in the day of disaster (v. 17). He is an example of those who trust in the Lord (v. 7).

iii. Keep the Sabbath holy! (17:19–27)

Context

No date is given for verses 19–27, which are written in prose, whereas verses 1–18 are mostly poetry. A possible link between these two sections of Jeremiah 17 may be that two ways are offered to the people (cf. vv. 5–6, 7–8), one leading to life and happiness, the other to death and disaster (cf. vv. 24, 27; see also v. 21: ‘For the sake of your lives …’ NRSV, following Hebrew reading). The beginning of verse 19 links with the opening words of Jeremiah 18 and 19 (God addresses the prophet and commands him to go and stand, go down, go and buy). There is still an opportunity for the people to change and secure Judah’s future (vv. 24, 25), so Jeremiah 17:19–27 may be dated to the time before Jehoiakim’s burning of the scroll (see Introduction, p. 24).

Comment

19–20. Another sermon (see Jer. 7 and 11), again at a strategic place, one of the gates of Jerusalem (cf. 7:1–2), is addressed to all the people – kings, officials, inhabitants of the city and Judah in general.

21–23. The issue at stake is that the Sabbath day is not being kept holy and separated from the rest of the week. Business is just carrying on as normal. This is a serious matter, for God commanded the Sabbath day to be kept special when he confirmed his covenant with Israel and gave them the Ten Commandments (Exod. 20:8–10). It is a matter of death and life, as verse 21 reads: ‘For the sake of your lives …’ (NRSV). The people have ignored this rule from the beginning; the ancestors were as stubborn as the present generation (see also 7:24, 26; 11:8).

24–27. The people have two options: ‘If you listen … the royal house, all the people, Jerusalem, Judah, the temple worship will all be blessed and flourish’ – in other words, all the components that make up the identity of the people of God (vv. 24–26; for a similar construction, see 22:4). People will come from everywhere in the land to worship God in his temple. Six different parts of the land are mentioned: the towns of Judah and the villages around Jerusalem, the land of Benjamin north of Jerusalem, the Shephelah (the western foothills, TNIV) or western flat area, the hill country in the centre and finally the southern part, the Negev (see also 32:44; 33:13). For the different kinds of sacrifice, see Leviticus 1 – 7. Verse 27 mentions the alternative option: ‘If you do not listen … Jerusalem will be burned and destroyed’ (see 7:20; 15:14; 21:12). For a similar oracle of judgment, see Amos 1:4, 7, 10, 12, 14; 2:2, 5.

Meaning

Keeping the Sabbath as a day set apart for God is a sign of Israel’s commitment to the God who entered into a covenant with them. In the context of Jeremiah, it is not ‘just’ about keeping one commandment; it is about a whole lifestyle in which God is taken seriously. Those who trust in the Lord (v. 7) will follow his commandments.
