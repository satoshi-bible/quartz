B. A wounded nation (8:4 – 9:3)

Context

A poetic section now follows after 7:1 – 8:3, which is mainly prose, and runs through to 10:25, with some short prose sections in between. The themes here are interrelated, but there is a division after Jeremiah 9:1–3 (MT: 9:2). The early verses of Jeremiah 9 cannot be separated from the final part of chapter 8, so 8:4 – 9:3 are taken as one section.

The passage does not provide any historical information, but there is a sense of the inevitability of judgment, which suggests that it belongs to the latter half of Jeremiah’s proclamation (i.e. the reign of King Jehoiakim, when idolatry and injustice were part of daily life again). Jeremiah 8:13 and 20 mention a bad harvest, linking the passage to 14:1, just as the lament in 14:17 has similarities with 8:21 and 9:1. This verse section (8:4–22) refers to famine, and future disaster in terms of war.

Two rhetorical questions are followed by why then? (8:4–5, 19, 22). This stylistic device (also in 2:14, 31; 14:19; 22:28; 49:1) highlights the absurdity of a situation. It may cause the people to think about the abnormality of their behaviour (see below).

The whole passage can be divided as follows: verses 4–7, the people’s unnatural behaviour; verses 8–12, deceptive leaders; verses 13–17, God’s judgment; 8:18 – 9:3, laments.

Comment

4–5. As in previous chapters (Jer. 2; 3:1), the verb šûb plays an important role. The basic meaning is ‘turn’, but it has a range of connotations (e.g. return, turn away, repent; see Introduction, p. 37). The example in verse 4 describes normal behaviour: if someone falls, they usually try to get up again as soon as possible; if they turn away, they normally return. These general truths contrast with the abnormal behaviour of the people of God (they don’t return; on the contrary, they turn away from God even more). Jerusalem even acquires a nickname: ‘the one who constantly turns away’ (Lundbom 1999: 509 – ‘the rebel perpetual’, although this is less clear in TNIV: Why … always turn away?). They refuse to return (again using the verb šûb). The rhetorical questions function as an accusation. It is not normal for people not to return after they walk away: God’s people are behaving in an unnatural way, and the results should not surprise them!

6–7. The I who has listened carefully may refer to God (Craigie, Kelley and Drinkard 1991: 133; Holladay 1986: 277), continuing the opening words of verse 4, although others (Thompson 1980: 298; Lundbom 1999: 509) think it is the prophet. The picture of the warhorse storming into battle makes it clear that the people are incorrigible in their pursuit of wrongdoing. They continue in their ways, without any sign of repentance. Another animal metaphor is used to describe the people (v. 7). The birds know the times for their migration (set by God), but the people do not know the requirements of the LORD. The birds instinctively fly south and back again at the appropriate time. Human beings, on the other hand, do not just rely on their instincts, but are able to make deliberate decisions. However, their behaviour is totally unnatural: they forget about their God and his will. Various forms of the verb ‘to know’ crop up several times in these verses – of the birds who ‘know’ and the people who ‘do not know’. The theological meaning of the verb has the sense of ‘living in a relationship with God’ – it is not that the people do not know anything about God, but their relationship with him does not influence their daily behaviour (also 9:3). The word observe, used of the birds, is the typical term for observing the commandments of the Lord, again highlighting the contrast between the behaviour of the birds and that of the people. This is expressed in the Hebrew text: the word for stork sounds like ḥesed (loyal or covenant love), emphasizing the people’s lack of faithfulness compared with the loyalty of the birds, who do what is expected of them. Furthermore, the Hebrew for swift is very similar to the word for horse (v. 6), and the Hebrew for dove sounds like the word for law (v. 8), thus reminding the people of their lack of knowledge of the law, which is obvious from their behaviour. In Israel many birds can be spotted on their way to the south. The Hebrew names are not always clear, and translations of verse 7b may differ. However, the wordplays in Hebrew are used to underline the prophetic message.

8–9. How can you say …? This may be addressed to the people in general, but the remaining verses focus on the scribes and the wise. Scribes would have worked in the court, but here the reference is specifically to priestly scribes who were meant to hand on the law of God. However, they have produced what is a ‘lie’, a key term in Jeremiah (cf. 7:8; 9:3, 5). They have added things that are against God’s will, which will lead the people astray. Such wisdom is not wisdom at all. The wise are not worth their name, and they will be condemned and put to shame, instead of being honoured.

10–12. See also 6:13–15 for a parallel of these verses, which may also have been used elsewhere in various contexts. The judgment on the wise will be the curses for not keeping the covenant (see Deut. 28:30). However, in the remainder of verse 10, judgment is announced to a wider audience. It is not only the wise who are guilty, but so are the prophets and priests. The spiritual leaders have led the nation astray. The Hebrew word for deceit is the same as lying and falsely (v. 8; see further 6:13–15).

13. The Hebrew of this verse is difficult. The verse actually opens with two words (verbs) which have the same sound in Hebrew, meaning ‘gathering I will end them’. Several alternatives have been suggested. TNIV reads take away for the Hebrew word ‘to gather’, and interprets the verse as judgment in the form of a lack of fruit. Another reading is that God is trying to find grapes and figs (=fruit) in Israel (see 2:21; 6:9; Isa. 5:1–7), but to no avail (see NRSV). Therefore judgment will follow, and there will be no harvest (vv. 16, 20). The people’s spiritual state is reflected in the condition of the land (cf. 4:23–28). The final part of the verse (lit. ‘and I gave them [they] will pass away from them’) is also difficult. It may indicate that what is eventually left in terms of fruit will also be taken away.

14–16. These verses are presented as the words of the people. If disaster comes, perhaps the fortified cities can hide us! The verb for perish, used twice, means literally ‘to be silent’ and, with God as the subject, ‘to silence’ (see NKJV). The poisoned water (cf. 9:15; 23:15) recalls the story of the golden calf, when Moses gave the people powdered water to drink as a judgment (Exod. 32:20). The water may have been poisoned by a plant, or perhaps the enemy has cut off the fresh water supply so that only filthy water is available. However, this refers essentially to the bitterness of judgment through disaster and suffering. The peace and healing promised by the prophets and priests (v. 11) has turned out to be a far cry from reality. The report of disaster will come first from Dan, a city in the north (see 4:15–16). Horses were used in warfare.

17. In Numbers 21:6–9 we read of poisonous snakes sent to punish the people. But here the snakes may just indicate the idea of destruction and disaster sent by God. Nothing will be able to prevent it, not even a bronze snake (Num. 21:9).

8:18 – 9:3. The laments in 8:18–22 and 9:1–3 may be seen as a reaction to the previous verses and can be structured as follows:

18 Lament of the prophet
19a Listen! Word of the prophet
19b Lament of the people: Is God not …?
19c God’s answer: Why have they …?
20 Lament of the people
21 Lament of the prophet
22 Lament of the prophet and the people
9:1–3 Lament of the prophet or God, including accusations

18. The Hebrew here is difficult, and translations differ greatly. The first word in Hebrew can be translated as ‘my joy’, ‘my cheerfulness’ (‘My joy is gone’, NRSV). However, the Septuagint has ‘incurable’, leading the editors of the BHS to suggest that the word should be split in two, meaning ‘without healing’, and added to the end of verse 17. Lundbom (1999: 530–531) translates ‘my joy is gone’, by changing the text slightly and reading an archaic form of the infinitive absolute of the verb ‘lh (‘to go up, away’). But the word can also be an archaic form of ‘l, meaning ‘to, upon, over’ (Gesenius/ Kautzsch, 1980: 304). The translation then reads something along the lines of: ‘My cheerfulness upon me, distress in me, my heart is ill’, with the implied meaning: ‘Is there any cheerfulness in my distress? No, there is not.’ Lundbom (1999: 531) takes the sentence as a tricolon (having three parts), rather than a bicolon: ‘My joy is gone – grief is upon me – my heart is sick.’

19. This verse follows the pattern of verses 4–5 and 22: three rhetorical questions which together form an accusation. Of course God is in Zion: he has not left the people, but they have left him! TNIV’s rendering, from a land far away, can mean ‘from the exile’, but that implies that Jeremiah anticipates their cry. The words can also be translated as ‘from the land far and wide’ (see NRSV) or ‘from everywhere in the land’, which makes more sense. The expression worthless … idols (one plural word in Hebrew) has the connotation of ‘idleness, nothingness’, and it is the same word used in Ecclesiastes to signify something vain or meaningless – just a breath of air which is gone quickly. These idols are ‘nothings’ (see also Jer. 10). In the structure above, verse 19c is clearly central to the thoughts expressed here. The answer to why disaster happens is to be found in the fact that the people are forsaking their God. Their worship of other gods has provoked God to anger (cf. 7:18).

20. There may have been a literal drought (see v. 13) with the danger of famine, but this verse may also refer to the hopelessness and disaster caused by the enemy coming into the land.

21–22. Since my people are crushed (lit. ‘broken’) speaks of an incurable wound (see v. 11), a breach that cannot be healed (cf. 8:15 and see Introduction, p. 42). The word used for healing (v. 22) means literally ‘new skin which grows in the place of a wound’. In Gilead, in the Transjordan area, balm from a tree was famous as a perfume and also as a medicine (Gen. 37:25, Jer. 46:11). One would expect healing from such an excellent medicine, but there is none. The wound must be treated more thoroughly. Priests and prophets have tried to ‘heal’ the people, offering reassurance without calling them to a radical repentance and return to God (cf. 30:12–17).

9:1–3. Verse 9:1 corresponds to 8:23 in the Hebrew Bible, and the Hebrew reads ‘the slain of the daughter of my people’. The expression ‘daughter of my people’ is used in the context of lament and accusation, where God’s people are personified as a woman (see on 4:11). Is God the speaker here or is it the prophet? Verse 3 (Heb. 9:2) ends with a confirmation that the word of the Lord is being spoken. It is hard to decide whether verses 1–2 are the Lord’s or a personal expression of the prophet’s grief, and maybe there is no compelling reason to do so. The verses may be deliberately ambivalent. The prophet speaks God’s words, and he is a spokesman for God and the people. It is clear from verses such as Jeremiah 31:20 that God suffers on account of the disobedience of his people and the judgment he is bound to bring upon them. The prophet, or God, wish they could run away from the people into the desert and stay in one of the lodging places meant for travellers. They want to run away from an ‘adulterous’ people; this description may be used literally, or metaphorically with reference to their worship of other gods (see Jer. 2). Moreover, the Israelites use their tongue like a bow, to shoot lies (v. 3), which means that both the leaders and the people cannot be trusted (see 8:8, 10 for the same use of the Hebrew word ‘falsehood’, ‘lie’). A society full of deceit is doomed to break down: no-one is safe and no-one can be trusted, as indicated in the following verses. The end of verse 3 provides a clue to the previous laments, which started in Jeremiah 8:18 (lit. ‘and me they do not know – word of the LORD’). To ‘know’ or acknowledge the Lord means to live in a relationship with him by following his commands (see also comment on 8:7). This ‘knowledge’ results in a life that is characterized by righteous, honest, upright behaviour, contrasting with the society of Jeremiah’s day.

Meaning

These verses challenge the behaviour of the people and their leaders, using dramatic poetic language and images from the natural realm. The vivid description of the coming disaster is reminiscent of chapters 4 – 6. The laments that follow add greater weight to the prophetic (=God’s) message.

Laments frequently alternate with the analysis of a sinful people and the consequent announcements of judgment. In 8:18 – 9:3 the diagnosis of the nation’s situation contrasts sharply with that given by the prophets and priests. Priests in Israel, whose role was to check whether people had been genuinely healed, are accused of superficial healing, which makes the wounds worse (vv. 11, 22). Judgment reveals the truth, but it is not announced with dry eyes.
