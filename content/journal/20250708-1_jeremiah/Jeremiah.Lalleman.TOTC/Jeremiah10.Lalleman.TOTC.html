D. Of idols and judgment (10:1–25)

Lundbom (1999: 333) describes Jeremiah 4:5 as the start of a new collection of oracles of judgment and laments (as previously mentioned on Jer. 4), ending at 10:22, and speaks of a ‘“foelament” collection’. He regards Jeremiah 10 as an addition to the original collection. The theme of the enemy from the north bringing desolation and disaster is dealt with in 4:5–7, as well as in 10:22, thus forming an inclusio. In the commentary below, however, verses 17–25 are taken as a literary unit, with the theme of judgment binding them together.

i. Idols are ‘nothings’ (10:1–16)

Context

Verses 1–16 address the house of Israel. This need not be restricted to the northern kingdom, which was exiled in 722 BC, but probably refers to the ‘covenant people of God’ in general. From his early days as a prophet, Jeremiah has spoken out against idolatry (see 2:11, 23, 25, 27; 5:19; 7:9). It is hard to limit this passage to one particular period, although we may think of the time of King Jehoiakim, when all Josiah’s efforts were to no avail. Jeremiah may also have addressed those exiled to Babylon in 597 BC, as he did in a letter in chapter 29, to warn them against the idols of the Babylonians. This would account for the use of Aramaic in verse 11, which was spoken in many parts of the world in those days. Jeremiah refers to the idols, using language that the exiles could use in Babylon.

These warnings concerning idols are similar to what is found in Isaiah (see Isa. 44:9–20). Both passages use irony to demonstrate the absurdity of worshipping wooden images.

Verses 12–16 are repeated in 51:15–19, a prophecy against Babylon, although the verses have their own specific function within each context.

Note the contrast in 1–16 between God and the idols:

(1–2a Introduction)
2–5 The idols of the nations are made by humans
6–7 but God is King over the nations and nobody is like him!
8–9 Idols are lifeless products made by humans
10 but God is the living God!
11 Idols have not created anything
12–13 but God made the heavens and the earth!
14–15 Those who make idols are silly and will perish
16 but Israel’s God made everything – and Israel belongs to him!

Comment

10:2–5. The phrase ways of the nations is a reference to their behaviour, in this case specifically their religious behaviour. Israel should be completely different (Deut. 7:1–6). The signs in the sky allude to the widespread astrological practices of the nations in the Ancient Near East. Israel was frequently tempted to worship sun, moon and stars (see 8:2; 2 Kgs 21:3, 5). King Josiah had tried to ban these practices in Judah (2 Kgs 23:4), but without lasting success. Verses 3–5 describe in detail how the nations went about making their idols, but it’s all worthless, idleness, ‘nothing’. The word heḅel used here is prominent in the book of Ecclesiastes. The idols are ‘nothings’; they are like vapour, a breath that disappears immediately. These worthless and silly (v. 8) objects are made by human beings, who, by their own power, cut down wood in the forest in order to make an idol which is then adorned by a human craftsman. Moreover, if these wooden statues are not fastened down, they will fall over! Jeremiah even compares them with a scarecrow in a melon patch, or a ‘field with cucumbers’. Usually the word for scarecrow means ‘palm tree’, but in this context the former is a better translation. Idols were carried around in processions, as in Babylon (Isa. 46:1–2). The word for walk has the sense of walking in a procession rather than just going somewhere, so it is highly appropriate here. There is a wordplay in the second half of verse 5: Do not fear them and they can do no harm sound similar in Hebrew. The idols have no power at all to do harm or good, so they are not to be feared.

6–7. There is no-one like the God of Israel. He is the King, and the Ruler over all the nations who worship other gods. He is the one to be feared, not the idols! The universal power of God is opposed to the gods of Babylon, Assyria or any other region or land. The confession that there is no-one like you comes at the beginning and end of these verses, thus forming an inclusio to emphasize the importance of the statement., Similar confessions are found in the psalms (see Pss 48:1; 86:8, 10; 95:3; 96:4; 97:9).

8–10. The gods of the nations are all senseless and foolish (‘both stupid and foolish’, NRSV). TNIV’s senseless does not adequately express the silliness of the matter. The second half of the verse is not easy to translate (lit. ‘instruction of worthless things/nothings – wood it is’; see also v. 3). Obviously, the idols (they) are meant to be the subject of the sentence, rather than human beings, as in TNIV. So ‘wood it is’ refers to the idols, which are made of wood and therefore their instruction is worthless (or ‘the instruction given by idols is no better than wood!’, NRSV). In other words, the instruction of worthless idols is worthless itself. Everything is done to make the idols look beautiful and royal (v. 9; blue and purple are the colours of royalty). The silver and gold used to adorn them is taken from the best sources, no matter how far away they may be. The exact locations of Tarshish and Uphaz are unknown. If Tarshish is in southern Spain, as some suggest, this would have been the furthest end of the known world in those days. The places are famous for their treasures of gold and silver (see Ezek. 27:12; Dan. 10:5). Ironically, the idols are made by skilled workers (v. 9): they are beautiful pieces of art, but completely worthless. But how different is the true God, the living God, the eternal King (v. 10), who is active in the world and can do good. However, he can also cause disaster when he shows his anger to those who sin (cf. v. 5), namely the idol-worshipping nations.

11–13. The Aramaic of verse 11 (in prose) has a chiastic structure: heavens – earth – earth – heavens. The idols have not created the heavens and the earth; indeed, they will disappear from them altogether. As a powerful contrast, God, the Maker of heaven and earth, is glorified. The structure in verses 12–13 is: earth – heavens – heavens – earth. These words are all-encompassing: those who ‘do not have any creative power at all’ are contrasted with the power, wisdom and understanding of God, who makes his presence known in creation, and even provides the rain (an impossible feat for Baal or any other god). To ‘stretch out the heavens’ (v. 12) like a tent is a description also found in Psalm 104:2. The prophet Amos used the same kind of ‘creation-language’ (see Amos 4:13; 5:8).

14–16. Human beings who trust in idols are themselves ‘silly’ and ‘stupid’ (see v. 8). Images of idols are a fraud, (Hebrew word for ‘lie’) and a delusion; they are worthless, ‘nothing’ (v. 15; see also vv. 3, 8). These objects of mockery are ridiculous things, and they will perish. But not so the Almighty God (lit. the ‘LORD of hosts’). He is the mighty one, the Maker (NRSV: ‘for he is the one who formed …’, using the same verb as in Gen. 2:8) of everything. TNIV’s translation, including Israel, gives the impression that Israel is just one among the nations. However, the thought is rather that God has a specific relationship with Jacob/Israel, and they are committed to one another: God is Jacob’s Portion, that which is specifically assigned to him (cf. Pss 16:5; 73:26; 119:57), and Israel is the tribe of his inheritance (Deut. 9:26, 29). This verse is a reminder that Israel is, and indeed should be, different from the other nations, and should keep away from idol worship, the ways of the nations (v. 2).

Meaning

In a world full of impressive statues and idols, the people of the invisible God have to be reminded repeatedly that he has the power. The impressive reliefs and statues of Ancient Near Eastern gods displayed in the British Museum give an indication of the kind of world the people of God were facing on a daily basis, first in Egypt and later in Babylon. In such an environment, it takes courage to stand firm and believe in the only true and living God, Creator of everything.

God is not restricted to one piece of land, but is the Creator of heaven and earth. He committed himself to one nation and remained faithful to the people, even though they were expelled from their land (see Block: 2000).

ii. Variations on the theme of judgment (10:17–25)

Context

Jeremiah 10:17–25 seems to be loosely connected to the previous passage and to consist of several separate verses brought together. However, there is repetition of the word ‘stupid’ (NRSV; senseless, TNIV) in verses 8, 14 and 21, and verses 17–25 consist of further reflections on the exile/judgment to come as a result of Israel’s idolatry.

The passage can be subdivided as follows:

17–18 God’s judgment: exile
19–20 Lament in the I-form
21 Those responsible for disaster
22 Judgment is near!
23–24 Prayer for reduced punishment
25 Prayer for wrath to be poured out on the enemy

The poetic language vividly describes the horror of the coming judgment.

Comment

17–18. Gather up your belongings (Hebrew means ‘your bundle’). The city of Jerusalem is addressed here as a female. Assyrian reliefs in the British Museum show exiles carrying their bundles of belongings over their shoulders. NRSV has ‘Gather up your bundle from the ground’ (cf. to leave the land, TNIV). It is a vivid description of what will happen when the exile comes, just as Ezekiel had to enact the exile by carrying out his luggage (Ezek. 12:1–7). God will fling his people away, far from the Promised Land (see also 22:26 about what will happen to King Jehoiachin). The literal reading of the end of verse 18 is: ‘so that they find’. Translators have tried to expand on this (so that they may be captured, TNIV). A better translation is ‘so that they find out …’ (i.e. what judgment means), but the line was possibly left open to make people think. Poetic language is not always as logical and nicely polished as we would like.

19–21. This lament is similar to the one in 4:19–21. Verse 20 certainly does not refer to the prophet’s own suffering, since he had no children. So is it Jerusalem or Judah who is speaking? And who is the speaker of verse 19? As we have noticed elsewhere (4:19–21; 9:1–2), it is probably not an either-or situation. The prophet utters words in the name of the people, but suffers because of them as well. And throughout all the laments and accusations, we hear God’s hurt because of what has become of his beautiful child (or ‘wife’, as in Jer. 2). For the use of ‘medical metaphors’ in verse 19, see Introduction, p. 42. This is my sickness can also be translated as ‘this is suffering (I must bear it) …’ Verse 19 describes a mortally wounded person, whereas verse 20 uses the image of a tent of Bedouins which is destroyed by the enemy. The shelter is literally the tent curtains. Sons would usually help to set up the curtains and pitch the tent for their father, but they are no longer alive, a terrible prospect in a society where children were the providers for their aging parents. The leaders, or shepherds as kings were frequently called in the Ancient Near East (cf. 23:1–4), are guilty of bringing on the disaster of the exile. They are ‘stupid’ (rather than senseless, see vv. 8, 14), because they refused to ‘seek’ the Lord (see Deut. 4:29; Jer. 29:13; Amos 5:4, 6). Because of bad leadership, the people have ended up scattered like lost sheep (see 23:1–2).

22. The enemy comes from the north (see 4:5–8, 13–17 for a similar vivid description of the approaching enemy, followed by a lament 4:19–21). The picture of wild animals, which adds to the feeling of desolation, is also found in other passages (see 9:11; 49:33; 51:37). It conjures up an image of ruins inhabited by birds of prey and other animals (see Zeph. 2:13–15).

23–24. This prayer acknowledges God’s sovereignty in judgment, but at the same time pleads for a softening of what is to come. This is the same kind of supplication found in 3:21–25, spoken by the prophet on behalf of the people.

25. The plea to God not to pour out his full anger on his own people is matched by a prayer asking God to be angry with those who are responsible for Israel’s disaster. Punishment is deserved, and it will be God’s initiative (vv. 23–24), but that does not mean that the enemy is not guilty of bringing disaster. Jacob, a name that reminds God and the people of past promises, has been ‘eaten’ or devoured, like the sheep of a flock (v. 21). His homeland or ‘habitation’ (NRSV) has been ruined. Amos also uses Jacob’s name in his plea to God not to destroy Israel, for he is so small (Amos 7:2, 5).

Meaning

Judgment is not announced ‘with dry eyes’, in an indifferent way. The prophetic message alternates between harsh words and laments. In Jeremiah’s pain over the coming disaster in verses 19 and 20, we see judgment enacted, as it were, and it becomes visible and tangible. It depicts the people’s suffering and God’s hurt over his lost son. Several centuries later, another Prophet would cry over the fate of Jerusalem (Luke 19:41–44).
