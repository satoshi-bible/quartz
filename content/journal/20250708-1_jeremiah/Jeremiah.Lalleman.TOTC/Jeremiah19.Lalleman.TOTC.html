M. Brokenness all over (19:1–15)

Context

Jeremiah 19 continues the theme of the potter’s handiwork. As in chapter 18, this message of doom is to be illustrated by the vivid scene of a clay jar being broken in front of a delegation of the people. Since there seems to be no escape from judgment, the date of these events can be placed after the scroll had been burned by King Jehoiakim (see Introduction, p. 24).

The division of Jeremiah 19 and 20 is somewhat artificial, since the story continues. Lundbom (1999: 842–843) therefore regards 19:14 – 20:6 as a unit. Verses 14–15 can be read as a summary of the previous message in front of the people, with the following structure:

19:1–3a Go, buy and speak!
19:3b–9 Content of God’s message: Judah’s sin and its consequences
19:10–11a Break and say!
19:11b–13 Content of God’s message
19:14–15 Summary for the people

The phrase this place is used several times (vv. 3, 4 [twice], 6, 7, 12). In some cases the allusion is obviously to Jerusalem (v. 12) or Topheth/the Valley of Ben Hinnom (v. 6), but sometimes it can refer to both of them (v. 3). Maybe the ambivalence is deliberate. At the end of Jeremiah’s proclamation, all hearers should be aware that they are included in this message of judgment, which can be applied to Jerusalem and its inhabitants, the temple, the temple area, the surroundings of Jerusalem and the whole of Judah (see also the comments on 7:3–8 for similar ambivalence).

Comment

19:1–3a. Jeremiah is told to buy a clay jar, probably used for water, at the potter’s house (cf. 13:1; 18:1 for a similar command). The elders of the people and of the priests are the senior, and therefore important, people in public and religious life, who act as representatives of the whole nation, so the act is a public affair. Jeremiah is instructed to take them to a place of idolatry where sacrifices to Molek have been conducted (cf. 7:31; 32:35; 2 Kgs 23:10; 2 Chr. 33:6). The Valley of Ben Hinnom (‘of the son of Hinnom’, NRSV) was situated south-west of Jerusalem. The Potsherd Gate (lit. ‘the Gate of Potsherds’) may have been the gate leading out of the city into the Ben Hinnom Valley where broken pottery was probably dumped. It can be identified with the Dung Gate of Nehemiah 2:13 and 3:13–14.

3b–9. The words spoken in the presence of the elders and priests are addressed to all of Jerusalem, including the kings during Jeremiah’s lifetime. Because of what follows (i.e. idolatry and child sacrifice), we may also include kings from the past, such as Ahaz and Manasseh (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6). God will bring judgment on this place, meaning Jerusalem, but it may also refer to the Valley of Hinnom. Judgment is near (‘I am about to bring disaster’; the participle used here indicating that judgment is imminent). For make the ears … tingle, see also 1 Samuel 3:11 and 2 Kings 21:12. The sins mentioned here occur frequently in Jeremiah’s message (see 7:31; 11:12–13; 16:11–12; 25:6–7; 32:30–35). The words ‘have profaned this place’ (v. 4, NRSV) or made this a place of foreign gods (TNIV) are (lit.) ‘and they have made this place foreign/alien’. This unique phrase expresses the thought that something completely alien to the nature of the people of God has taken place. The gods they have served are foreign gods (5:19; 8:19), so Jerusalem, God’s city, the temple and the surrounding area where this defilement has taken place (v. 4), have been made ‘foreign/alien’ as well. God’s city has been made ‘pagan’, as if it did not belong to the one and only God. The phrase with the blood of the innocent may refer to child sacrifices in the Valley of Ben Hinnom, or injustice in court resulting in the death of innocent people (see 2:34; 7:6; 22:3, 17). God had never commanded child sacrifice (v. 5; also 32:35)! The words So beware (‘therefore’, NRSV) in verse 6 introduce the verdict on what is mentioned in verses 4–5. Topheth is the specific place in the Valley of Ben Hinnom where children were sacrificed (see 2 Kgs 23:10 where Josiah makes the place useless; see also Jer. 7:31–32). Apparently Josiah’s reform had not stopped these horrible practices. The Valley of Slaughter (v. 6) is an appropriate name for what happened there, but it mainly indicates what will happen in the future (see v. 7). God will destroy the plans of Judah and Jerusalem (lit. ‘I will pour out’; ‘I will make void’, NRSV). The Hebrew verb sounds like the jar in verses 1 and 10, and makes the connection between ‘breaking the jar’ and ‘breaking Judah and Jerusalem’ because of their sins. So many will be dead that there will be no-one to bury them (see 7:33; 8:1–2; 15:3; 16:4). Passers-by will be so horrified at the sight of the ruined city that they will ‘hiss at it’ (v. 8, twice, as a noun and a verb, see NRSV; see also 18:16; 25:9; 29:18; 42:18; Lam. 2:15–16).

Verse 9 became a reality in the days of Elisha (2 Kgs 6:28–29; Lam. 4:10). The people have sacrificed their children to other gods; now they will kill and eat them, as part of the judgment matching the crime. The repetition of flesh adds to the horrific effect of such a terrible announcement. NRSV reads, ‘And I will make them eat the flesh of their sons and the flesh of their daughters, and all shall eat the flesh of their neighbours in the siege, and in the distress with which their enemies and those who seek their life afflict them.’ The words ‘siege’ and ‘distress’ are almost identical in Hebrew, and, together with the verb ‘afflict’ from which ‘distress’ is derived, they send a threatening message.

10–11a. The symbolic act first mentioned in verse 1 is to be completed, and the jar is to be broken. The words of judgment which accompany this vivid act underline its meaning: similarly, nation and city will be smashed beyond repair. The passage does not mention that Jeremiah actually did what was required, but we may assume so.

11b–13. Topheth, repeated three times, is a horrible, profaned place, a dreadful burial site. And when the place is full, the bodies will just lie there as food for the birds of prey and the beasts (v. 7). That Jerusalem, this place (v. 12), will be turned into a place like Topheth must have sounded terrible to the elders, priests and other people. Jerusalem was God’s holy place, and the temple was thought to be safe from any enemy because of God’s presence (Jer. 7). Ezekiel the priest also had to watch Jerusalem and the temple be defiled through idolatry. In the end, the glory of God had to depart (Ezek. 8 – 11). The ‘defiling’ idolatry (7:30) results in a defiled city. Verse 13 mentions some of the sacrifices: to the sun, moon and stars (8:2; 2 Kgs 23:4–5), and drink offerings to other gods (Jer. 7:18; 32:29; 44:15–25). The ‘whole host of heaven’ (Heb.) has been worshipped, yet the LORD of hosts (Lord Almighty, v. 15) is the only one worth worshipping. He will bring judgment on the people’s false worship.

14–15. The message above is now repeated for a wider audience in the courtyard of the temple. Disaster will come not only on Jerusalem, but also on the surrounding villages. People have hardened their hearts and persisted in ignoring God’s commands (see also 2:27; 7:26; 17:23; 32:33).

Meaning

Jeremiah 19 presents a vivid illustration of broken dreams and illusions. God had other plans for his people, land and city, but because of their idolatry everything has been defiled and is now given over to destruction, to a ‘breaking down’ of everything that was already broken. As mentioned above, the first verb in verse 7 sounds like the word for jar or ‘jug’ in verse 1, which is repeated in verse 10, thus emphasizing the theme of the chapter: Judah and Jerusalem will be broken, destroyed.
