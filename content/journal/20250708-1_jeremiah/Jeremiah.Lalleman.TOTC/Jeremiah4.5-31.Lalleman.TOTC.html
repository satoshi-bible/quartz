C. Chaos announced (4:5–31)

Context

Lundbom (1999: 333) refers to Jeremiah 4:5 as the start of a new collection of oracles of judgment and laments ending at 10:22. He describes this section as a ‘“foe-lament” collection’, with Jeremiah 10 as an addition to the original collection. Jeremiah 4:1–4 and 9:23–26, both with the themes of ‘boasting’ and ‘circumcision of the heart’, are defined as the frame for the original foe-lament collection of 4:5 – 9:22 (Lundbom 1999: 569, 570).

In any case, Jeremiah 4, 5, 6 belong together as pronouncements of judgment. They announce the coming of the enemy from the north who will bring disaster and cause panic and terror (see 1:13–16).

Jeremiah 4:5–31 is like a heightened drama with different ‘actors’: God, the prophet and the people. The passage is full of metaphors and terrifying pictures of a swiftly approaching enemy, bringing war and disaster. Laments accompany this harsh message, which can be dated to the early days of Jeremiah and will have been part of the first scroll mentioned in Jeremiah 36:1–3.

Comment

5–9. Announce in Judah is followed in Hebrew by ‘and in Jerusalem proclaim’, thus forming a chiasm (see Introduction, p. 47). The announcement is urgent and dramatic, as is clear from the many imperatives used. The shofar or trumpet usually sounded at important events (such as the Year of Jubilee, Lev. 25:9) would have been used to announce war. Also, the signal or banner should be raised so that everyone can hear and see the approaching danger. People from the countryside are summoned to the fortified cities for refuge (see also Jer. 8:14). However, in Jeremiah 6:1 the people are told to flee away from the fortified city Jerusalem, which also becomes unsafe. There is panic everywhere. It is God who is making this happen (emphatic I in I am bringing disaster, v. 6). The enemy from the north, already announced in Jeremiah’s call (see also 6:22), is not specified until later, when it turns out to be Babylon (20:4). The expression terrible destruction (lit. ‘a great breach’) recurs in verse 20 (see the explan-ation, p. 93). The enemy is compared to a lion going around, roaring, growling and seeking to devour his prey (v. 7; cf. 2:15; 5:6; also 25:38 where the metaphor is applied to God). Not only will Judah be destroyed, but this applies to other nations as well, a theme developed in Jeremiah 25. (For more on the destroyed and empty land, see v. 29; similar expressions are used in 9:10–11; 12:11.) The people are called to lament and wail as an appropriate reaction to disaster and ruin (v. 8). In Israel people would put on sackcloth, a robe of coarse fabric, and cry out in a loud voice over someone’s death (Gen. 37:34; 2 Sam. 3:31–32; see also Jer. 6:26; 49:3; God’s call for wailing women in 9:17–21; and the lament in 9:10). God’s anger is fierce, burning like fire (see also 4:4). It has not turned away (cf. end of v. 28). The word šûb occurs again, as it does so often in Jeremiah 3. Looking at the passage as a whole, it is clear that, because the people did not turn to God, he will not turn away his anger from them. God declares that both the political and the spiritual leaders (cf. 2:8, 26) will be terrified by what is happening.

10. This is a personal interjection by the prophet (see Jer.1:6 and 14:13). Is he blaming God for ‘deceiving’ the people via the false prophets who said there would be peace (6:13–14; 8:10–11; 14:13; 23:17)? See 1 Kings 22:22 for a similar issue. But God did not send these prophets at all, as is regularly stated in Jeremiah (e.g. 23:9–40). The text critical apparatus suggests an alteration: ‘and they said’ instead of Then I said. However, the difficulty presented by this text is insufficient reason for changing it. The preferred interpretation here is that the prophet is crying out to God because he has allowed (rather than sent) the false prophets to do their work and lead the people astray.

11–18. God will bring his judgment like a scorching wind from the barren heights in the desert. It will be a stronger wind than the one to winnow or cleanse the grain, and it will blow away both grain and chaff. The Hebrew for my people (v. 11) actually reads, ‘the daughter of my people’, an expression often used in Jeremiah in the context of judgment, and in the ensuing lament (see 8:11, 19, 21, 22; 9:1, 7; 14:17). Allen (2008: 66) comments that God’s own anxiety about his people’s judgment is heard in these words, which he paraphrases as ‘my dear people’. The enemy from the north will come over Judah like a storm, with swift chariots and horses (v. 13). Woe expresses terror and fear. Clothes are washed to cleanse them, but in this case it is the heart that needs to be cleansed of evil (v. 14), although 2:22 says that it will be to no avail. How long are words often used in laments by the people (e.g. Pss 13:1–2; 35:17; 74:10; 89:46), but in this case God is lamenting over his people who are persisting in their sinful ways. They harbour wicked thoughts (the Hebrew word translated ‘harbour’ is ‘to lodge’). Sin ‘lives’ in the people’s hearts; it has become their regular guest. A voice is heard from Dan (v. 15), the most northern place in the land (in the former northern kingdom); the message is of disaster approaching from the north (as in vv. 5–6). Again, the nations are included (see v. 7). The hills of Ephraim are situated just north of Jerusalem. The cry goes out that war is rapidly advancing from the border to the centre of the land. The enemy is everywhere, surrounding the people like those who watch over a field to protect it against wild animals (v. 17). This is the outworking of God’s decision to bring judgment on Judah’s rebellion, apparent in all her words and deeds (vv. 17–18).

19–21. The prophet expresses his physical reaction to the approaching disaster. His heart is pounding, and he utters cries of anguish (lit. ‘bowels, innards’, often regarded as the seat of the emotions in Hebrew thought). Oh, the agony of my heart is literally ‘walls of my heart’. The prophet is so overwhelmed by what God has revealed to him in terms of judgment that he laments and cries out in pain and anxiety. The sound of the trumpet (vv. 19, 21) can be linked to verses 5 and 6. Disaster follows disaster (lit. ‘breach upon breach’). The image of a ‘breach’ is familiar in Jeremiah (see v. 6; see further 8:21, where the word crushed is actually ‘broken’; 10:19, where injury is lit. ‘breach’; and 30:12–15; on the use of medical metaphors in Jeremiah, see also Introduction, p. 42). The tents (v. 20) refer to the people’s houses (see also 10:20 for tent in the context of a lament). The word may indicate the vulnerability of shelters in this time of crisis and war. The use of the plural may indicate that the prophet is speaking on behalf of the people. Having to announce destruction to his people caused him much pain.

22. God responds in verse 22 by giving the reason for this disaster (cf. v. 18): the people do not know him, which means that they do not live according to his will as revealed in his commandments (see 2:8; see also 9:3, 6 where TNIV translates acknowledge). It is stated ironically that there is ‘wisdom’ amongst the people, but it is ‘wisdom’ in how to do wrong. They have understanding, but of the wrong things: They are skilled [lit. wise] in doing evil. They do not know me can be read as a parallel to they know not how to do good – they have deliberately ignored God and his will as expressed in the commandments.

23–26. These verses read like a poem. It is said four times that the speaker ‘sees’: I looked. What he sees is a return to the chaos that was present before the act of creation. The earth is formless and empty again (see Gen. 1:2 and Lalleman 2009). These words sound rather menacing in Hebrew; they are also used in Isaiah 34:11, although they do not appear together as in Genesis and Jeremiah. The prophet speaks as someone looking from the earth and then up to the heavens. The light has gone, and there is darkness. The mountains quake, and all the people and birds have gone away; the fruitful land has returned to a wasteland (cf. v. 7). Whereas in Genesis God saw all that he had made, and it was very good, the prophet seems to say, ‘I saw and look! It is very bad!’ Sin affects people as well as creation as a whole (see also Hos. 4:1–3). These verses are not about an environmental disaster as such; they are a description of what happens when God shows his fierce anger (see also v. 8). God is still in control of this ‘return’ or reversal of creation, just as he was the one who turned chaos into order.

27–28. But there is still a glimmer of hope, expressed in verse 27 (see also 5:10, 18). The prophet and the people mourn (vv. 8, 19–21), and so do the earth and the whole of nature (cf. Hos. 4:3). The sun and moon in the heavens are darkened, and the stars do not shine. These verses, together with verses 23–26, have an eschatological/apocalyptic feel about them (cf. Joel 2:10, 31). Decision day is coming! God will not relent or ‘turn back’ from his decision to destroy the land (see v. 8; 18:8 and the comments there; cf. the story of Jonah in which God does relent, Jon. 3:9–10).

29–31. Again a sound is heard, as in verses 15 (a voice is the same word in Hebrew as sound in v. 29), 19, 21. Once again, it is the sound of war. The enemy is penetrating everywhere (see vv. 13, 17). Jerusalem or Daughter Zion (v. 31) is addressed as devastated one (v. 30). Yet she continues to adorn herself with make-up and costly clothes of scarlet, because she is still trying to find lovers and seduce other nations into making a treaty, and thereby save herself from trouble (see 2:36). But these will turn out to be her enemies (13:21). In contrast, the ‘beautiful woman’ will end up in pain and distress. The metaphor of a woman in labour indicates distress and anxiety (see 6:24; 13:21; 22:23; 30:6; 50:43; and Isa.13:8; 21:3 for other instances of this metaphor).

Meaning

In Jeremiah 4:5–31 a powerful message of doom is conveyed by means of lively images, full of action. There is no sense of triumph over Israel’s deserved punishment, only distress and anxiety. The prophet identifies with the painful message he is called to bring to his people, God’s dear ‘Daughter of my people’ (v. 11). Behind the ‘enemy from the north’ who is bringing disaster, God is still in charge. Verses 23–26 are especially powerful, and contrast with Genesis 1. ‘Sin causes chaos and changes God’s good creation into its opposite.’2 Judgment is depicted as a reversal of God’s work of creation.
