C. A VISION OF RELIGIOUS FAILURE (8:1–14)
1. Vision: A Basket of Disaster (8:1–3)
1Thus the Lorda Yahweh showed me: Behold, a basket of summer fruit.

2And he said, “What do you see, Amos?” I said, “A basket of summer fruit.”b And Yahweh said to me, “The end c has come to my people Israel. I will not pass by him again.”

3The songsd of the templee will wail on that day—a saying of the Lord Yahweh.f Many bodies!g Cast outh everywhere! Hush!i

This fourth vision is paired with the third (7:7–9) and is comparable structurally and lexically in several ways.179 The first line follows the pattern of God showing Amos something introduced by wəhinnēh (behold, 8:1; cf. 7:1, 4, 7).180 Yahweh then questions the prophet about what he sees, and Amos responds with a brief answer that identifies the object (8:2a; cf. 7:8a). Subsequently, Yahweh provides an explanation of the significance of the object (8:2b; cf. 7:8b). Both 8:2 and 7:8 close with the ominous statement, I will no longer pass him by. To this is added a statement that relates that explanation to concrete implications for the coming judgment (8:3; cf. 7:9). This further revelation functions, too, as the transition to a further elaboration of that message. In the case of 7:7–9, the expansion was the exchange with Amaziah (7:10–17); here, it is a set of oracles (8:4–14).

There also are a few differences between the third and fourth visions. To begin with, the God who reveals is the Lord Yahweh in 8:1 (cf. 7:1, 4), whereas 7:7 reads simply thus he showed me. This combination from v. 1 reappears in v. 3. What is shown is expressed in just two words (a basket of summer fruit), not in a full clause (cf. 7:1b). The longer subject and the briefer description could suggest greater seriousness. What is more, in contrast to 7:7–8, this vision depends more directly on a play on words (see the exposition below). Another slight change is that God speaks in the first person in 7:9 but not in 8:3.

The additional statement of 8:3 is more emphatic than its counterpart (7:9). First, it contains the two phrases on that day and a saying of the Lord Yahweh. The former appears earlier at 2:16, occurs in the oracles of the expansion (8:9, 13), and initiates the book’s closing hope passage (9:11). The warnings of an imminent “day” of divine judgment are ubiquitous, especially for Israel (3:14; 4:2; 5:18, 20; 6:3; 8:9, 10) but also for other nations (1:14). A saying of the Lord Yahweh connects back to that phrase’s occurrences in judgment oracles (3:13; 4:5)181 and points forward to the other oracles (8:9, 11). These two phrases link the vision to other passages of doom in the book and Amos’s theology of the “day” in ways that 7:7–9 did not. At the same time, a saying of the Lord Yahweh injects a further note of seriousness and finality. The second literary difference, which gives weight to 8:3, is that the last line can be interpreted as a series of exclamations of horror. This provides an emotional quotient to the prediction of disaster. In other words, 8:1–3 should be read together with 7:7–9, but not as a mere reflection. The passage confirms and strengthens the predictions of the defeat of Israel, the death of its leaders, and the exile of the people (7:7–17) with its announcement of the nation’s end.

Other elements of 8:3 reappear in 8:4–14 beyond the two mentioned in the previous paragraph (in that day and a saying of the Lord Yahweh). The most obvious is the lamenting in vv. 8, 10. In addition to the phrase a saying of the Lord Yahweh, the theme of divine speech also surfaces in God’s swearing in v. 7. His oath stands in sharp contrast to the malicious words of the unethical traders in vv. 5–6, and especially to the oaths of v. 14.

The critical questions pertaining to the fourth vision are concentrated on v. 3, with discussions centered primarily on whether this verse in toto or certain elements within it are secondary. Some argue that the entire verse is a later supplement designed as a transition to 8:4–14, like 7:9 to 7:10–17.182 On this basis, a few align it with 8:4–14.183 Some scholars of an older generation suggested that v. 3 was originally located somewhere else and rearranged the oracles of chapters 7 and 8 to fit into what they believed was a more logical sequence of ideas or scenes.184 There is disagreement, however, as to when v. 3 would have been inserted. Suggestions include soon after the time of the prophet by his disciples (the school of Amos)185 or a Deuteronomistic redactor186 or in postexilic Yehud.187 Other critical scholars accept the authenticity of the vision as coming in some form from the prophet, but delete on that day,188 a saying of the Lord Yahweh,189 or Hush.190

The text’s literariness, which interconnects vocabulary and themes from preceding and following passages either to build on or anticipate them, does not require postulating successive redactions. The number and variety of interlocking elements testify to a measure of coherence that can make speculation of many hands at work over different centuries doubtful. In addition to the book-wide links, there is also the literariness evident within the passage. For instance, the Lord Yahweh in vv. 1, 3 serves as an inclusio that binds the dialogue to the tragic scene in the temple. Syntactically, v. 3 follows on v. 2 with a waw-consecutive perfect, which shifts the timeframe from Amos’s present into the future. What is more, the wordplay in v. 3 (summer fruit and the end) fits well into the eighth-century context because of the agricultural seasons listed on the Gezer Calendar, which predate the prophet. In sum, there are good literary and historical-cultural reasons for assuming that the fourth vision is suited to the prophet’s context. Alternative hypotheses are always possible, of course, but not necessary.

1 The fourth vision begins very similarly to the first three, with the Lord Yahweh191 showing something to Amos; here it is a basket of summer fruit. The term for basket (kəlûb) appears elsewhere only at Jer 5:27 and here probably refers to a woven wicker basket. The summer fruit (qayiṣ; 2 Sam 16:1–2; Isa 16:9; Jer 40:10, 12; 48:32; Mic 7:1) most likely includes figs, grapes, and pomegranates.192 This vision could be quite ironic in light of Amos’s work with sycomore fig trees, but this is a reference to different kinds of fruit.193 The summer fruit was harvested in August and September. The Gezer Calendar (tenth century BC) calls the eighth and final month of the agricultural season “the month of summer fruit” (yrḥ qṣ).194 This phase of the agricultural cycle contrasts with the late spring planting of the first vision (7:1), when the destruction by the locusts threatened starvation. This harvest, after the hot summer months with the autumn rains in the offing, was a time of celebration of divine provision and of hope for good things in the coming months.195 Might the prophet think that the basket contains an offering to God in thanksgiving at a festival, most likely the Feast of Ingathering (also called Tabernacles; Exod 23:16; 34:22; Deut 16:13–17)?196

2 The prophet correctly identifies the object. Yahweh then puns summer fruit (qayiṣ) with qēṣ (end).197 These words come from different roots, qayiṣ from qyṣ (“to spend the summer”) and qēṣ from qṣṣ (“to cut off”). The dialectical pronunciation in Israel, however, would have yielded a similar sound for qayiṣ and qēṣ.198 It is difficult to reproduce puns in another language, but several English versions try to communicate this one; for instance, “I answered, ‘A basket of ripe summer fruit.’ Then the LORD said to me, ‘The time is ripe for my people Israel…’” (NEB; cf. NJB, NIV).199

The wordplay startles with this surprising assonance. Whereas the image of the basket could imply a new start with ample provision, qēṣ discloses that the end has come. Is it that the harvested fruit is overly ripe and will soon perish—analogously, that the time of reckoning has arrived? Perhaps the message is that Israel itself is the harvested fruit, plucked in divine wrath. Judgment is related to the time of harvest elsewhere, but here it may be for the first time in the prophetic literature (2:13; cf. Isa 16:9–10; 17:5–6; Jer 51:33; Joel 3:13 [MT 4:13]). Motyer’s interpretation of the line mimics the wordplay:

They came into the presence of God not just with ripe fruit but as ripe fruit, ripened over all the months and years of moral and spiritual probation which he had afforded them (cf. 4:6–11) and now, sadly, ready for a particularly dreadful harvest-time.200

Yahweh announces, “The end has come” (cf. Gen 6:13; Lam 4:18; Ezek 7:2–6). The intercession is over (7:1–6), and Israel’s leaders have proven themselves incapable and unwilling to accept God’s prophet (7:10–17). That all the nation is implicated, not just the royal court and high priest, is clear in the declaration that the judgment is for my people Israel. This phrase echoes 7:8, where ănāk is placed in the midst of my people Israel. Even those in power, who in the past may have been able to put off that “day” (6:3) and enjoy plenty (6:4–6) as the rest of the nation suffered want, cannot escape the devastation. The destruction will impact everyone. As Rudolph expresses so well: Death “ist der grausige Nivellierer” (“is the great equalizer”).201 The other words repeated from 7:8 are I will not pass by him again—that is, there is no chance that the nation might be spared. How different from the repeated it will not be in response to the intercession of the first two visions (7:3, 6)! As in the third vision, I will not … again communicates finality; in this verse, that reality is underscored by the end has come.

For some scholars, the latter statement is the defining moment of Amos’s ministry, and they reconstruct the chronology of the prophet’s life and ministry accordingly. Whereas early on he had held out hope for Israel, it is argued, the experience of this vision dispelled any illusion of pardon. From this moment, Amos became a prophet of absolute doom.202 George Adam Smith’s eloquent comments represent this point of view:

These then were perhaps the last words which Amos spoke to Israel. If so, they form a curious echo of what was enforced upon himself, and he may have meant them as such. He was cast out; he was silenced. They might also be the verbal repetition of the priest’s orders. In any case the silence is appropriate. But Amaziah little knew what power he had given prophecy the day he forbade it to speak. The gagged prophet began to write; and those accents which, humanly speaking, might have died out with the songs of the temple of Bethel were clothed with the immortality of literature. Amos silenced wrote a book—first of the prophets to do so—and this is the book we have now to study.203

For others, most famously Smend,204 these words are Amos’s core message. Its decisiveness, they claim, calls into question (or at least emphasizes the tension with) the legitimacy of the offers to seek Yahweh and live (5:4–6, 14–15) and any possibility of restoration (9:11–15). However, as explained in the introduction under “Religion and Theology” (pp. 90–94), the inevitability of judgment does not negate the genuineness of challenging the people to respond to the prophet, follow the kind of life demanded by God, and persevere until the restoration. That call stands, irrespective of the nation’s fate.

3 The interpretation of this verse is a challenge for several reasons.205 The first difficulty is lexical. Does it make sense that the songs (šîrôt) of the temple will wail? A simple emendation revocalizing šîrôt to “songstresses” (šārôt; cf. 2 Sam 19:35 [MT 19:36]; 2 Chr 35:25; Eccl 2:8) is a long-standing option,206 dating back to before Wellhausen.207 The line then translates, “the songstresses … will wail.” Nevertheless, while recognizing that šîrôt is an anomalous plural of šîrâ, I tentatively opt for the MT,208 understanding songs as a metonymy for those singing. Songs appear twice in the book (5:23; 8:10)—a fact that buttresses this option.

A second difficulty is determining the location of the lamenting. The word hêkāl can mean either “temple” (2 Kgs 18:16; Isa 6:1; Jer 7:4; Ezek 8:16; Hag 2:15, 18) or “palace” (1 Kgs 21:1; 2 Kgs 20:18; Isa 13:22; Dan 1:4). The choice for “songstresses,” who would perform in the royal court, would suggest the latter.209 This redirects the focus to Samaria and could allude more directly to the monarchy, whose demise was announced in 7:9 (cf. 7:11). Those who sing to entertain at the court will be driven to wail. This could also recall the merriment in 6:5.

On the other hand, the confrontation between the prophet and the high priest in 7:10–17 takes place in the temple precincts, 8:4–6 hits at the hypocrisy of some attending religious celebrations, 8:10 reflects the impact of judgment on religious rituals, and the fifth vision foretells the disruption at the sanctuary at Bethel and the death of worshippers (9:1). If, too, the basket of summer fruit is connected to the Festival of Ingathering (v. 1), then a cultic setting is appropriate to the oracle as a whole. The temple at Bethel, then, is my preferred option.210 Accordingly, “in every place” (everywhere) in the second half of the verse could refer to the high places of 7:9 (if not to the various parts of the temple). So, while both options potentially are viable,211 the immediate context suggests that the vision continues the delegitimating of the national sanctuary and its ideology by predicting a scene in the temple after a terrible judgment.212 Songs, which should rise in praise to God, will become howls of shock and grief in the very place where that national ideology is celebrated. This corporate mourning reminds the reader of 5:16–17. The ubiquitous lamenting in all the streets described there will have its complement in the cries within the most sacred site in Israel.

This event will happen on that day—a major theological emphasis in the book. This phrase reappears in 8:4–14, thereby associating this wailing with the laments in the judgment (v. 9) and the fainting of the young (v. 13).213 As it does throughout the book (see above), the phrase a saying of the Lord Yahweh confirms this as a sure word of judgment (also at vv. 9, 11).

What causes this wailing (cf. v. 8)? It is the overwhelming number of dead bodies. The multitude of corpses is so painful and repugnant because they are left publicly exposed, rotting (cf. Isa 34:3), and as potential food for wild animals (cf. 1 Sam 17:46; 2 Sam 21:10; 1 Kgs 14:11; 2 Kgs 9:36; Ps 79:2; Jer 8:2; 9:22 [MT 9:21]; 16:4). This devastating grief (cf. Jer 4:8; 25:34; Lam 2:10; Mic 1:8; Zeph 1:10–11) is exacerbated by the ignominy of not being able to properly bury the dead.214 These bodies have been thrown out into the open (cf. 4:3),215 although by whom the text does not say.216 Perhaps they lay where they fell (cf. Nah 3:3). This scene may be linked to the judgment on the Bethel sanctuary in 9:1. Otherwise, contextually, the deaths would be due to the armed conflict mentioned in 7:7–17.217

The prophet declared that Amaziah and his children would die (7:17), the public mourning was for the casualties in the coming war (5:1–3, 16–17), and the search parties of 6:9–10 retrieved the corpses of the dead (whether from war, an earthquake, or a plague). In none of those scenarios, however, is there a hint of bodies not being buried. Even in 6:9–10, the purpose is to dispose of the corpses to avoid the shame reflected in this verse and the danger of pestilence. Thus, this vision presents a stunning—even surreal and brutal—juxtaposition of a basket of fruit and the dead strewn everywhere.218 Auld captures the paradox: “The very basket suggests the gathering of the fruit harvest: harvest with its associations of judgment and of death the reaper.”219 The shock of what has happened yields three brief exclamations:220 Many bodies! Cast out everywhere! Hush! Not all agree with this interpretation,221 but it captures well the sense and tone of the passage.

Hush can be interpreted as a cry of desperation and grief.222 It can also be understood as an imperative to be quiet, a stunned reproach to Israel’s cherished beliefs.223 If so, this would recall 6:10. It is not motivated by a reverent fear or superstitious dread of the deity’s return to cause more damage.224 Rather, this is frustration and anger at their God! Yahweh was supposed to be the nation’s champion and protector, the one whom they had worshipped at Bethel and who sanctioned the national ideology. All of that now is contradicted by the corpses. Why call on the One who has betrayed all that the nation believed to be true and brought it to such horrific ruin? From singing to howling to silence. The end had come.

2. Expansion: The Cost of Religious Perversion (8:4–14)
As 7:10–17 did for the third oracle (7:7–9), 8:4–14 develops the fourth vision (8:1–3). It expands the themes of definitive judgment: mourning and the demise of sacred space and ritual. This section also evokes material from earlier passages even as it serves as a transition to the fifth vision (9:1–4).225 It can be divided into four parts: vv. 4–8,226 vv. 9–10, vv. 11–12, and vv. 13–14. The extent of the first pericope is marked by an inclusio: hear this (v. 4) and because of this (v. 8). The other passages begin with the formulas on that day (vv. 9, 13) and behold, days are coming (v. 11). In vv. 9 and 11, the opening formula is followed by a saying of the Lord Yahweh.

There are multiple connections between 8:4–14 and the earlier chapters of Amos that surface as one moves through the section. The opening hear this in v. 4 recalls 3:1, 9, 13; 4:1, 5:1; 7:16. The first clause in v. 4 echoes 2:7 and its censure of the mistreatment of the poor. Religious hypocrisy that separates worship from social justice in v. 5 confirms the critique of Israel’s cult in 5:4–6 (and its chiastically paired passage, 5:14–15) and the demand at 5:24 for justice. God’s rejection of irresponsible feasts and songs, which is vividly described in v. 10, reinforces the sentiment of 5:21–23.

The injustice of debt slavery that is condemned in 2:6 reappears in v. 6. There is a third divine oath in v. 7 (cf. 4:2; 6:8), and the statement that Yahweh will not forget any of Israel’s transgressions is a similar reaction to that expressed in 5:12 (Surely I know your transgressions are many, and your sins numerous) and communicates in yet another way the impossibility of escaping judgment (see 2:14–16; 5:19; 6:11, 14; cf. 9:2–4). A prediction of an earthquake appears in v. 8 (1:1; 2:13; 3:14–15; 4:11, 13; 6:9–11; cf. 9:1, 5, 9); its description is linked to the third hymnic fragment of 9:5–6. It is the sovereign God who brings disaster on the nation. This theophanic intervention brings ubiquitous mourning, as at 5:1–2, 16–17; 8:3.

The formulas in vv. 9, 11, and 13 that speak of an imminent day (or days) return to the prominent theme of the day of Yahweh,227 and in v. 9 it is coupled with darkness like at 5:18, 20. The change of light and darkness echoes the second hymnic passage (5:8). The famine of the word of Yahweh and its inaccessibility (vv. 11–12) are expected outcomes of its rejection (2:11–12; 7:10–13). The thirst and hunger in vv. 11, 13 are reminiscent of 4:6–8. The fainting of the virgins and young men in v. 13 echoes the virgin Israel who has fallen (5:2) and the young men who died in battle (4:10). The mention in v. 14 of the way to Beersheba brings to mind the command not to journey to Beersheba in 5:5, and the Beersheba-Dan combination fits within the judgment of the destruction of Israel’s high places and sanctuaries in 7:9. The closing line of the section, that those who have gone astray will fall and not rise again, repeats the fate of Israel in 5:2.

These correlations are instances of “threading,” where themes reappear with different formats and purpose in the course of a work and help tie the book together as an integrated unit. As the text repeatedly returns to motifs in various ways, the reader is led into deeper reflections on their import.228 In addition, 8:4–14 exhibits several of the book’s characteristic stylistic features. The most obvious is the quote in vv. 5–6 of those whom the text condemns. This self-incrimination is repeated throughout Amos (2:12; 4:1; 5:14; 6:13; 8:14; 9:10). Another is the rhetorical question in v. 8 (2:11; 3:3–8; 5:18, 20, 25; 6:2, 12; cf. 9:7). A stylistic element missed by most commentators is the heptad. There are seven unacceptable practices in vv. 4–6, which suggests the complete violation of the poor.229

Taken together, the thematic, lexical, and stylistic data offer good reasons to associate 8:4–14 with the prophet. It must be remembered that the book that bears his name is not simply a collection of oracles delivered orally but an impressive literary work originating with Amos that would be rooted in public messages and more. The intricately interwoven literariness in this section generates coherence and continuity within the larger work. Several critical scholars do recognize the many interconnections between 8:4–14 and the rest of the book. For example, commenting on 8:4–14, Wolff says, “Those who formulated these oracles apparently (as in 7:14–17) still had Amos’ own words ringing in their ears.”230 Yet interestingly, for Hadjiev, “the sheer density of allusions” is an argument against authenticity. A few items in the section, he believes, suggest a later composition, so that “in spite of the extensive links with the previous material, vv 4–14 breathe an atmosphere which is different from the rest of the Amos oracles preserved in the book.”231 Jeremias believes that “the citations of earlier words of Amos are artificially organized.”232 In other words, working from different presuppositions, these critics give the source of the textual phenomena another explanation. From their standpoint, the prophet could not have produced this impressive textual mutuality and rich advancement of earlier passages. Instead, creative redactors spread over time are said to stand behind this theological and lexical tapestry.

There is a spectrum of critical views on the provenance of the various oracles in 8:4–14.233 Passages are dated to various time periods, ranging from the time of the prophet to the “school of Amos” or generally before the fall of Jerusalem234 to Deuteronomistic redactors235 and to the exilic or postexilic periods.236 The hypotheses are many and complex, so a sample list must suffice. Additional literary and historical engagement is provided in the exposition.

First of all, because there is a definite change in topic in v. 6 from v. 5 and because it is similar to 2:6, some scholars reject as not original the entire verse, the echo of 2:6, or just the final clause (and we sell the chaff of wheat).237 Others transpose that last clause to the end of v. 5 as a logical continuation of so that we might offer grain for sale238 or place it after the statement in v. 5 about changing the measures and weights.239 In v. 8, many follow LXX and delete and be tossed about.240 The correspondence between that verse and 9:5 has generated debate about the relationship between the two verses and the originality of v. 8.241

The subsection 8:11–12 has also drawn a lot of critical attention.242 To begin with, the opening combination of formulas (Behold days are coming and a saying of the Lord Yahweh) is regularly taken as a gloss,243 in part because it is found most often in the book of Jeremiah.244 Beyond this first line, some scholars find the mixing of the physical and figurative (or spiritual) problematic. Thus, they contend that the entire pericope or parts of it (e.g., the mention of thirst in the context of famine or Yahweh’s words) are the work of later redactors.245 The principal critical change in 8:13–14 is to move the last line of v. 14 (they will fall and not rise again) to the beginning of the verse to continue the theme of fainting from thirst in v. 13.246

LXX expands the first clause, probably due to difficulty with the verb š’p: “you who rub out the poor in the morning and oppress the poor.” For Gelston, this is a misreading of the text (“Some Hebrew Misreadings in the Septuagint of Amos,” 494–95). Glenny counters that this is a case of double translation (“Hebrew Misreadings or Free Translation in the Septuagint of Amos?,” 532–33; Finding Meaning in the Text, 50; cf. BHQ, 86*). For an explanation of the translation, see the exposition at 2:7; cf. Barthélemy, Critique Textuelle de l’Ancien Testament, 681–84.

b. Tg. adds “like the dust of the earth,” probably to harmonize with 2:7.

c. MT has the infinitive construct plus the preposition (lašbît), whereas LXX has the participle (katadynasteuontes, “oppress”; cf. 4:1) in coordination with the preceding clause. The Hebrew construction, which usually signals purpose (NASB), can also continue the thrust of the participle because it is prefixed with the conjunctive (GKC §114p, IBHS §36.3.2). This syntactical option is reflected in almost all English versions. BHS suggests deleting the waw and replacing the verb with ləhašbît (full form of the infinitive instead of MT’s contracted form) or lašḥît (“destroy,” contracted form).

d. Ketiv has ʿăniwwê, “the humble, afflicted” (from ʿānāw). Qere is ʿăniyyê, “the poor” (from ʿānî). Tg. reads “the words of the poor.” Qere harmonizes with the previous clause. A change is not necessary, as the terms’ meanings overlap.

e. Tg. has “sabbatical year.”

f. LXX has anoixomen thēsaurous (“we will open storehouses”); cf. Syr.

g. Tg. reads “in order to acquire an inheritance.”

h. LXX has apo pantos (“in every”), probably reading mikkōl instead of mappal (“chaff”).

i. Tg. changes this to “the Lord who gave greatness to Jacob swears.”

j. LXX misreads “forever” (lāneṣaḥ) and renders “unto victory” (eis nikos).

k. For “tremble,” Tg. has “laid waste.”

l. Instead of MT “like the light” (kāʾōr), I read this in parallel with the next clause, “like the Nile” (kayʾōr); cf. Barthélemy, Critique Textuelle de l’Ancien Testament, 684–86. LXX, Syr., Tg., and Vulg. have “river”; cf. BHS; BHQ, 87*. Perhaps the copyist’s eye caught ʾōr in v. 9. LXX has kai anabēsetai hōs potamos synteleia (“and consummation rise up like a river”), probably reading MT kullâ (“all of it”) as kālâ (“consummation”) with an eschatological interest. See Dines, “The Septuagint of Amos,” 250; Glenny, Finding Meaning in the Text, 210–11; Amos, 141.

m. In contrast to MT’s two verbs, LXX has only one (katabēsetai, “sink”); cf. BHS. For “mourn, and all of it rise like the Nile and be tossed about,” Tg. reads, “be desolated. A king shall come up against it with his army which is great like the waters of a river, and he shall cover it all and drive out its inhabitants.” Smolar and Aberbach suggest that Tg. relates this verse and 9:5 to the invasion by Vespasian, 67–69 CE (Studies in Targum Jonathan, 74–75).

n. Ketiv has wənišqəhā (“and is given to drink”). With qere, I read wənišqəʿâ (“sink”), niphal of šqʿ (yet see Andersen and Freedman, Amos, 812–13). In 9:5, šqʿ is in the qal.

These verses present the last indictment of socioeconomic injustice in the book.247 Verses 4–6 reiterate the book’s opening salvo against oppression by repeating the vocabulary of the Israel oracle of the OAN (2:6–7). The subtle changes to that wording and the expanded censure provide more insight into the machinations in the marketplace that led to so much misery. In terms of the book’s overall structure, then, the reiteration of 2:6–7 serves as an important closure to the prophetic denunciation of the exploitation of the poor. The divine oath and the vow never to forget these acts of cruelty add a sober note of finality to Yahweh’s decision for judgment (v. 7), the impact of which will literally shake the land of Israel (v. 8).

Several literary elements help configure the passage.248 As mentioned above, an inclusio with this indicates the extent of the passage at vv. 4–8. Another inclusio, the repetition of the poor (ʾebyôn) in vv. 4, 6, marks out a subsection that focuses on injustice against the poor. Within this subsection of vv. 4–6, the long quote in vv. 5–6 can be presented as follows:

5a“When will the new moon be over so that we can sell grain, and the sabbath so that we might offer grain for sale

5bin order to make the ephah small and the shekel great, to bend fraudulent scales,

6ato buy the weak for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals,

6band we sell the chaff of wheat?”

Syntactically, vv. 5a and 6b are an envelope around vv. 5b and 6a, which contain a series of three infinitives with the lamed preposition, a common way of expressing purpose in Hebrew. This verbal construction is revealing. These infinitives, the last of which is to buy, reveal the purpose of the oppressors’ dishonesty: They are anxious to get to the marketplace to take advantage of the poor. This structure demonstrates that the words in v. 6a, which are drawn from 2:6–7, need not be secondary. That is, the debt slavery (buying and selling the poor) is connected to corrupt practices in the market. The manipulation of commerce in grain had as its goal the very thing that some scholars want to excise from the passage, which is to drive the destitute into debt slavery for personal gain! The repetition of intent in an inclusio confirms the envelope structure. That we might offer grain for sale in v. 5a (našbîrâ šeber) is echoed by we sell the chaff of wheat (mappal bar našbîr) in the last clause of v. 6. The sale of chaff (first-person plural) follows the series of infinitives without detracting from their import the emphasis on buying the vulnerable. Critical emendations that move the last clause of v. 6 to somewhere in v. 5 miss this literary artistry.249 Measures and balances are manipulated, the poor commodified, and the product diluted. This information supplements 2:6–7.

4 The section begins with the call, Hear this (cf. Hos 5:1; Joel 1:2; Mic 3:9). This reminds the reader of 3:1, 13; 4:1; 5:1; and 7:16, although this call is briefer.250 As in 4:1, the message is directed at a particular group of people and not at the nation. It identifies them with the same verb as 2:7 (šʾp, “trample on”)251 and then explains the methods of their oppression in vv. 5–6. Their identity will become clear in the next verse. The echo of 2:7, however, is not verbatim. Those who are crushed are not the weak (dallim), as in 2:7, but the needy (ʾebyôn). Also, while in 2:7 the weak are trampled into the dust of the ground, here the afflicted (ʿānāw) of the land suffer (cf. Job 24:4; Ps 76:9 [MT 76:10]; Isa 11:4; Zeph 2:3). There is debate about the distinct nuances of these three terms because of their semantic overlap; all three appear in vv. 4–5 and in 2:6–7. They can seem almost interchangeable, and that could be the case here.

The actions of these perpetrators of injustice bring an end to their victims.252 In what sense are the poor eliminated? On the one hand, the very ones that the unjust need to increase their wealth eventually disappear, ironically destroying the source of their perverse income. This is the nonsensical contradiction of an exploitative economy. Second, and more crucially, from the perspective of the oppressed, this situation means that the lives of the poor are essentially over. The next two verses explain how. The fourth vision announced the end (haqqēṣ) of Israel in divine judgment. This verse, with different terminology (šbt hiphil, “cause to cease”), decries the destruction of the economically vulnerable within Israel. If the poor of the land refers to peasants in rural areas or to those who have migrated to the cities and who endure dire subsistence conditions (see below at 8:5, 6), then they are alienated from and have lost their ancestral lands or the small plots they farmed to survive. This violation of the land as it was meant to be contributes to the shaking of that land in v. 8.253

5 As in multiple other instances (2:12; 4:1; 6:13; 7:10–13; 8:14; 9:10), the words of the condemned are cited. Their guilt is confirmed by what they say. Again, this would not be a direct quote. Rather, it is meant to capture characteristic attitudes and is an imaginative way of depicting their actions. What is different from previous passages is that their intention is revealed, not just the injustice (cf. Mic 2:1–2). This quote extends through to the end of v. 6.254

Their words begin in a surprising way with the mention of two historic feasts in Israel.255 The new moon was celebrated at the start of every month and was evidently observed from early times (Num 10:10; 28:11–15; 1 Sam 20:5, 18, 24; 2 Kgs 4:23; Hos 5:7). Rest from work was mandated in the sabbath regulations (Exod 20:8–11; 23:12; 34:21; Lev 23:3; Deut 5:12–15), and abstinence from commercial activity would have been consistent with that (cf. Neh 13:15–22; Jer 17:21–27). The sabbath rest was to apply to servants and outsiders. That is, charitable justice was to be inherent in its practice (cf. Isa 56:1–8). These same two feasts are also paired in judgments on religion by other eighth-century prophets (Isa 1:13–14; Hos 2:11 [MT 2:13]).

The critique of the cult within the book of Amos condemns various religious beliefs and activities. The prophet mocks self-indulgent religious practices (4:5) and condemns the nation’s deaf ear to the word of God (2:11– 12; 7:14–17). He also deconstructs the self-deceiving and destructive national ideology that is legitimated in the sanctuaries (5:18–20; 7:10–17). In 5:4–6, 10–15, 21–24, the target is the bifurcation of ritual and social concern. This last reproach is what is condemned in 8:4–6, but this passage surfaces a new dimension. The quote exposes the interior thought process of this group for whom religious ritual is an obstacle. The question they ask (“When will the new moon be over … and the sabbath…?”) reveals a malicious impatience obsessed with opportunities to turn a profit.256

Who are the guilty in 8:4–6? Their social role and status are not specified, nor is their relationship to those who are condemned in other passages. In 3:9–11 and 5:11–12, the perpetrators are probably somehow connected to the administration in Samaria (officials of the crown, the king and royal family, or tax collectors); 4:1–3 and 6:1–7 direct attention to the well-to-do (a merchant class, wealthy landowners, or individuals connected in some way to the royal court). Other passages might have in view elders at the gate (5:10, 12). Most scholars assume that in 8:4–6, the words and actions are those of traders in grain, although some point to powerful landowners.257 Others propose that the scene is more complex than simplistic rich-poor or rural-urban dichotomies (also see at 2:6–8). Perhaps these verses portray local merchants, better-placed farmers (more fortunate in terms of health and crops), or leaders within a clan or town who take advantage of more vulnerable kinsmen, neighbors, or fellow citizens.258 Another question is whether religious authorities benefited from these unjust arrangements (4:4–5; 7:17) and therefore kept quiet or somehow justified them. One could also ask whether this exploitation was generalized in markets in rural areas, villages, and towns throughout the land or was restricted to the cities, more specifically Samaria. Are the victims the rural poor, the urban poor, or both? There are no easy answers to these queries. Each reconstruction of the socioeconomic conditions makes sense to some degree.259 Whatever the actual settings and whoever the wrongdoers, the indictment in this verse is patent.

The product is grain. The Old Testament has several words for grain, and two appear in this verse. The first is šeber, the second bar. They are used interchangeably in the Joseph narrative (Gen 42:1–3, 19, 25–26) and here as well.260 Three means are employed to defraud others in commercial transactions. The first dishonesty involves tampering with the standard measure for dry goods (the ephah) by making it smaller, resulting in the buyer getting less than what was paid for. The second is to make the customary weight for payment (the shekel) heavier, so that the buyer pays extra.261 Third, these individuals employ fraudulent scales, literally “scales of deceit” (mōʾznê mirmâ), which means that the balance arm is bent to skew weighing. These corrupt practices violate the expectation of fair measures, weights, and balances in ancient Israel (Lev 19:35–36; Deut 25:13–15; Prov 11:1; 20:10, 23). The eighth-century prophets Hosea and Micah also censure this kind of dishonesty (Hos 12:7 [MT 12:8]; Mic 6:9–11).

6 At first glance, the disapproval of this verse sounds like the disapproval of 2:6.262 There are significant differences, however. To begin with, in the Israel oracle, the transgression was selling (mkr) the innocent into debt slavery (cf. Exod 21:1–6; Deut 15:12–18), whereas in this verse the verb is buy (qnh). Second, as mentioned above, the verbal construction (infinitive plus the preposition lamed) indicates purpose. In other words, buying the weak is the goal of the dishonest dealings of v. 5.

The buying of the poor in debt slavery is the other side of the transaction of 2:6.263 If someone is forced into debt slavery, for whatever reason, there must be someone into whose hands they go. There are two ways of understanding the preposition bet in the clause for silver … for a pair of sandals (bakkesep … baʿăbûr naʿălayim). The first is causal. That is, the poor are bought because of a money debt (silver), even for a minimal obligation (a pair of sandals) that these unfortunate individuals cannot repay.264 The preposition can also be categorized as pretii, meaning that the poor are bought in exchange for (the price of ) silver, even for as low an amount as a pair of sandals.265 Grammatically, either option is viable. Perhaps the latter fits the context better, as it underscores the unscrupulousness of those who bring ruination to the poor (v. 4).266

The association of these actions with the sequence of infinitives in v. 5 has other implications. One is that the poverty of these unfortunates is connected, at least in part, to the sale of grain, a food staple that is being manipulated. They are both indebted and hungry. What also is not said explicitly is whether the acquisition of debt slaves is made possible by the fraudulent gains of v. 5. In other words, whether the money earned underhandedly funds this injustice! In addition, if acquiring these debt slaves in some cases also entailed taking control or possession of the land of the poor, then the ironic cruelty deepens. Would these debt slaves have to work their own plots for their oppressors (cf. Mic 2:1–2; Neh 5:1–5)? The character of these callous persons is expressed nicely by Mays: “They love the Lord less, mammon more, and their fellows not at all.”267

The last clause of this verse forms an inclusio with v. 5 (see above), effectively closing the indictment before the strong divine reaction in the next verse. The deceitfulness is extended to the selling of a mix of grain with chaff that may have been swept up from the threshing floor.268

7 Yahweh’s response is forceful. That it comes via an oath underscores its seriousness. Twice before in the book, Yahweh has sworn an oath, both times in contexts of accusation and judgment. In 4:2 the divine oath follows the self-indulgent words of the wives of the powerful. The oath in 6:8 occurs after the description of the lascivious marzēaḥ feast with its religious undercurrent. One oath precedes an announcement of doom for those whose behavior is unacceptable (4:3), the other, more broadly, an announcement of doom for the nation’s fortifications (6:8). This oath is God’s answer to the injustice in the marketplace of 8:4–6. In the second clause of the verse, Yahweh asserts, “Surely, I will never forget any of their deeds.”269 Emphasis is communicated by never and any. There will be no escaping the wrath of God for their actions and the misery they have caused the defenseless. This is a dramatic change from the first two visions, in which Yahweh relented from what he said he would do (7:3, 6). The end has come for those who have brought an end to the poor (v. 4). This is no “colorless threat”!270

The interpretive challenge in this verse is how to understand the pride of Jacob by which Yahweh swears. The phrase appears in 6:8 not as part of a divine oath but in his statement, I abhor the pride of Jacob, where it is a reference to arrogant self-confidence based on military fortifications. Scholars argue for one of three options. The first is that the proper pride of Jacob should be Israel’s God (cf. “pride of Israel” in Hos 5:5; 7:10). The expression would then mean that Yahweh swears by his person as in 4:2 and 6:8.271 It could also imply that the transgressions against the weak are ultimately against God himself.272 Some scholars suggest that the phrase was originally a divine epithet (cf. the names for Yahweh in 1 Sam 15:29; Mic 5:4 [MT 5:3]), but that is difficult to prove.

A second explanation, based on Ps 47:4 (MT 47:5) and Nah 2:2 (MT 2:3), is that the pride of Jacob signifies either Israel as the chosen people of God or the land of Israel, the inheritance and loving gift of Yahweh.273 If the land of Israel is the correct option, then the phrase ties in ironically with the poor of the land in v. 4 and anticipates the trembling land in v. 8. Yahweh swears by the land that has witnessed abuse and will soon be shaken.

A third possibility is to correlate the phrase with 6:8, its only other occurrence in the book. In this case, Yahweh sarcastically swears by Israel’s confident self-importance.274 In 6:8, Israel’s haughtiness was grounded in military self-sufficiency. This was quickly disabused by the announcement of the destruction of the city that the fortresses were designed to protect (6:9–11), a play on words regarding the insignificance of their military victories (6:13), the prediction of foreign occupation (6:14), and the plea for mercy because Israel is so small (7:2, 5). In 8:4–6, the arrogance is not rooted in an aura of invincibility but in attitudes of unaccountability. This conceit now is turned against the unjust; their greedy insolence prompts the judgment of God. Although it is difficult to decide among the three interpretations,275 the third option may be the best, as it does not require giving a different meaning to the phrase and allows the book to define its own terms.

8 An interrogative particle (ha) is prefixed to the first word (the preposition ʿal, because of), indicating that this verse is a rhetorical question. The expected response to this question would be an uncontestable affirmative, another instance of self-incrimination. The judgment is deserved. What is the this (zōʾt) to which God refers and that triggers his wrath? The term signals an inclusio with the first two words of the section in v. 4 (šimʿû zōʾt, Hear this). Although some believe that it refers to the oath in the preceding verse,276 more likely this refers to the injustices described in vv. 4–6 and their terrible impact on the weak.277 As with the other divine oaths (4:2; 6:8), an announcement of judgment follows the oath in v. 7.

The first thing to notice is that sin negatively impacts creation, a reality stated in other prophetic books (Isa 24:4–6; Jer 12:4; 23:10; Hos 4:3) and a fundamental theological principle since the transgression in the Garden of Eden (Gen 3:17–19).278 The series of natural calamities in 4:6–9 had been sent to drive Israel to repentance. That time has passed; this natural disaster will surpass anything that they have experienced heretofore. The first two visions spoke of incredible disturbances in the natural order as judgments, but Yahweh relented from bringing those to pass (7:1–6). Now, there is no escaping a great catastrophe: The land will tremble.

The verb (rgz) is used for shaking the ground (e.g., Pss 18:7 [MT 18:8]; 77:18 [MT 77:19]; Joel 2:10),279 so it likely refers to an earthquake. The superscription mentions an earthquake (1:1), and several passages in the book may depict its destructive power (2:13; 3:14–15; 4:11, 13; 6:9–11; 9:1, 5, 9). Some scholars, however, see this clause as figurative of something gone awry in the land (cf. Prov 30:21), partly because the second part of the verse seems incongruous with such a disaster (see below).280 It might allude to warfare, since the same verb is used in the context of overwhelming military conflict (1 Sam 14:15; Joel 2:10; cf. the verb rʿš, Jer 8:16; 51:28–30). Nevertheless, the significance of a literal earthquake in the book makes that the best option. Yahweh is announcing an imminent earthquake as part of his punishment (which will also include an invasion).281 The reaction of all of its inhabitants will be pervasive lament for the destruction wrought and the lives lost in the earthquake. The mourning in 5:16–17, and probably in 8:3, was for the dead in battle. This verse expands the mourning to the ones who will perish in this natural disaster.

The second line of the verse symbolically depicts the earthquake. It is as comprehensive as the mourning: All of the land will be affected. There are several textual issues, however. Besides the universally accepted emendation of kāʾōr (“like the light”) to kayʾōr (“like the Nile”),282 two terms in the Hebrew text are a challenge. The first is the verb nigrəšâ, tossed about (niphal of grš; cf. Isa 57:20). It does not appear in the similar line of 9:5, and there is no equivalent in LXX. Many commentators cite these facts and delete it.283 Some believe that it was inserted to clarify the simile of the rise and fall of the Nile and link it to an earthquake. This conjecture is a possibility,284 but literarily it does serve the purpose of interfacing the two lines of the verse with earthquake imagery. The second problem is the verb nišqəhā (niphal of šqh, “to water, drink”). Scholars and modern versions opt for the qere form, vənišqəʿâ, sink (niphal of šqʿ; see the translation notes). The MT is most likely a scribal error.

The language of the second line is puzzling. Cripps calls the association of the earthquake with the rising and falling of the Nile “a perplexing simile,” and Eidevall calls it a “strange image.”285 One would expect any comparison to be about swift, strong action. The Nile rises and falls, but that process takes months. This line, however, is not about the devastating shock of an earth-quake, which is over quickly. The lesson likely concerns the irresistible force of that huge river that periodically impacted a vast territory. Such is the case in Jer 46:7–8, where the Nile is compared to a vast army (cf. the Euphrates for the Assyrian invasion in Isa 8:7–8). The simile portrays the unmatchable power of Yahweh in his judgment, as do the three hymnic passages (4:13; 5:8–9; 9:5–6).286

One final concern is to understand the possible relationship between 8:8 and 9:5. Although the two verses are largely equivalent, there are notable discrepancies. The verses begin differently. In 8:8 the land trembles; in 9:5, however, the Lord Yahweh Ṣəbāʾôt is the subject; he touches the land, causing it to teeter.287 To this direct and personal intervention is added the connection to the heavenlies in 9:6. These dissimilarities are significant. If the verb tossed about actually does belong in 8:8, it grounds that verse in the historical moment of the earthquake. Amos 9:6 is cosmic in an eschatological sense; 8:8 is not.288 The prediction of 8:8 responds to dealings in the market in vv. 4–6, and the fact that an earthquake is mentioned elsewhere also suggests that this is not a cosmic event. This verse speaks of what is coming soon upon the land of Israel and the mourning that will occur then. The local scale of the verse is superseded by the magnitude of what is celebrated in the hymn of 9:5–6, which will be similar to, but grander than, the earthquake of 8:8. The language of 8:8 anticipates 9:5 and establishes a point of comparison with the last hymnic passage.

Critical debates wrestle with whether the composition of 8:8 is chronologically prior to 9:5 (or the other way around), whether there was mutual influence between the two verses, or whether a well-known hymn lies behind both. There is no way, however, to reconstruct with confidence the direction of dependence, and scholars defend diverse views.289 A literary approach can appreciate that, in the flow of the text, 8:8 sets the stage for 9:5–6 and keeps the unfolding drama of the fate of Israel moving forward. What is clear is that this divine theophany will be overwhelming and unexpected. It will contradict popular expectations of the appearance of God (5:14, 18–20).290 In the words of an earlier passage, Prepare to meet your God, O Israel! (4:12).

b. Mourning the Judgment (8:9–10)
9And it will come about on that day—a saying of the Lord Yahweha—I will make the sun go down at noon, and I will darken the land in broad daylight.b

10I will turn your festivals into mourning and all of your songs into lament, and I will put sackcloth on all loins and baldness upon every head, and I will make it like mourning for an only son and the end of it like a bitter day.c

a. Unlike here, in v. 11 LXX has simply kyrios for MT ʾădōnāy yhwh (“Lord Yahweh”). For the translation of the divine names in LXX, see the discussion in the introduction under “The Text,” p. 111.

b. LXX has “sun” and “light” as the subjects with third-person verbs. For a possible explanation, see Glenny, Finding Meaning in the Text, 197–98.

c. For LXX (“And I will make him like the mourning for a loved one and those with him like a day of suffering”) and suggestions for the identity of “him” and “those with him,” see Dines, “The Septuagint of Amos,” 252–53; Glenny, Amos, 142–43.

Although this subsection is introduced by a combination of formulas (And it will come about on that day and a saying of the Lord Yahweh), it is also connected syntactically to 8:4–8 by a waw conjunction. That same pair of formulas also appears in 8:3. Structurally, therefore, this passage is a continuation of 8:1–3 and 8:4–8. This subsection also links thematically to those two passages by continuing the mourning motif (vv. 3, 8). The association is also lexical: The šîrôt (“songs”) of v. 3 find their counterpart in šîrêkem (“your songs”) in v. 10, and the verb ʾbl (“mourn”) of v. 8 in the noun ʾēbel (v. 10 twice; also qînâ, “lament”). The description of mourning in v. 10 is much more extensive than in vv. 3, 8. Five first-person verbs with Yahweh as the subject underscore his involvement in the sadness wrought by the judgment.291

9 The passage begins with an announcement about that day. The theme of the “day,” like in the rest of the book (with the exception of 9:11, 13), is about loss (e.g., 1:14; 2:16; 3:14; 4:2; 5:18–20; 6:3; 8:3, 13). As with the other occurrences, the time of fulfillment is indefinite. Disaster is coming soon. Some details about the catastrophes may be provided, but nothing is said about when these events will come to pass. The phrase a saying of the Lord Yahweh alerts the reader that what follows will be God’s words. This is confirmed by the series of first-person verbal forms, the first two of which are in this verse.

Yahweh will make the sun go down at noon and will darken the land in broad daylight. This theme of darkness harkens back to 5:8 and 5:18–20. The hymn of 5:8–9 extols the incomparable power of Yahweh, who can darken the day to night, while 5:18–20 depicts the imminent day of Yahweh as deep gloom. The present line stresses the reality of that power again, with Yahweh as the subject of the two verbs. A partial eclipse occurred on June 16, 763,292 and that may have inspired in a very tangible way the imagery of darkness here.293 This threat is directed specifically at the land (ʾereṣ) of Israel and not against the entire earth. As in 8:8, there is no need to extrapolate the scope of the event to eschatologically cosmic proportions. The day of Yahweh is portrayed as darkness on several occasions, where the referent is an imminent invasion, and its terrible aftermath lies in Israel’s future (e.g., Isa 5:30; Jer 13:16; Zeph 1:15).294 An invasion and an earthquake lie in Israel’s future.

10 With an eclipse comes the swift transformation of sunlight to utter, ominous darkness. The theme of the rapid change of things that should bring gladness to things characterized by sorrow and loss drives this verse. The wording engenders a sensation of total despair, with the triple repetition of kōl (all your songs, all loins, every head) and the death of the firstborn. Again, the subject of the verbs is Yahweh.

The vocabulary in the first clause is rooted in previous passages. In 5:7–8 and 6:12 the verb “turn” (hpk) is used for transforming an item into what it is not. When Israel is the subject (5:7; 6:12), the conversion represents a perversion of God’s ethical demands. In 5:8, it demonstrates the omnipotence of divine intervention in the natural realm. Here, the turning comes because of Yahweh’s judgment, and the setting is the cult. The phrases your festivals (haggêkem) and your songs (šîrêkem) are found in 5:21, 23.295 These feasts were the annual pilgrimages to the principal sanctuary (Exod 23:14–17; 34:22–25; Lev 23:4–8, 15–22, 33–43; Deut 16:1–17).296 These large communal gatherings were to be occasions for blessing Yahweh with songs of joy for the gifts of his provision and care. Instead, Yahweh decrees that they will be times for mourning (ʾēbel) with songs of lament (qînâ); 8:3 adds wailing.

To the laments in the temple are added mourning for the dead in the next two lines.297 Loci of lament are mentioned in 5:16–17: the public square, streets, and vineyards (there surely would have been others, like the homes). The Old Testament contains many passages that describe how the Israelites reacted to death. This verse gives attention to two activities, putting on sackcloth and shaving one’s head (or beard), but other actions also included wailing, tearing one’s clothes, rolling in dust or ashes, abstaining from anointing the head or beard with oil, and fasting (e.g., 2 Sam 1:11–12; 3:31–34; 1 Kgs 21:27; Neh 9:1; Esth 4:1–4; Dan 9:3).298 Sackcloth was a coarse garment usually made from goat’s hair. Rough and uncomfortable, it was bound around the waist in times of mourning (Lam 2:10).299 Shaving the head (or beard), although prohibited in a few passages, perhaps because of overtones of pagan rituals (Lev 21:5; Deut 14:1), was another expression of grief (Mic 1:16). These two practices frequently appear together (e.g., Isa 3:24; 22:12; Jer 48:37–38; Ezek 7:18; 27:31).

The third first-person verb states that Yahweh will make the events of that day like mourning for an only son.300 This is a devastating, heart-breaking loss for anyone. In ancient Israel, however, the death of a firstborn son was particularly significant. If there were no more children, it meant the loss of the family’s posterity and the disappearance of the family line (Gen 22:2, 12, 16; Prov 4:3). The death of the firstborn is associated with bitterness in the two other prophetic passages that mention the death of the firstborn (Jer 6:26; Zech 12:10). Indeed, the final clause of the verse sums up (the end of it301) what is announced in this pericope as a bitter day (cf. Zeph 1:14).

c. A Famine of Yahweh’s Word (8:11–12)

11Behold, days are coming—a saying of the Lord Yahweha—and I will send a famine on the land. Not a famine of bread and not thirst for water, but surely of hearing Yahweh’s words.b

12And they will stagger from seac to sea, and from north to east. They will roamd to seek the word of Yahweh,e but they will not find [it].

a. See p. 459 n. a above.

b. Some manuscripts of MT, LXX, Syr., and Vulg. have the singular “word” (NEB, NJB, CEB); cf. Barthélemy, Critique Textuelle de l’Ancien Testament, 686–87. This is perhaps to harmonize with v. 12 or with 3:1; 4:1; 5:1 (cf. 7:16). See the exposition.

c. LXX misread “sea” (miyyām) as “waters” (hydata)—perhaps because that Hebrew term has the same consonants (mym)—and made it the subject of the clause (“the waters will be shaken as far as the sea”). See Glenny, Finding Meaning in the Text, 131–32. Also note, however, that water (mym) appears in 4:8, the other place where the verb “wander” (nwʿ) occurs.

d. Following the Masoretic division, They will roam (yəšôṭəṭû) is placed with the second line of the verse (NRSV, NASB, NKJV, NJPS, ESV, CEB), not at the end of the first (NJB, NEB, NIV, HCSB). The initial verb, nāʿû (“They will wander”), does double-duty for the first line. See the exposition.

e. Tg. has “instruction from the Lord.”

The judgment shifts from natural disaster (and perhaps invasion in 8:3) to the absence of a word from Yahweh. The language of hunger and drought echoes 4:7–8, but whereas those verses referred to physical want, 8:11 speaks to the deprivation of communication from God and 8:12 to the desperate desire to hear a prophetic word. This is Yahweh’s response to Israel’s obstinate refusal to listen to the prophets (2:11–12; 7:10–17) and respond to the offers to seek him (5:4–6, 14–15)—even as they continue with their unacceptable religious activities. This dearth of God’s word complements his rejection of those rituals (5:21–23; 8:3) and the reversal of celebration into mourning (8:9–10). Twice the book records the people’s Hush! after disaster, which expresses anger and disappointment with Yahweh (6:10; 8:3). Now Yahweh mandates his own silence.

11 The two opening formulas, Behold, days are coming and a saying of the Lord Yahweh, mark the start of a new oracle. The first occurs in 4:2 in the context of a pronouncement of judgment upon well-to-do women. It also inaugurates an oracle at 9:13 that portrays the abundant restoration of Israel in its land. In 8:4–14, Behold, days are coming stylistically differs from it will come about on that day, which occurs at 8:3, 9, 13, even as it continues to develop the theme of the day of divine judgment. At the same time, a saying of the Lord Yahweh is consistent with earlier oracles of the chapter (8:3, 9), reinforcing the sense of continuity.

The first-person verb (and I will send) also maintains the connection with the previous two verses and their five first-person verbs that announce what Yahweh will do. “Send” (šlḥ) is in the hiphil, one of the five occasions in which the Old Testament uses the verb in this stem, each time with Yahweh as the subject. In every other case, he brings misfortune: either a plague (Exod 8:17; Lev 26:22; Ezek 14:13) or an enemy (2 Kgs 15:37). Now, Yahweh will send a famine. But dramatically, the verse clarifies that this will not be a physical famine, nor will it be a literal drought—two terrifying natural disasters in the ancient world. The litany of disasters of 4:6–11, which had been designed to drive Israel back to God, begins with hunger (v. 6) and the lack of water (vv. 7–8).302 The allusion here is brilliant and frightening. This catastrophe will be divine silence; Yahweh will no longer be heard (cf. 1 Sam 3:1; 28:6; Mic 3:6–7). It will be worse than what they experienced in the physical realm (Deut 8:3; Ps 42:1; cf. Matt 4:4).

What they will not hear are Yahweh’s words.303 In context, this plural probably refers to the words of the prophets, in particular of Amos (cf. Jer 10:1; 18:18; 37:2).304 That is, there will be no more divine messages delivered through God’s spokesperson, as he has been commanded to leave and return to Judah. The verb “hear” (šmʿ) also can have the meaning of “obey.”305 If that nuance is in any way implied here, it is an ironic statement. Just as there will be no more words from Yahweh, so Israel’s disobedience to his words will continue. This statement would surely come as a surprise. There is no suggestion in the confrontation at Bethel, for instance, that Amaziah feared the absence of God with Amos’s departure. The priest demands that the prophet leave (7:12–13), but he does not appear to expect that the religious life at Bethel will change. Israel tried to intimidate Yahweh’s prophets (2:11–12), but they assumed that their rituals were valid and efficacious and that they would encounter God at the sanctuaries. But in those spaces and on those occasions, there was no meeting Yahweh (4:4–5, 12–13).

12 The impact on the people of God’s silence is portrayed in dramatic fashion. As in 8:11, the initial verb (nwʿ) recalls 4:7–8. This verb is used in 4:8 to describe how people staggered about looking for enough water to drink. One of the meanings of this verb is “to wander” (e.g., Ps 59:15 [MT 59:16]; Jer 14:10; Lam 4:15), but in certain settings it suggests “stumbling around” in that wandering (e.g., Pss 107:27; 109:10; Isa 24:20; 29:9; Lam 4:14). Another definition is simply “to tremble” (e.g., Exod 20:18; Isa 6:4). The nuance of staggering fits best here, especially in light of v. 13.

In 4:8, the Israelites search from town to town. In this case, the range is incredibly wide, from sea to sea, and from north to east. Most understand the phrase from sea to sea to signify from the Mediterranean Sea to the Dead Sea or vice versa (i.e., from west to east or east to west). Paired with north to east in the next phrase, the idea is that the people will comb the entire national territory. On the basis of Psalm 72:8 and Zech 9:10, some understand from sea to sea to denote the ends of the earth, which could provide greater rhetorical impact.306 Nevertheless, the focus on the land of Israel in this section (8:4, 8, 9, 14) suggests the first and more common interpretation. What seems to be missing is a southern boundary. It has been suggested that the Dead Sea (or alternatively the Gulf of Aqaba; cf. 2 Kgs 14:25; Ps 107:3) marks a southern limit.307 Some say that the south is not mentioned because the word of God was received and active in Judah.308 There is no obvious explanation, however, for the absence of a clear reference to the south. What is not in doubt is the intent to stress Israel’s expansive and fruitless wandering.

The next verb is roam (šwṭ, cf. 2 Sam 24:2, 8; 2 Chr 16:9; Dan 12:4). MT’s accents indicate that, instead of this verb continuing the first part of the verse to match stumble,309 it is to be connected to the following infinitive: They will roam to seek…310 This is roaming with an express purpose. Israel desires the word of Yahweh. The singular word of Yahweh is more generic than Yahweh’s words in v. 11. The people pursue any communication from God. The phrase to seek (bqš) the word of Yahweh is unique in the Old Testament, but the encouragement to seek Yahweh is pervasive (e.g., 2 Chr 20:4; Pss 27:8; 105:3; Prov 28:5; Hos 3:5; Zeph 2:3).311 Earlier, the people were told to seek Yahweh and good (5:4–5, 14). The verb utilized there is drš, a synonym of bqš. The book depicts a religious Israel, for whom the cult would have been the normal means of encountering God. But the performance of rituals is no guarantee that he will be found. Yahweh has rejected the cult and its manner of seeking him (cf. Hos 5:6; see, with different verbs or objects, Prov 1:28; Ezek 7:26; Mal 2:7).
d. The Failure of False Faith (8:13–14)

13On that day, the beautiful virgins and the young men will faint with thirst.a

14The ones who swear by the guiltb of Samaria and say, “By the life of your god, O Dan, and by the life of the wayc of Beersheba,”d they will fall and not rise again.

a. For this verse, Tg. has: “At that time, the congregations of Israel, who are like beautiful maidens that in their beauty fornicated with wicked young men, shall be weary and they shall be struck down and prostrated with thirst.”

b. This is the translation in most English versions. LXX has “atonement” (hilasmou); Syr., “idols”; Tg., Vulg., “sin”; cf. Barthélemy, Critique Textuelle de l’Ancien Testament, 688–89. See the exposition.

c. Perhaps to harmonize with the preceding clause, LXX has “your god” (ho theos sou) for “the way.”

d. For the quote, Tg. reads, “The god who is in Dan lives, and the laws of Beersheba endure.”

This is the final oracle in the section that expands on 8:1–3. It is connected in several ways to other verses in 8:4–14 and beyond.312 To begin with, the introductory formula on that day is similar to 8:9 (but without the verb) and opens the last in the series of oracles about the “day(s)” (8:9–10, 11–12) that depict the end of Israel (8:3). The thirst in v. 13 alludes to 8:11–12 and suggests that this pericope develops the theme of the fruitless search for the word of God. The swearing by those condemned to ruin in v. 14 contrasts sharply with the swearing of the omnipotent God in 8:7, who judges the evils of vv. 4–6. The oaths are reminiscent of the sentiments of the oppressors that are cited in v. 5. These various links weave this oracle into 8:4–12 and make it a fitting conclusion to the section.

There are at least four connections to earlier passages in the book. The young men of 8:13 appear in 2:11 and 4:10. The term virgin is the descriptor of Israel in 5:2, which also has the phrase has fallen, never to rise—essentially repeated in 8:14. Finally, Beersheba reappears as a cultic site (5:5).

13 This verse continues the figurative meaning of the deep thirst for the word of Yahweh in 8:11–12. Those verses said specifically that the hunger and thirst that Yahweh is sending are not about physical need but about the scarcity of the word of God. The stumbling and wandering quest for such a word is to encompass the breadth of Israel’s territory. That seekers will faint with thirst (cf. Jonah 4:8) in this verse follows naturally on the metaphor of the preceding pericope.313 Their efforts will be incredibly fatiguing. The rhetorical purpose of switching the subject from the nation in 8:12 to a certain group, specifically the maidens and young men,314 is to make the most vigorous segment of the population exemplars of the effect of the drought of hearing from God (cf. Isa 40:29–31). Even the strongest cannot endure! The next verse returns to the broader public.

The nation of Israel is the virgin who lies dying in 5:2. Here, the beautiful virgins within Israel are weak from not being able to secure some word from Yahweh. Young men are mentioned first in 2:11. From among them, Yahweh had chosen some to be Nazarites, men who were models of devotion. In contrast, in 8:13, they represent the obstinate yet religiously active nation that suffers from not obeying the ways of God. In the recent past, Yahweh had slain the young men in battle in an effort to have Israel return to him (4:10); in the near future, they will be faint because he will not give them spiritual sustenance.

14 There are two significant interpretive matters in this verse. The first is the identity of the ones who swear (participle) and say (finite verb). Literarily, the young women and men in the previous verse are the subject of both verbal forms as, syntactically, the opening participle maintains the description of the preceding verse.315 It is to be remembered, however, that these young people are representative of Israel (v. 13) and that the natural calamities and the famine of the word of Yahweh will befall the entire nation (vv. 8–12). In addition, the last line of the chapter, they will fall and not rise again, is almost identical to 5:2, which speaks of Israel’s fate. Consequently, this verse can also refer to the actions and words of the broader population.316

Andersen and Freedman, on the other hand, connect the participle to 8:4 (those who tread). In both cases a damning quote and a sentence of judgment follow (8:5–7). In addition, the divine oath of v. 7—a strong contrast to the oath in this verse—could serve as an inclusio for 8:4–14. This would connect those who swear to those who take advantage of the vulnerable.317 There are potentially two other points in favor of their interpretive suggestion. On the one hand, other objectionable speech in the book comes from the mouths of those in positions of privilege (4:1; 7:10–13; perhaps 9:10). The book also exhibits a pattern of universal censure accompanied by a focus on specific groups within the nation. Note, for example, that interplay in 3:9–4:3; 5:1–17. This movement from the broader to the concentrated is true of speech as well, where there are occasions in which all are culpable (2:12; 6:10; 8:3), not just the privileged. In other words, 8:4–14 could be another example of this pattern that has both the nation and a smaller target in view. The position taken here, however, is that it is primarily the people as a whole who are censured. Other links with the rest of the book substantiate this interpretation.

The second interpretive challenge is to ascertain the meaning of the religious references in the first three clauses. The complexity of the alternatives and their combinations can be daunting. To anticipate the conclusion: I hold that the text condemns a distorted Yahwism; any allusion to other deities would be secondary.

To swear by a deity affirms belief and trust in, and loyalty to, that deity in the process of making a statement or a vow. Israel was to swear only by Yahweh and his name (e.g., Deut 6:13; 10:20; 1 Sam 20:42; Ps 63:11 [MT 63:12])318 and had been warned not to swear by any other god (e.g., Josh 23:7; Jer 5:7: 12:16; Zeph 1:5). This much is clear, even as is the difference between swearing by the guilt of Samaria and Yahweh’s oath in 8:7, between the swearing of those exhausted in their futile search and that of the deity who judges them.

What does it mean to swear by the guilt of Samaria (ʾašmat šōmərôn)? Some argue for emending guilt to retrieve an original mention of a goddess, which this descriptor replaced. One proposal is that at one time the text read “Asherah (ʾăšērat) of Samaria” (cf. 1 Kgs 16:33; 2 Kgs 13:6),319 an option that requires several changes to the text and has not found much support. The worship of Asherah (or some vestige of it) in ancient Israel is a generally accepted fact, especially after the much-debated discoveries at Kuntillet ‘Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom. But even Hadley, who believes that the goddess (and her symbol) was part of the religious landscape, does not find the emendation persuasive.320 A more common view repoints the text to read “Ashima (ʾăšimat) of Samaria.”321 This goddess may have been brought to Israel after its defeat by the Assyrians in 722 by groups that the empire transplanted there (2 Kgs 17:30), decades after the putative dates of Amos’s ministry.322

There is no need to alter the Hebrew text, however, even if one holds that guilt is a pejorative reference to a goddess. It is an evaluative term that could signify any deity or unacceptable practice that brought fault upon Israel. What must be decided is whether the veneration of a goddess (or any deity) is meant or if the target is an improper Yahwism and what that might have looked like. If the latter, there are at least two possible ways to understand the guilt of Samaria. The phrase could allude specifically to the golden calf or bull at Bethel, Israel’s most important state shrine that had been established by Jeroboam I (1 Kgs 12:28–30).323 This image was foundational for Israel, whose capital at this time was Samaria. Hence, Hosea calls it the “calf of Samaria” (8:5–6; cf. 7:1; 10:5–8; Mic 1:5–7; Jer 23:13).324 Another option is to take the guilt of Samaria as a general reference to what the prophet saw as the illegitimate worship centers and rituals that negatively impacted true faith in Yahweh in Israel. Either way, the issue concerns proper belief in Yahweh. Could other deities have motivated this insulting label? Yes, but the harshest prophetic invective in the book is directed at a skewed Yahwism that was precipitating Israel’s demise. Either Israel swore by a false Yahweh out of desperation as it looked to somehow receive a word or these sorts of oaths were another reason for their deserved judgment.

The next two clauses are introduced by a finite verb (and [they] say). Is the change from the participle (the ones who swear) to this verbal form a stylistic modification, or is the intention to distinguish the two activities? If the purpose is to make a differentiation, swearing by the guilt of Samaria could be a generic summary and the oaths related to Dan and Beersheba particular examples of wrongdoing. They would form a merism incorporating the geographic scope of Israel’s religious sin (“from Dan to Beersheba”; cf. 1 Sam 3:20), as 8:12 had done in its own way. That is, the condemnation would incorporate the most northern cult site at Dan and the pilgrimage to Beersheba in southern Judah that is mentioned in 5:5.325 If this interpretation is correct, then the absence of Bethel and Gilgal, which appear elsewhere in the book, is explained. There would be no need to refer to them within this sweeping framework.

The oaths are by the life of (ḥê)…. The term “life” is pointed differently when it refers to Yahweh or is spoken by him (ḥay; the only exception is Dan 12:7) than when it refers to someone else or another deity (ḥê).326 The choice of ḥê in this line could signal that these are not suitable oaths. The first of the two is by the life of your god (ḥê ʾĕlōhêkā), O Dan. In Hebrew, your god is a plural and could be translated as “your gods,”327 but scholars usually assume the singular. A few take this to be a reference to a local Baal or another deity,328 but many understand this to be the golden calf or bull installed by Jeroboam I (along with the one at Bethel; 1 Kgs 12:28–30).329

Recent archaeological studies at Dan in Area T, Stratum II, are helpful at this juncture.330 Excavations reveal the existence of a functioning cult center at Dan in the eighth century. The finds demonstrate significant correspondence with aspects of Yahwistic feasts listed for the offerings (e.g., Lev 4–7; Deut 18:1– 8). These include the makeup of the animal remains and various cultic realia, like shovels and bowls (including probably a mizrāq vessel). In addition, the layout of the cultic area is similar to that of Solomon’s Temple in 1 Kgs 6–7. Interestingly, the data imply both formal cultic activity and more communal family feasts and rites offered by individuals (cf. 1 Sam 1:3–28; 2:12–17). While many of these items have much in common with religious sites and practices across the ancient Near East, the level of resemblance with what is prescribed for venerating Yahweh in the Old Testament strongly suggests that 8:14 speaks of the worship of Yahweh at Dan, even though the nature of this Yahweh is distorted.

By the life of the way (derek) of Beersheba is interpreted in several ways, some of which are based on emendations. Each of these seeks to inject a deity into the clause,331 whether Yahweh or another deity. A prominent suggestion is “your uncle” or “your loved one” (dōdəkā), signifying a patron deity.332 Another possibility is “power,” based on the Ugaritic drkt (“dominion, strength”). This would yield the divine epithet “the strength of Beersheba.”333 A more recent proposal comes from Linville, who argues for a participle, “the one who strides” (dōrēk).334 Nevertheless, the phrase by … the way of Beersheba can be taken at face value as referring to the pilgrimage road or journey to that Southern site.335 The inscriptions at Kuntillet ‘Ajrud in the northern Sinai include the phrase “Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah.” This could indicate that some from Israel traveled south; so, presumably, pilgrims from the Northern Kingdom made their way to Beersheba to worship Yahweh there. The value of this interpretive option is that it reinforces this concept in 5:5.336 The book of Amos mentions traveling to cultic sites in two other passages (4:4–5; 5:4–5). The two clauses in this verse expand their geographical extent and connect oath-taking to pilgrimages (cf. Hos 4:15).337

In sum, the three oaths in 8:14 all have Yahweh as their primary referent. This position does not deny that there may be a hint of venerating other deities, but the evidence does not explicitly point in that direction for this verse. The possible links to the tradition of Jeroboam I (1 Kgs 12:28–30), the Israelite religious context of the rest of the section (especially 8:5, 11–12), the mention of pilgrimage to Beersheba elsewhere (5:5), archaeological research (particularly at Dan), and the connection of the last line to 5:2 and Israel’s defeat (see below) prompt the conclusion that the text continues its attack on the Yahwism of the Northern Kingdom.

An additional feature that surfaces in this verse is the probable existence of local manifestations of belief in Yahweh. It is beyond the purview of this commentary to enter into debates about whether these manifestations were simply local or regional beliefs and practices related to a generally unified concept of Yahweh or if they represent what has been called fragmentation, where each manifestation is of a somewhat distinct deity, with each sharing the name and other aspects of a more fluid understanding of Yahweh.338 Either way, the prophet found this religious worldview and its activities unacceptable and worthy of judgment. This Yahweh was not the Yahweh who had called him to prophesy and to announce Israel’s ruin.

This section, 8:1–14, expands on 8:1–3 and its declaration that the end has come to my people Israel. The last line of this verse vividly expresses this same verdict: They will fall and not rise again. It sums up the series of oracles as the inevitable outcome of the nation’s social and religious attitudes and behaviors. This line echoes 5:2, which metaphorically described a terrible military defeat. In 8:1–14, the judgment is through natural disaster and divine silence.
