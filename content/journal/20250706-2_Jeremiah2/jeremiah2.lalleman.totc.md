
2. CALLS TO REPENTANCE AND WORDS OF JUDGMENT (2 – 6)
A. A broken marriage (2:1–37)
i. Religious and political adultery (2:1–19)
Context

In the context of the previous chapter, Jeremiah 2:1 illustrates that Jeremiah is indeed appointed as the prophet who speaks the word of the Lord. Verse 1 introduces the words that follow, but it is difficult to identify where God’s words end. Chapters 2 – 6 as a whole are regarded as Jeremiah’s earliest prophecies during the reign of King Josiah, and they constitute part of the content of the first scroll read to King Jehoiakim in Jeremiah 36 (see Introduction, p. 24). The whole of chapter 2 deals with how the people are forsaking their God, either by worshipping idols or by making political treaties with other nations, instead of trusting in God alone.

Following the idyllic picture of love and marriage in 2:1–3, the people of Israel are accused of forsaking their ‘marriage vows’. This is conveyed in the form of a court hearing in verses 4–13. Verses 14–19 pose several questions, charging Israel with the sin of relying more on alliances with other nations than on God.

Verses 5c and 8c repeat the theme of following worthless idols, thus forming an inclusio around the words, Where is the LORD, in verses 6 and 8. These verses together form a chiasm (on the use of inclusio and chiasm see Introduction, p. 47):

A – following worthless idols (5c)

B – where is the LORD? (6)

B’ – where is the LORD? (8)

A’ – following worthless idols (8c)

The charges follow in verse 9, and the heavens are called as witnesses in verse 12.
Comment

2:1–3. The message is to be proclaimed in the capital of Judah, not in Jeremiah’s home town. The overall theme of what follows is that God’s people have forsaken him. The past is pictured as an ideal time (vv. 2–3). When God saved his people from slavery in Egypt, he led them through the desert and its dangers. They were completely dependent on God and, according to verse 3, completely committed to him, holy and separated from the other nations to be God’s special people (Exod. 19:4–6). Israel was God’s bride, and the covenant at Sinai was regarded as a ‘marriage’. However, the Old Testament is very clear that God does not have a partner and that worship of a female goddess is absolutely forbidden. The devotion in verse 2 is a translation of the word ḥesed, indicating faithfulness, loyalty and love, in the sense of God’s steadfast love for his people within the covenant, but also the love of God’s people towards one another. The use of ḥesed here fits with the idea of covenant, which is the main message of the prophetic speech: there has been a loyal relationship since Sinai, but Israel has broken it by being unfaithful to God, following other gods (vv. 11, 13) and relying on other nations more than on God (v. 18). Verse 3 refers to Israel as God’s firstfruits. The firstfruits of the harvest were dedicated to God (Exod. 23:19; Deut. 26). Those who devoured Israel (lit. ‘eat’) are the nations who attacked Israel during the wanderings in the desert (e.g. Amalek, Exod. 17:8–16). Those who ill-treated God’s special people were ill-treated by him.

4. Verses 4–13 present a ‘court case’ in which God addresses his people. Jacob and Israel are the usual names for the northern kingdom which was exiled in 722. In the context of this chapter, the prophet deals with the unfaithfulness of the whole covenant people from the beginning, including Judah in his own day (see v. 2, Jerusalem). The word ‘clan’ (TNIV) is a good translation of the Hebrew and indicates a unit of kinship larger than just one family, but smaller than a tribe. However, in this context we can read it as ‘tribes’.1

5–8. In this rhetorical question, the appalling truth of Israel’s apostasy is exposed. How can the people forsake a God who is holy and perfect? Compared to him, idols are worthless (cf. 10:15). The word idols is absent in Hebrew, so it could be translated ‘worthless things’, but idols are certainly the intended meaning. The result of worshipping worthless things is to become worthless yourself. The Hebrew word for ‘worthless’ (heḅel) plays an important role in the book of Ecclesiastes: it is vanity, a breath, a puff of air which is gone immediately – it is ‘nothing’. Lundbom (1999: 259) translates: ‘They went after The Nothing [Hebrew uses the singular form with a definite article], and became nothing.’ Idols do not provide a foundation for a person’s life. Those who trust in gods who are no gods are ‘building on sand’, rather than on solid ground. There is a long history of unfaithfulness, beginning with the ancestors of the present generation. Soon after the death of Joshua and the rest of the generation who had experienced the conquest of the Promised Land, people forgot their past and their God and started worshipping the fertility gods of the Canaanites (Judg. 2:10–13). Verse 6 recalls the miracle of God’s faithfulness to Israel throughout dark and dangerous times in the desert, when only God could save them. The Hebrew word for utter darkness (v. 6; ‘deep darkness’, NRSV) is the same as in Psalm 23:4 (valley of the ‘shadow of death’). The Promised Land is the opposite (v. 7): a good and spacious land, a land flowing with milk and honey (Exod. 3:8). It is therefore all the more serious that Israel has defiled the land, which is God’s inheritance. The Hebrew reads, ‘and my inheritance you made into an abomination’. The land remains ‘God’s land’, given to his people (Lev. 25:23; Deut. 15:4). Sin ‘defiles’ or ‘pollutes’ the land. The words ‘defile’ and ‘abomination’ (NRSV) are used in Leviticus 18:22–30 with reference to sexual sins. In those verses it is clear that the nations living in the land had defiled it and were therefore driven out: the land vomited out its inhabitants. This should be a warning to Israel. In Numbers 35:33–34 murder (bloodshed) is said to defile the land. The word ‘abomination’ is also used of the idols and practices of the Canaanite nations which resulted in their being expelled from the land (see Lev. 18:27, 29; Deut. 18:9, 12; 20:18). Those who are responsible for the people have led them astray: the priests, leaders and prophets (v. 8). The word for leaders is ‘shepherds’ in Hebrew, and often refers to political leaders or even kings (see 23:1–2; Ezek. 34). The people who deal with the law may be the same as the priests, who were meant to teach the law of God (Deut. 31:9–13; 33:10; Hos. 4:4–6; Mal. 2:7). In the book of Jeremiah we regularly read about Jeremiah’s battle with the false prophets (e.g. 8:10–11; 23:9–32; 28; 29:8–9). The false prophets are said to prophesy by Baal (v. 8). This is the first mention in Jeremiah of the name of the Canaanite fertility god: the name of the ‘nothing’ of verse 5 is now revealed. Worshipping this god had always been a danger to the covenant people, despite all the warnings (Deut. 6:10–15). These prophets follow ‘worthless things’, things that cannot help, ‘that do not profit’ (NRSV). TNIV interprets those ‘things’ as idols (see vv. 5, 11). Idols are worthless because they do not give you anything, in contrast with the one and only God, the Creator of heaven and earth, who provides the rain and the harvest.

9. Therefore marks the transition to the announcement of judgment. The Hebrew verb for bring charges, used twice in this verse, refers to a lawsuit, as in Hosea 4:1–3. God is both accuser and judge. He has the right to be both, for the people have broken his covenant.

10–11. By means of a rhetorical question, the people are accused of doing something abnormal: no people would ever change their gods. Moreover, the gods of other nations are no gods at all. So imagine how totally absurd it is that Israel should exchange the only one, true and living God for things which are no gods at all (cf. also Deut. 32:15–18). The Hebrew word for nation (gôy, v. 11) is often used for non-Israelites. Israel regarded themselves as ‘better’ than the gôyîm. God is called Israel’s ‘Glory’ (NIV), glorious God (TNIV; see also Ps. 106:20 about the golden calf in the wilderness; and Rom. 1:23). The covenant people, like the prophets in verse 8, are accused of following worthless idols, which do not profit and cannot rescue or sustain them as the living God does. ‘Kittim’ (NIV) is Cyprus (TNIV and NRSV). Here the reference is to the inhabitants of all the islands and coastlands to the west, around the Mediterranean. Kedar is the desert area in the east where Bedouins lived. So the invitation is to search in the west and the east.

12–13. NRSV reads ‘be shocked, be utterly desolate’, whereas TNIV takes the two verbs together: and shudder with great horror. The Hebrew verb ‘to be desolate’ can also mean ‘to be dry’, which may indicate that the heavens will not give rain. They are witnesses of Israel’s apostasy (see also Deut. 4:26; 32:1; Isa. 1:2; cf. Mic. 6:1–2) and are shocked at Israel’s unfaithfulness. The idea of drought links verses 12 and 13: the people have left God who is the spring or source (better than ‘fountain’, NRSV) of living water (see also 17:13). God is the only one who can provide for all the people’s needs, both spiritual and material. This is what he always did throughout the period in the wilderness. Yet in Canaan, his people came to rely more on the fertility god Baal. They have chosen to pursue their own means of obtaining water, life and abundance. But those supplies are unreliable. They are like broken cisterns. These holes in the ground were used to hold water in times of drought and would have been plastered to prevent seepage. But the plaster could easily crack and the water would leak out. Similarly, these ‘suppliers of human needs’ or idols are unreliable.

14–16. Three questions are asked, which establish a pattern found in other Jeremianic passages (8:4–5, 19, 22). The first two questions are rhetorical: of course Israel is not a slave, because God has delivered them out of slavery. Of course they are not a slave born in their master’s house. So why then are they taken away into slavery through war? Why then emphasizes the absurdity of the situation and also implies an accusation. Lions are the great nations who threaten to kill and conquer God’s people. The Assyrians were known for plundering and destroying nations and land (2 Kgs 17:20; see also Isa. 5:29–30). These verses in Jeremiah 2 may be referring to the Egyptians or the Babylonians, who were growing stronger and becoming a real threat to Judah. Eventually the Babylonians conquered Judah and led many people into exile, after plundering and destroying Jerusalem and the temple (see Jer. 4:7; 50:17).

Memphis (v. 16), also called Noph, once was the capital of Egypt, south of modern-day Cairo. Tahpanhes was a city in the north-east, on the caravan route to the land of Israel. NRSV reads ‘have broken the crown of your head’, which slightly changes the meaning of the Hebrew verb (‘to graze’), and NIV translates as ‘shaved’. Shaving the head was a sign of submission and disgrace (2 Sam. 10:4; Isa. 7:20). The nation is personified as a woman, who will be put to shame by Egypt in which she had trusted at intervals. We may recall Josiah, the then ‘(crown of the) head’ or leader of the nation, who in 609 BC was killed by the Egyptian Pharaoh Necho on his way to the north. Maybe Necho plundered Judah on that journey (2 Kgs 23:29–30).

17–18. The disaster caused by the great nations of these days has been inflicted on account of Israel’s behaviour. They have preferred to trust more in political treaties than in the Lord their God, who had made a covenant with them after their deliverance from Egypt when he led them through the desert. They have allowed themselves to become dependent for their livelihood (water; see also v. 13) on others, rather than on the true living God, and this will surely bring disaster. The ‘Shihor’ (NIV) is another name for the Nile, although it can also mean an arm of the Nile. The ‘River’ (v. 18, NIV) is the Euphrates (cf. Gen. 15:18).

19. On the subject of backsliding, see also 3:22; 5:6; 14:7. The root of the Hebrew verb is šûb, which is used with many variations throughout the book of Jeremiah (see Introduction, p. 37). Forsaking God by not living according to the covenant and its stipulations will result in disaster, as already proclaimed by Moses (cf. Deut. 27 – 28). The message of the great prophets of Israel is completely in accordance with the Torah. This section of the prophetic message ends with the statement that these are the words of the Lord Almighty.
Meaning

The idyllic picture of a blossoming love between God and his people (2:1–3) contrasts sharply with what is to come: the bride has forgotten her ‘first love’. What could have been a ‘lifelong’ loving relationship soon turns out to be a ‘lifelong’ disappointment on the part of the ‘bridegroom’.

Thus far, the people have been confronted with their own unfaithfulness, having exchanged the true and living God for idols that are worthless. They have relied more on other nations than on the God who called them into being. Astonishment at such behaviour is expressed throughout the whole passage.
ii. Consequences for a defiled and incorrigible nation (2:20–37)
Context

The people’s breach of the covenant is pictured in vivid, metaphorical language. The passage develops what has already been said. Israel is compared unfavourably with a bride (v. 32; cf. v. 2); she is a prostitute, a corrupt, wild vine, a she-camel – metaphors that express God’s great disappointment, and which contrast with earlier expectations in 2:2–3. This section is one long complaint by a hurt covenant partner.
Comment

20. The grammatical form (you broke off) is an archaic one and corresponds to the first-person singular (so NKJV: ‘I have broken’, with God as the subject). However, you is also a possible translation, in which case it is Israel who has tried to break away, like an ox breaking the straps of a yoke. This again indicates rebellion against the covenant by a resistant nation. The second image is of a prostitute willing to serve her lovers. The high hills and green trees were known as cultic places where Canaanite fertility idols were worshipped, involving prostitution (see 2 Kgs 17:9–11; Hos. 4:10–14). Fertility in the land was, as it were, stimulated by cultic fertility rites. We may understand this allusion to prostitution in two ways: first, Israel had ‘left her husband’ (God), by serving other ‘men’ (idols); secondly, people were literally involved in cult prostitution.

21. The image of a high-quality vine also occurs in Isaiah 5:1–7. The opening of verse 21 contrasts with the previous verse: ‘Yet I’ (with emphasis; see NRSV). God planted a lovely vine and expected excellent grapes, but the vine has degenerated, a metaphor for Israel’s bad behaviour resulting in disaster.

22. The metaphor used here is one of being dirty; yet nothing can wash away the stains. The first type of soap mentioned is carbonate of soda; the second is made of soda plants. The stains may be the result of blood from committing crimes. Sins pictured as ‘dirt’ that needs to be ‘washed’ is a metaphor used also in Isaiah 1:15–20.

23–25. The people are compared to a young female camel (v. 23), unsteady in her movements, distracted this way and that. This is how Israel is behaving, approaching different gods and making treaties, sometimes with Egypt, sometimes with Assyria. The valley is a reminder of the Valley of Ben-Hinnom, where dreadful idolatry took place (cf. 7:31–32; 2 Kgs 23:10). Here the Hebrew reads the ‘way’ in the valley, and ‘way’ often indicates behaviour (how you behaved, TNIV). The second image is of a female wild ass sniffing and following where the scent leads at mating time (v. 24). The males will easily find her. Similarly, the Israelites can easily be found at the places of idol worship. Again, this image refers to the Canaanite fertility cult. The people are addicted to idol worship and exhaust themselves by doing everything they can to please these foreign gods (v. 25, lit. ‘strangers’).

26. The people are compared to a thief who is ashamed at being caught. Shame is a serious matter in countries of the Middle East, even today. The people of Israel and their political and religious leaders are disgraced. Worshipping Baal, who is also called ‘Shame’ (cf. 3:24; 11:13; Hos. 9:10), leads to ‘being ashamed’.

27–28. God should be called ‘Father’ (see Deut. 32:18). But wooden poles representing Asherah, the female Canaanite fertility god, are worshipped instead. She would logically be ‘mother’. The stone is the male fertility symbol and represents Baal, who is said to have given birth, a role which naturally belongs to a mother. According to Lundbom (1999: 284–285), these mixed images are meant ironically. God’s people regard the Canaanite gods as their creator, from whom they have received fertility in the land, rather than from the only true and living God. People have turned their backs towards God, and are facing the idols (cf. Ezek. 8:16). This is another sign of contempt. When God shows his face, this is a sign of favour (Num. 6:26), but there is a time when he shows his back (Jer. 18:17). In times of trouble, the people run towards their God for help and deliverance, but, as verse 28 states ironically, they should ask for help from the gods they have continually been serving. For the last part of verse 28, see also Jeremiah 11:13.

29–30. Israel accuses God, but it should be the other way round, since they have rebelled against him (see also 2:9). The strongest word for ‘sin’ is used here; it is wilful rebellion. God has punished his people (lit. ‘your sons’) for their correction, but it has not produced the desired result. The ‘sons’ or ‘children’ (NRSV) may be young people killed in wars, or generally members of Israel being warned by disastrous events (see also Jer. 5:3; 7:28; 17:23). Prophets who speak the truth have been killed (26:20–23; Amos 2:11–12; Matt. 23:35, 37).

31. Unlike the desert (2:6), God is not associated with darkness, danger or emptiness. On the contrary, he was a safe place for the people and carried them through the wilderness. The word for great darkness occurs only here, and its meaning is uncertain. The Hebrew for free to roam is not clear, and translations differ. TNIV, however, seems to bring out the meaning well, or we might just translate, ‘we roam’. Reinforced by the following verse, the meaning is that the people wandered away from God and did not want to serve him (‘come to him’ to worship) any more. This verse contains three rhetorical questions, ending with ‘why’ (cf. v. 14), expressing unbelief about such rebellion (‘why then?’).

32. More rhetorical questions follow, expressing the shock and unexpectedness of Israel’s behaviour. A bride would never forget her husband’s wedding gift (cf. Ezek. 16:10–13). The meaning of wedding ornaments (TNIV) is uncertain. The overall significance is that Israel, as God’s bride, has forgotten him for many years now, which is unthinkable.

33. This verse is difficult. NRSV speaks of ‘wicked women’ who can learn from Israel’s behaviour. Yet the Hebrew does not include the word for ‘women’, and gives only ‘wicked’ in the feminine form. The verb learn can mean ‘to be used to’. Thus McKane (1986: 49) translates the sentence: ‘So you have become expert at evil behaviour.’ This seems to be closest to the Hebrew. The love that the people are seeking resumes the metaphor of foreign (often fertility) gods.

34–35. Apart from idolatry, the people of God are also guilty of the sin of social injustice (see also Jer. 7:9; Amos 2:6–7). Innocent people are killed as if they were thieves breaking in at night-time (see Exod. 22:2–3), and their bloodstains can still be seen on the murderers’ clothes. However, the people are denying their guilt, which makes things even worse.

36–37. The first Hebrew verb (go about, TNIV) causes some problems. Holladay (1986: 111) takes it to mean ‘doing something lightly’. The idea is that Israel is easily going from one nation to another to secure their safety by making treaties. They do not trust their God alone. But they will suffer disappointment and be led into exile, like captives with their hands above their heads, a scene depicted in ancient reliefs.
Meaning

God’s judgment does not come out of the blue. It is well explained, and the reasons can be understood by everyone. Chapter 2 clarifies the validity of God’s charges against his people and the resulting verdict. The prophet’s role is to convey this rather morbid message to his own people. It is an ‘uprooting’ message indeed (cf. 1:10).
