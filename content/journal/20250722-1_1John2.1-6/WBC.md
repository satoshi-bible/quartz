Translation

1What was there from the beginning—which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have observed, and felt with our hands—is our subject: the word of life. 2The life was revealed. We have seena it; we are bearing witness, and proclaiming to you the eternal life which existed with the Father and has been revealed to us. 3What we have seen and heard we are declaring to you as well, so that you also may share our fellowship: a fellowship which we have with the Father, and with his Son Jesus Christ. 4And web are writing this in order that ourc joy may be complete!
Notes

a. B inserts the relative ὃ (literally, “that which”) before ἑωράκαμεν, “we have seen” (and uses the alternative spelling ἑοράκαμεν), thus forming a parallel to the phrase ὃ ἑωράκαμεν (“what we have seen”) at the beginning of v 3. However, the paratactic Gr. construction is most naturally interpreted if ἑωράκαμεν is connected with the preceding ζωή (“life”), as in our translation, rather than with the object of ἀπαγγέλλομεν (τὴν ζωὴν τὴν αἰώνιον) (“we are proclaiming to you [the eternal life]”). We therefore regard the B text as secondary. But see Haas, Handbook, 25, who allows the possibility that ἑωράκαμεν may be connected with either “the life” or “the eternal life”; cf. also Westcorr, 9.

b. Instead of ἡμεῖς, “we” (read by A* B, et al.), some MSS (including Ac C K) have ὑμῖν (“to you”). This variation may be discounted, since it probably represents a subsequent desire on the part of a scribe to supply a missing personal object for the verb γράφομεν (“we are writing”). As it stands, however, the phrase ταῦτα γράφομεν ἡμεῖς (literally, “we are writing these things”) is deliberately emphatic.

c. Some witnesses (AC2 vid K, et al.) read ὑμῶν (“your [joy]”) instead of ἡμῶν (“our [joy]”), which is the version of B Ψ, et al. The variant (ὑμῶν) is well-attested, and versions of this variation occur in other texts of 1 John (cf. 2:25). The reading ἡ χαρὰ ὕμῶν (“your joy”) may also have been influenced by the Gr. of John 15:11 and 16:24. The words ἡμῶν and ὑμῶν are written almost identically in the original, and it is easy to see how the confusion may have arisen. Both readings make sense in the context (see the comment below), but the writer’s indentification with his readers in terms of “fellowship” suggests that a mutual “joy” (ἡ χαρὰ ἡμῶν, “our joy”) is in view at this point also.
Form/Structure/Setting

The preface of 1 John clearly echoes the prologue, or introduction, to the Fourth Gospel (John 1; see further below, 5–6). Unlike the author of John’s Gospel, the writer of 1 John does not announce in his preface all the ideas which will be developed in the body of the work (cf. Smalley, “Johannes 1, 51,” 300–307). But three of the main themes are introduced, and these are subsequently picked up and developed: life in Christ (cf. 5:11–12, 20); the historical reality of God’s revelation in Jesus Christ (cf. 4:2; 5:6); and the idea of “seeing” in association with witness (cf. 4:14, 5:6–12). Nevertheless, perhaps because 1 John is different in character and approach from the Fourth Gospel, the preface to the letter is less clear in its expression and less profound in its thought than the Johannine prologue (see further 5; also xxvi–viii, especially xxvii).

Possibly the preface to 1 John was constructed by the author after he had written the remainder of the letter (cf. the prologue to the Fourth Gospel; see Smalley, John, 119–21), as a summary statement of his main topic and some of its ramifications. But equally, and in our view more probably, the preface may have been composed shortly before the main body of the work (note the way in which the terms ἀγγελία [“news”], ἀκηκόαμεν, [“we have heard”] and ἀναγγἑλλομεν [“we are proclaiming”] in v 5 appear to pick up and clarify the leading ideas expressed in vv 1–4).

The opening of 1 John, like its conclusion, lacks any of the literary features which are normally associated with the composition of a letter as such (contrast 2 and 3 John; and cf. the introduction, xxxiii); although Francis (ZNW 61 [1970] 110–26) points out that formal endings were not a necessary part of ancient letter-writing. Rather than acting as the beginning of a letter, 1 John 1:1–4 appears, on the contrary, to be a self-contained literary unit. (Houlden, 46, stresses this characteristic, and suggests that a secondary, editorial hand was responsible for the preface; but in view of the connections between vv 1–4 and v 5, stated above, we find this view unconvincing.)

Several commentators (e.g. Dodd, 1–3; Houlden, 45–46) have noted some roughness in the grammar of these four verses, and a consequent obscurity in their exact meaning. Nevertheless, they constitute an impressive introduction to the work, and are constructed with dramatic sensitivity (cf. Bultmann, 12–13). Thus, (a) the interest in the passage is constantly thrown forward, from the momentous fact that the word of life has been revealed to the further climax that this word is now being disclosed to the present readers; (b) the suspense is built up and intensified in the movement toward this climax by a series of similar, short (and sometimes repeated) phrases: ὃ ἀκηκόαμεν (“what we have heard”), ὃ ἐθεασἀμεθα (“what we have seen,” v 1), and so on. In this introduction the main subject of 1 John is firmly stated: the gospel means “life in Jesus Christ.”
Comment

1. The emphasis in the opening verses of 1 John falls on “the word of life” (v 1) which the writer is proclaiming. In a long and grammatically complicated sentence, John announces his theme by stating first the object of his proclamation (ὃ ἦν ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς, “what was there from the beginning”), which he later defines as “(concerning) the word of life” (περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς). Not until v 3 does he complete the syntax of his sentence by supplying the subject and verb (ἀπαγγέλλομεν, “we are proclaiming”). Most English translations of John’s preface, including our own, attempt to unravel this “grammatical tangle” (Dodd, 3) by creating separate sentences out of the single period. But, despite its cumbrous construction, the important effect of the Gr. is to underscore the nature of the “object which is proclaimed” (the “word”), rather than “the activity of proclaiming it” (Marshall, 100).

ὃ ἦν ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς . . . περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς. A major problem in the opening verse of 1 John is the meaning of the ambiguous phrase, “(concerning) the word of life” (περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς). (The genitive τῆς ζωῆς [“of life”] may indicate both the content and the gift of the word, as Stott, 60, suggests; for the concept of “life” in John see further the comment on v 2.) Does this refer to Jesus himself, as the living Word (Logos) of God (cf. John 1:14), or to the life-giving message, the “word” (logos) preached about him? (The earliest extant NT manuscripts, being written in capital letters throughout, make it impossible for us to tell, in cases like this, whether the original writer intended to give an “upper” or “lower” case significance to technical terms such as [Λ]λόγος.) On the solution to this problem depends the meaning of the whole passage, 1 John 1:1–4. Thus, although it is the final phrase in the verse, we shall consider it first.

περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς. The preface to 1 John contains obvious reflections of the introduction to the Fourth Gospel; and (against Robinson, Redating, 307) this suggests the likelihood that the Gospel in its finished form, including the prologue, was written before 1 John. Note in v 1 the use of ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς (“from the beginning”; cf. John 1:1, ἐν ἀρόῆ, “in the beginning”); and περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς (cf. John 1:1, ὁ λόγος, “the word”). Note also the thought of “life revealed” (1 John 1:2 =John 1:4, 9), and of the Incarnation as an historical reality demanding response (1 John 1:1–3 = John 1:9–14). If the opening verses of 1 John are intended to echo the introduction to John’s Gospel, then it may be claimed that the “Logos of life” is personal in reference, and ultimately describes Jesus himself (so Haas, Handbook, 23–24; Schnackenburg, 60–61). In this case the background to the term λόγος, in both the Gospel and 1 John, is Jewish (cf. Gen 1; Ps 33:6; Prov 8:22–31; Sir 24) as well as Greek (Stoic; cf. also Philo, Spec. Leg. 4.14; De Opific. 26). See further Dodd, 3–4. The first clause in 1 John 1:1 will then refer to the pre-existent Logos, the following three clauses “to the incarnate Logos, that is to say ‘the Jesus of history,’ who, as a Man among men, could be seen, heard and touched” (Dodd, 1–2). Similarly, when the writer claims here that “the eternal life which existed with the Father has been revealed to us” (v 2), it could be argued that he is saying virtually what is expressed in the prologue to the Fourth Gospel: that the creative Logos, who was with God in the beginning, and who is both light and life, tabernacled among us in the Incarnation (John 1:1–14, especially 9–13). In this way God’s being was finally revealed, and his will was decisively expressed; heaven and earth were uniquely brought together.

But it is not absolutely certain that the writer of 1 John is modeling his preface on the prologue to the Gospel, and that λόγος in v 1 must be construed in this way. Westcott (6–7) and Dodd (4–5), for example, argue that the reference in this passage is to the gospel, of which Jesus is the center; and they base this interpretation both on their understanding of the context (where it is the “life,” and not the “word,” which is said to have been revealed, v 2), and on the parallel uses of the expression “word of life” (meaning “gospel”) elsewhere in the NT (e.g. Phil 2:16; Acts 5:20; although, as Dodd, 5, admits, “Pauline and Lucan usage is not decisive for a Johannine passage”).

However, these two interpretations of) λόγος need not be opposed to each other; and possibly there is an intentional ambivalence at this point. For the life-giving word of the gospel is essentially a proclamation about Jesus who is the living Word of God. “Jesus gave the word and embodied it” (Houlden, 52; cf. Westcott 7; Marshall 103). In any case, the eventual focus here is on Jesus himself, more than on the witnesses to him (le Fort, Structures, 59–60). See further the debate on the meaning of “word” in this passage between Weir (ExpTim 86 [1974/75] 118–20) and Grayston (ExpTim 86 [1974/75] 279); cf. also Dunn, Christology, 245–46.

A further problem surrounding the phrase περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς is its relation to the preceding clauses in v 1. It may be taken closely with these, to mean that the writer is declaring what he has heard, seen and felt in respect of “the word of life” (so Marshall, 102 n. 11). However, this brings four neuter relative pronouns into apposition with the masculine λόγος and overlooks the change of construction from relative clauses to the use of the preposition περί (literally, “concerning”). Alternatively the phrase may be construed as an independent, coordinate clause, which supplies an additional object to the verb “declare” (v 3; for ἀπαγγειλλομεν περί, literally, “we are declaring about,” see John 16:25). In this case John is defining further the area with which the things heard, seen and felt are concerned (cf. Law, Tests, 368–69). However, as Houlden (50) points out, the difference between these two constructions (and the interpretations to which they give rise) is not very significant. Our translation attempts to lean in the direction of the former.

ὃ ἦν ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς. The clause at the start of v 3, ὃ ἑωράκαμεν καὶ ἀκηκόαμεν ἀπαγγέλλομεν καὶ ὑμῖν (“what we have seen and heard we are declaring to you as well”), resumes the structure, and to some extent the language, of this verse (ὃ ἀκηκόαμεν, ὃ ἑωράκαμεν, “which we have heard/seen”), showing that the relatives in the two verses are identical in meaning. Thus the presence of the neuter pronoun in v 3 (“what we have seen and heard”) means that the relative pronoun ὃ here must be interpreted in a similarly impersonal way (“that which was,” not “he who was”). This may suggest that, even if John has the person of Jesus at the back of his mind here (see above), the stress at the opening of this verse is on the message about Jesus.

The significance of the phrase “what was there from the beginning” is partly determined by the way in which “the word (λόγος) of life,” at the conclusion of this verse, is understood (see the comment above); although, as Marshall (102 n. 10) says, the antecedent of the relative clauses which introduce v 1 need not be directly identified with the phrase περὶ τοῦ λόγου τῆς ζωῆς. If an impersonal reference is maintained (the “word” as Christian preaching), the sense of ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς (“from the beginning”) becomes purely temporal, and indeed local. The author is then speaking about the “beginning” of the gospel, in terms of both its content (the ministry of Jesus) and its proclamation (the witness of the disciples). Cf. 2:7, 24; 3:11. This interpretation is adopted by Wendt, ZNW 21 (1922) 38–42; cf. also Dunn, Christology, 245–46.

However, a personal reference (the “word” of God as ultimately disclosed in Jesus) need not be excluded from this context. This is confirmed by the absence of the article with ἀρχή, as in John 1:1 (a stylistic feature which seems, in both cases, to be influenced by the LXX of Gen 1:1). If, as in the prologue to the Fourth Gospel, the reference of λόγος here is personal (and in any case the “message” concerns the “person” of Jesus), John is claiming that the Christian revelation is “coeval in some sense with creation” (Westcott, 5; cf. Brooke, 1–2; note also 2:13–14). Moreover, the allusion is possibly to the pre-existent Word in eternity itself, rather than to the “beginning” of creation as such. For, unlike John 1:3, there is no mention of the creation in the preface to 1 John; and ἀπνμδ᾽ ἀρχῆς (“from the beginning”) here differs from ἐν ἀρχῇ (“in the beginning”) at John 1:1 (so Marshall, 100 n. 5; cf. also Conzelmann, “ ‘Was von Anfang War,’ ” 207–14; Schnackenburg, 59).

If this verse includes a reference to the pre-existent Word of God, it means that John begins his letter by introducing a christology which is deliberately and insistently “high.” He views Jesus, on this showing, as one with God (cf. John 10:30; 13:3), and not only as one with man (cf. John 14:28). In this case the writer may be attempting to balance the “low” christology of his ex-Jewish readers, just as elsewhere he is resisting the “high” christology of his ex-pagan church members (see further above, xxvi–viii, for a discussion of the intention behind 1 John).

ὃ ἀκηκόαμεν . . . ἐψηλάφησαν (“we have heard . . . we felt [with our hands]”). Perhaps for the benefit of those of his readers who were entertaining docetic (i.e. humanity-denying) views of Christ’s person, the writer stresses the reality of God’s self-disclosure in time and space. In so doing John moves beyond the concept of the life-giving word about Jesus, to the Word of God disclosed in Jesus (the life-giver) himself. For the reference of these four evocative verbs (“we have heard, seen with our eyes, observed, and felt with our hands”) is very practical. In a typically Johannine manner, the writer speaks of the Christian revelation as a unique conjunction of the timeless and the historical. What was true from eternity (“there from the beginning”) was gradually and actually disclosed, and personally experienced, in history (note the imperfect ἦς, “was,” a “past continuous” tense, followed by a succession of two verbs in the perfect, a tense denoting some past action with present implications; the change to the aorist in the final two verbs need not be significant).

The verbs in the first two clauses (“which we have heard,” “which we have seen”) are significant, since “hearing” and especially “sight” are ideas which are close to “faith” in the Fourth Gospel (cf. John 10:27; 20:29; see further Smalley, John, 139–40). In this verse a similar ambivalence may be associated with ἀκηκόαμεν and ἑωράκαμεν. John may thus be saying that the truths which prepared the way for the gospel were finally realized in Jesus (cf. Heb 1:1–2), when the “word of life” could be “heard” in his preaching and “seen” in his ministry by those who believed.

The next two clause (ὃ ἐθεασάμεθα κὰ αἱ χεῖρες ἡμῶν ἐψηάφησαν, “which we have observed, and felt with our hands”) may also be taken together, since they balance the previous pair. The use of the verb θεάομαι (ὃ ἐθεασάμεθα, “which we have observed”), following ὁράω (ὃ ἑωράκαμεν, “which we have seen”), probably represents a literary variation (so Schnackenburg, 60), rather than a difference in meaning (Westcott, 6, “calm contemplation”; Brooke, 4, “intelligent beholding”). In either case the writer repeats the typically Johannine notion of “sight” as being important for both history (the incarnate Word was visible) and faith (we are called to believe in him).

The words καὶ αἱ χεῖρες ἡμῶν ἐψηλάφησαν (“and felt with our hands”) possibly connect with the tradition behind Luke 24:39 (the risen Jesus says, “Touch me and see,” using the aorist ψηλαφήσατε, “touch”); although interestingly the resurrection is not otherwise mentioned in 1 John. They also make the final reference to the Incarnation in this verse a climactic and intensely personal one. As Houlden (53) says, for the fourth evangelist, as for this writer, “touching is somehow closely linked with awareness of the risen Christ.” (See also John 20:17, “do not hold on to me”; and vv 25–27, “reach out your hand and put it into my side.”)

The use of the first person plural (“we”) in the verbs which appear in the middle four clauses of this verse may indicate the presence of eyewitnesses (cf. Stott, 61–63; Marshall, 106–107). Equally, here and in the preface generally, “we” may be interpreted to mean the Church in solidarity with eyewitnesses (so Dodd, 9–16). Without excluding the possibility that eyewitnesses were associated with John’s testimony, and were thus able to support it, the writer is more likely to be taking the “mantle of orthodoxy” (Houlden, 53), and speaking for all those, including members of the Johannine community, who were champions of the apostolic gospel. Against the heretics in his community who were espousing false or inadequate views of the person of Jesus (he was not fully God, or he was not really a human being; see the Introduction, xxiii–v), John is recalling his readers to the fundamentals of the Christian faith (in terms of christology, Jesus is both divine and human), and identifying himself with those who believe as he does (“we have really witnessed the historical origins of the apostolic message of life in Christ”). Cf. John 21:24.

See further the discussion on the sense and significance of the “witness” sayings in the preface by Schnackenburg, 52–58. John tends to use the first person plural in this document when associating himself with the guardians of the apostolic faith (including eyewitnesses of Jesus), and handing on the gospel to others, or when he is identifying himself with his readers in terms of basic Christian experience (cf. vv 1–2; 2:1b). Similarly he often uses the first person singular in passages of direct address or exhortation (e.g. 2:1a).

2. This verse is a parenthesis, which breaks the syntactical construction of the preface and expands the content of v 1. It begins with a broad theological statement, couched in characteristically Johannine form (“the life was revealed”; cf. John 1:4.) The conjunction καί (“and”) is used to introduce this assertion, and the three clauses which follow. This paratactic style is common in 1 John (cf. vv 3, 5; 2:27: 3:19–24), and is reminiscent of the style of many OT authors. Such a literary feature may support the view that the fundamental ethos of the Johannine church, for which this document came to birth, was Jewish-Christian (see above, xxv).

καί ἡ ζωὴ ἐφανερώθη (“the life was revealed”). The term ζωὴ (“lift”) may refer to Jesus himself (as in the Fourth Gospel; cf. John 11:25; 14:6), in which case the writer is claiming that “Jesus was revealed.” But as Westcott (8) points out, the term “life” is not to be regarded as a personal name exactly equivalent to “Jesus,” or to λόγος (“word”) as used in association with Jesus (cf. John 1:14). Rather, “life” expresses one aspect of his being and activity. And in any case the clause at the end of this verse (“we are proclaiming to you the eternal life . . . revealed to us”) perhaps suggests that ζωὴ is in both cases to be interpreted, as in v 1, with reference not only to Jesus himself but also to the preaching about him.

In the NT the verb φανεροῦν (“to manifest,” “to reveal”) is used of Jesus in relation to his earthly ministry (1 John 3:5, 8; cf. John 1:31); his resurrection appearances (John 21:1, 14); and his parousia in glory (1 John 2:28; cf. Col 3:4; 1 Pet 5:4). Insofar as John is here speaking of Christ himself, he is obviously alluding to the Incarnation. The life which is God’s gift to man was revealed historically in Jesus. Significantly, the motif of revelation is central to the theology of the Fourth Gospel, and indeed forms the subject of its first “act” (John 1–12; see 1:31; 2:11; but also 21:1–14). cf. further Smalley, John, 194–200.

Both concepts present in John’s leading affirmation, “the life was revealed,” are developed in the closing sentence of this verse.

καὶ ἐωράκαμεν . . . ὑμῖν. The verbs ἑωράκαμεν, “we have seen” (cf. vv 1 and 3; also John 19:35), μαρτυροῦμεν, “we are bearing witness” (cf. 4:14; also John 21:24) and ἀπαγγέλλομεν, “we are proclaiming” (cf. v 3; also John 16:25) express in order the three ideas of experience, attestation and evangelism which form part of any genuine and lasting response to the gospel. Using again the theologically significant notion of “seeing,” as in v 1, with a similarly “personal” implication, John stresses the reality of God’s revelation in Christ. The divine life was historically manifested and completely visible. Perhaps John is at this point conscious of the need to resist the gnostic inclinations of some members in his congregation who were denying the historical actuality of the Incarnation.

Personal encounter with the living and historical Word of God (“we have seen it”) should be followed by the believer’s testimony to that Word (“we are bearing witness”). Such attestation may lead to death; for witness (μαρτυροῦμεν) and martyrdom (μαρτυρία) have been associated since the earliest days of the Christian Church. The act of testifying to Jesus may in turn, and depending on the Spirit’s gifts to the individual believer, be followed by explicit evangelism (“we are proclaiming eternal life to you”). John may have separated these two stages of spiritual experience, witness and proclamation, in order to indicate that they are not necessarily identical. What the Christian is may, in the end, be more important than what he says.

τὴν ζωὴν τὴν αἰώνιον . . . καὶ ἐφανερώθη ἡμῖν. In this final phrase John picks up and develops the thought (ἡ ζωὴ ἐφανερώθν, “the life was revealed”) with which the verse began.

First, he explains “life” as “eternal life” (τῆν ζωὴν τὴν αἰώνιον, the literal meaning of which—“the life, the eternal [life]”—indicates the forceful way in which the writer is describing the object of his important “proclamation” to the Johannine church). “Eternal life” is a spiritual quality of life, which God gives to every believer through Jesus his Son (cf. John 3:16; 17:2–3). It is not to be thought of as life prolonged to infinity, but as a sharing of living fellowship with the Father, in which “the category of time recedes before that of moral quality” (Law, Tests, 189). In the words of Maurice (27–28; cf. 159–60), if eternal life is “that which was manifested in Christ, in His words and acts, it is a life of gentleness, justice, truth”; and you “cannot measure these by the clocks.” In Johannine terms, “eternal life” is a synonym for “salvation.” (For ζωὴ αἰώνιος, “eternal life,” see further the comment on 2:25.)

John goes on to claim that the ultimate source of life, whether this is mediated through Jesus himself or through the preaching about him, is to be found “with the Father” (πρὸς τὸν πατέρα). As a title of God, “Father” is typically Johannine (cf. v 3; 2:1, 14, 15, 16, 22–24; 3:1; 4:14; also John 1:18; 17:1–26; 20:17, using πρὸς τὸν πατέρα). See further Westcott, 27–34; Bultmann, 47 n. 14; also Smalley, SE VI (1973) 499–500.

According to John 1:1 the Word (λόγος) was also “with God” (πρὸς τὸν θεόν). Thus it could be argued that, by speaking of eternal life as “existing with the Father,” John is not only referring to the exalted content of the apostolic proclamation, but also making a high christological affirmation by alluding to the pre-existence of Christ (see also the comment on ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς, “from the beginning,” in v 1). Indeed, the christological reference is almost certainly primary, in view of the qualitative relative ἣτις(ἦν πρός τὸν παρέρα), “which (existed with the Father),” and the force of the imperfect ἦν, “was” (note the connections with v 1, ὃ ἦν ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς, “what was there from the beginning,” and John 1:1, “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God,” where the Gr. has ἦν in both sentences). In other words John seems to be saying: “we are proclaiming to you the life which is indeed eternal, seeing that this life was (not “is”) with the Father (pre-existently).” There is also a clear theological balance between the subsequent statement that the eternal life which existed with the Father “has been revealed to us,” and the declaration in the Johannine prologue, “the Word, which was with God, became flesh and lived for a while among us” (John 1:1, 14). John’s lofty christology at this point may be intended to remind those of his readers who saw Jesus as human, and little more (namely, those with a Jewish background), that he was also divine (see above, xxvi).

By claiming that “the eternal life which existed with the Father has been revealed “to us” (ἡμῖν), the writer is expanding the second half of his original statement in this verse, “the life was revealed” (for ἐφανερώθη, “has been revealed,” see the earlier comment on the same verb). The revelation of God centered in Jesus is not only fully historical (a truth which John’s gnostically inclined readers were denying), but also challengingly personal. In Jesus Christ God’s saving life has been disclosed, and made available, to the whole of creation for all time (cf. 2:2).

For the tension between the “anti-gnostic” and “anti-Jewish” thrusts in the theology of the preface to 1 John (and elsewhere in this document) see further Smalley, “What about 1 John?” 337–43; cf. also above, xxvi.

3. The sentence which began in v 1, and was interrupted by the parenthesis of v 2, is now resumed and concluded. John recalls the objective character of the authoritative message which he is “declaring” to his readers; and he announces that its immediate purpose is fellowship (both human and divine, v 3), while its ultimate intention is joy (v 4). cf. Stott, 63.

ὃ ῥωράκαμεν καὶ ἀκηκόαμεν ἀπαγγέλλομεν καὶ ὑμῖν. Throughout the preface (vv 1–4) a close relation exists between the two meanings of the “word of lift” (v 1): Jesus as the life–giver, and the gospel message about him. The neuter pronoun ὃ (literally, “that which”) in the present verse, as in v 1, suggests that the initial reference of ὃ ἐωράκαμεν καὶ ἀκηκοαμεν, “what we have seen and heard,” is impersonal (“we are declaring what we have seen and heard”; cf. the similar phrase in v 1). However, the use of the verb ὁράω (“see”) indicates that John is also referring to the Incarnation, when the truth of God was made plain in Jesus for all to see (and hear).

For the two verbs ἑωράκαμεν, and ἀκηκόαμεν, repeated from v 1 without the second relative ὃ (“what”), see the comments on vv 1 and 2. The fact that the writer now places these verbs in the reverse order from that in v 1 may indicate a simple desire for literary variation (so Haas, Handbook, 27, who notes the sequence “see/bear witness” in v 2, and adds that “what we have seen” may have been mentioned first because it best fits the directly preceding verb ἐφανερώθη, “revealed”). However, a more theological reason for John’s arrangement can be suggested. In v 1 the writer may have been leading up to the Incarnation, whereas here he is starting from that point (so Westcott, 11). That is to say, the “sight” of Jesus, who embodies the word of life, is of first importance.

In either case John is clarifying his message for the benefit of his readers. In v 2 he describes the essence of his proclamation as “eternal life”; now he characterizes it as “what we have seen and heard.” In the face of heretical challenges, he insists on “the historical reality of the incarnation” (Dodd, 6), and “announces” as an envoy (the literal meaning of the verb ἀπαγγέλλω, here and in v 2), who is in possession of an authoritative message from the Lord (cf. ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ, “from him,” in v 5), that to know Jesus is to receive the life which God gives through him.

This is the heart of the apostolic gospel, to which (throughout this letter) the members of John’s community are being recalled. For the revelation of God in Christ concerns those (including the readers of 1 John; hence the force of καὶ ὑμῖν, “you as well”) who may not have “seen” the earthly and risen Jesus, as well as those who (like Thomas) had “seen” him, and believed in his divine lordship (cf. John 20:29).

ἵνα καὶ ὑμεῖς κοινωνίαν ἔχητε μεθʼ ἡμῶν. In the second part of this verse John moves on to declare his purpose in writing. It is that his congregation, divided perhaps in their estimate of the person of Jesus (see above, 7–8), may share continuously (ἔχητε, literally, “you may have,” is in the present tense) a “fellowship” which is both Christian in basis and apostolic in character (cf. vv 6–7). The practical dimension of John’s theology is evident here, as elsewhere in this document. Knowledge of Jesus is to be followed by action (cf. 2:3). Here the result of “seeing” Christ is a declaration about him (vv 2–3), and an invitation to have fellowship in him.

“Fellowship” (κοινωνία) is a richly significant theological term. The Gr. word literally means “joint ownership,” or “partnership.” In the NT this “mutual sharing” may refer to participation in either material goods, as when Christians in Macedonia and Achaia “raised a common fund” (κοινωνίαν τινὰ ποιήσασθαι) for the poor among the saints in Jerusalem (Rom 15:26); or in spiritual benefits, as when Paul speaks of “sharing” (being συγκοινωνός) in the blessings of the gospel (1 Cor 9:23), or enjoying—in the words of “the Grace”—the “fellowship of the Spirit” (2 Cor 13:13; cf. also Rom 15:27).

In either sense, the particular “sharing” of Christians, who relate to each other as branches to the true Vine (John 15:1–6), derives from a common faith in Jesus: “we are in him who is true, even in his Son Jesus Christ” (1 John 5:20). Christian fellowship is not the sentimental and superficial attachment of a random collection of individuals, but the profoundly mutual relationship of those who remain “in Christ,” and therefore belong to each other (cf. 3:23–24). Just how deep this fellowship can be is indicated by the Gr. in the present section of v 3. For the writer uses the phrase κοινωνίαν ἔχειν, “to have fellowship,” not the verb κοινωνεῖν (“to fellowship,” see 2 John 11); and this expresses not only the fact, but also the conscious enjoyment, of fellowship in Christ. The preposition μετά (μεθʼ ἡμῶν), “with (us),” emphasizes the deeply reciprocal spiritual relationship which can exist between one Christian and another.

This letter was written to promote such fellowship among those Christians in John’s church who, like their leaders, were champions of orthodox belief (see above, xxviii). 1 John is thus an invitation to “share our fellowship.” For the use of the first person plural to mean the (true) Church in general see 4:13–14 and the comment on v 1.

On “fellowship” (κοινωνία) see further Dodd, 6–9; also J. Schattenmann, NIDNTT 1 (1975) 644.

καὶ ἡ κοινωνία δὲ ἡ ἠμετέρα μετὰ τοῦ πατρός, “a fellowship which we have with the Father.” The καὶ . . . δέ (literally, “and . . . but”) construction is emphatic, implying some such thought as “moreover” (cf. Moule, Idiom-Book, 165); see 3 John 12. The possessive form ἡ κοινωνία . . . ἡ ἡμετέρα (“our fellowship”), which displaces the expected personal pronoun ἡμῶν, “our” (cf. John 15:9), is equally intensive. The opening words of the sentence may thus be paraphrased as follows: “moreover, the particular fellowship to which you are being called is one that belongs to us; and it is therefore both Christian and apostolic.”

In the preceding phrase of this v John has spoken of fellowship among Christians: a sharing which exists on the human level, even if it derives from a mutual indwelling in Christ. Now the writer describes the nature of Christian fellowship in terms of its divine origin and operation. It is “a fellowship which we have with the Father”; “with the Church and with God” (Westcott, 11, italics added). See the similar thought at John 17:21. For the characteristically Johannine title πατήρ (“Father”) see the comment on v 2.

καὶ μετὰ τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ (“and with his Son Jesus Christ”). The name of Jesus, implicit from v 1 onward, is at last specified. Christian fellowship is essentially a sharing “with the Father, and with his Son Jesus Christ.” (The fact that the third person of the Trinity is not mentioned at this point is perhaps characteristic of the restrained reference to the Spirit in 1 John; see above, xxviii.)

With the mention of Jesus, the thought which was initiated in v 1 and developed in v 2 reaches its conclusion. It is possible, John claims, to receive eternal life and be in communion with its source because the Incarnation has taken place: “the life was revealed” (v 2). Through the Son, God is made known as Father. Moreover, the knowledge of God in the incarnate life of Christ (cf. John 17:3) is the common basis of both ideas of “fellowship” described in this verse, the human and divine (so Law, Tests, 371). The choice of “Son,” as a title for Jesus, is no doubt influenced by the use of “Father” as a designation for God (cf. v 2; also 2:22–24). Both terms reflect the distinctive “Father-Son” relationship between God and Jesus which is characterized by the fourth evangelist (John 5:21–27; 10:36, 17:1; cf. further Smalley, John, 210–19). On “Son” see further the comment on 3:8.

A high christology is present in this part of v 3. Jesus is regarded as one (in being and function) with the Father, so that fellowship “with the Father” and participation in “his Son Jesus Christ” are treated as parallel and synonymous ideas (hence the Gr. formulation μετὰ . . . καὶ μετὰ, “with . . . and with”). As Jesus revealed God, moreover (vv 1–3), so he made fellowship with the Father finally possible. John’s use of the full title, “his Son Jesus Christ,” nevertheless points to the human as well as the divine status of the Lord. He was, as typically in Johannine thought, one with man (Jesus, who was “heard, seen, observed, felt,” vv 1–3); and also one with God (his Son, the Messiah, who “from the beginning existed with the Father,” vv 1–2). The form “Jesus Christ” is less common in the Fourth Gospel (1:17 and 17:3 only; but cf. 20:31) than in 1 John (2:1; 3:23; 4:2; 5:6, 20, depending on the MS reading; note also 2 John 3,7).

It is possible that the contrasting but complementary estimates of Jesus in this verse (seen and heard on the human level; the divine mediator of life and fellowship with God; the man of Nazareth and the messianic Son of the Father) were introduced by John in opposition to the two heretical dispositions which may have existed among his readers: docetic on the one hand (regarding Jesus as less than man), and “humanizing” on the other (understanding Christ as less than God). See the comment on v 2; cf. also above, xxiii–vi.

4. John has already stated (v 3) that his purpose in writing is the encouragement of a deeply shared fellowship. In this verse he adds a further objective: “in order that our joy may be complete!” The author’s pastoral concern for the members of his congregation is evident in all parts of this letter. It shines through this verse, and through the preface (1:1–4) as a whole.

καὶ ταῦτα γράφομεν ἡμεῖς (“and we are writing this”). For the enclitic καὶ (“and”) see the note about John’s paratactic style in the comment on v 2. The phrase ταῦτα γράφομεν ἡμεῖς, with its repeated subject in the verb and the pronoun, is deliberately emphatic: “we (the writer, in solidarity with all the representatives of orthodoxy in the church) are writing this.” Elsewhere in 1 John the verb γράφειν (“to write”) appears in the singular (e.g. 2:1, 7–8). In contrast to the solemn, joint proclamation of the apostolic message in the preface, the subsequent use of γράφειν is always in the context of an intimate and personal address.

To what does ταῦτα (literally “these things”) relate? Some commentators understand vv 1–3 as a general reference to John’s spoken or written proclamation of the Christian gospel, and v 4 (“we are writing this”) as the introduction to 1 John itself (so Law, Tests, 371; cf. Brooke, 9, who notes that in this document the demonstrative οὖτος, “this,” and its cognates, more often points forward to what is about to be written, rather than backward to what has just been said; cf. 2:3, 3:23). However, other exegetes interpret as an allusion to the particular statements made in the preface itself (vv 1–3) about the apostolic proclamation. So Stott (64–65), who notes that the final clause in this verse, introduced by ἴνα (“that”) and depending on γράφομεν (“we are writing”), is parallel to the ἴνα clause in v 3, which depends on ἀπαγγέλλομεν (“we are declaring”). As a result, he claims, “declaring” (v 3) and “writing” (v 4) refer to “the same message” (65). Stott also points out, rightly, that demonstratives in 1 John can also look back to what has already been said (2:26; 5:13, both using ταῦτα ἔγραψα, “I have written these things”).

Nevertheless, a simpler explanation is possible: that ταῦτα does not refer either to what directly precedes or to what follows, but to the content of 1 John as a whole (cf. Haas, Handbook, 28). In vv 1–3 John has described the Christian proclamation about Jesus as the embodiment of the word of life. This is now being expressed in written, and therefore permanent, form in the present document (“we are writing this”). The author is the representative of those who are indebted for their faith to the apostolic kerygma, and therefore he possesses the authority to write these things (ταῦτα γράφομεν ἡμεῖς).

ἴνα ἡ χαρὰ ἡμῶν ἦ πεπληρωμένη (“in order that our joy may be complete”). This clause echoes the words of Jesus in John 15:11 (“I have told you this so that my joy may be in you and that your joy may be complete,” using ἡ χαρὰ ὑμῶν πληρωθῇ). Cf. John 16:24 (“Ask and you will receive, and your joy will be complete,” using ἡ χαρὰ ὑμῶν ᾖ πεπληρωμένη). The writer of 1 John regularly reflects the teaching of the Fourth Gospel (e.g. 3:23); and it is possible that on occasions he cites the Gospel in order to refute some within the Johannine church who were using the teaching of the fourth evangelist to support their own heretical position (see the comments on 2:4, 6, 9; cf. also above, xxvi–xxx).

The intended result of both the spoken and written good news about Jesus, which forms the basis of John’s composition, is thus “joy.” It is a joy which springs from fellowship with him (v 3; cf. John 20:20), forms part of the fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22), and should characterize the fellowship of all Christian disciples (Acts 13:52; cf. Phil 2:2).

An eschatological dimension may also belong to the desire for “complete” joy mentioned in this verse (ᾖ πεπληρωμένη; Marshall, 105 n. 20, points out that this verb includes the note of “fullness,” unlike the cognate τελειόω, “I perfect,” which suggests “the attainment of an end”). “Fullness of joy” is a familiar idea in the Johannine literature (cf. John 3:29; 4:36; 15:11; 16:24; 17:13; see also 2 John 12), where it is regularly associated with the notion of fellowship with God or with other believers. And since “perfect” fellowship is impossible outside heaven (as perhaps the “resolved” perfect passive πεπληρωμένη, literally, “completed,” indicates; cf. John 16:24), the thought in this verse probably looks beyond this life to the “eternal life” (v 2) of heaven, when “consummated fellowship will bring completed joy” (Stott, 66). In this case John’s preface has, in four verses, brought us from eternity (v 1) to eternity (v 4).

Nevertheless, the desire for “complete joy,” like the longing for fellowship voiced in v 3, is related to a specific and historical situation. As always, the writer’s concerns are immediate and intensely practical. The Johannine community appears to be threatened with disintegration on account of doctrinal differences; and John may well be claiming that “our joy” (or the joy of the recipients, if we adopt the variant reading) will initially be completed only when mutual fellowship in Christ among the members of the circle is restored. Then, and only then, the church will begin to “enter into the joy of its Lord,” according to his word (cf. Dodd, 9; see further Malatesta, Interiority, 77–90). On “joy” (χαρά) cf. E. Beyreuther and G. Finkenrath, NIDNTT 2 (1976) 359.
Explanation

The main subject of the preface to 1 John, as of the letter as a whole, is “the word of life”: embodied in Jesus, and proclaimed about him. With this theme the document begins (1:1) and ends (5:13, 20). The writer’s overarching purpose is the very practical one of recalling his divided community to the fundamentals of the apostolic gospel, and answering the heretical extremes of those who were taking too “high” or too “low” a view of the person of Jesus by setting out a perfectly balanced christology.

The starting point is proclamation, however, and not apologetic; it is theology, not ethics. To be sure, these belong together: Christian conduct springs from Christian belief. But here, in the preface, John begins with a positive and uncompromising declaration of the life-giving gospel about Jesus, and confronts his heterodox readers at the outset with the very truth they were busy denying. As a result we find, both in the preface and in 1 John generally, a Christian manifesto which was relevant to the situation of the Johannine church, and which is also of timeless significance.

For the gospel to which 1 John bears witness so firmly, and in such exalted tones, is the good news of a God who had disclosed himself uniquely in the historical person of Jesus (vv 1–3). It is a message of eternal life available through him in the present (v 2); of fellowship both divine and human (v 3); and of complete joy (v 4). Above all, it is a testimony which demands personal acceptance and experience (vv 1–4).

It might be thought that the readers of this letter, who are urged so strongly in the preface to heed and respond to the Christian gospel, were not in fact Christians, but outsiders. However (despite 2:19), the letter itself eventually makes it clear that the intended audience consists of believers (5:13; cf. John 20:31, where one variant in the Gr. allows the interpretation that the Gospel was also written for believers, to clarify and reinforce their existing faith). In our view, the key to understanding these vv of the preface, and indeed 1 John in its entirety, lies in the suggestion that the believers in question were in danger of succumbing to heterodox tendencies; they were leaning in the direction of a christology which was either too strong or too weak in character (see above, xxiii).

a. God Is Light (1:5–7)

Translation

5Now this is the newsa which we have heard from him, and are proclaiming to you: God is light, and there is no darkness of any kind in him. 6If we claim to share fellowship with him, while living in darkness, we are lying and not practicing the truth. 7But if we live in the light as he himself is in the light, then we share fellowship with each other;b and the blood of Jesus his Son purifies us from every sin.
Notes

a. ἡ ἀγγελία (“the news”) is read by the major witnesses; but some MSS (e.g. C) read ἡ ἐπαγγελία (“the promise”). The context, however, demands the former reading; for the statement “God is light” provides the content of a proclamation (see v 3), rather than a prophecy.

b. Most MSS read μετʼ ἀλλήλων (“with each other”); but some (including A*) have μετʼ αὐτοῦ (“with him”). It is arguable that the weakly attested reading (“fellowship with him”) should be accepted, because this passage is otherwise concerned exclusively with the relationship between men and God, not men and other Christians; and also because a scribe is unlikely to have impoverished the theological level of the verse by changing αὐτοῦ to ἀλλήκων (so O’Neill, Puzzle, 8–10). On the other hand, it is a typical literary characteristic of this writer to advance his thought step by step, rather than repeating his ideas in exactly the same form (cf. 2:12–14). Thus, it is being claimed, fellowship with God (vv 6, 7a) can be realized and reproduced in fellowship with God’s people (so Brooke, 15; cf. Schnackenburg, 71). This consideration has prompted us to adopt the reading which is supported by the major witnesses (“fellowship with each other”).
Form/Structure/Setting

The teaching of 1 John is concerned essentially with the conditions for true Christian discipleship. The two main divisions of the letter set out these conditions and exhort the readers to live in the light (1:5—2:29) as children of God (3:1—5:13). See further the outline of 1 John in the introduction (xxxiii–iv).

After the preface (1:1–4) the writer launches forth into his first major section. Under the general heading of “living in the light” he deals first with the subject of God as light (1:5–7), and then with four conditions for living as a believer (illumined and illumining) in the world (1:8—2:29). Cf. the parallel structure in 3:1—5:13, where “God is Father” (3:1–3) balances “God is light” here. The reason for subdividing the material in 1 John as we do is entirely thematic; and this accounts for the treatment of 1:5–7 here as a separate unit.

This section contains a statement of the good news: “God is light, and there is no darkness of any kind in him.” This is followed by two vv (6, 7) which present an evenly balanced, if not entirely antithetical, structure. These vv may be regarded as “more than heightened prose” (O’Neill, Puzzle, 8); and it is possible, as a result, to arrange them in quasi-poetic form. (In this case the ends of the lines fall where punctuation marks occur in our translation.) Such an arrangement, if it were in the writer’s mind, may reflect to some extent the Semitic literary idiom of parallelismus membrorum (“parallelism of members”); that is, couplets in which the second line either echoes or reverses the sense of the first (as in the Psalms, and from time to time in the teaching of Jesus; cf. Mark 4:30 = Luke 13:18; Matt 6:14–15).

The setting of 1:5–7, with its basic theme of “light versus darkness,” may appear at first glance to be Hellenistic, and indeed gnostic. But the ideas of “light” and “darkness” are also common in Judaism (both in the OT and in the Qumran documents). See further below. If, as we assume, the primary background of the Johannine tradition (and of the community associated with that tradition) was Jewish-Palestinian (see the introduction, xxv), it is not unreasonable to identify the initial background to which the writer was indebted here as similarly Judaistic in character. But the very fact that the “light/darkness” theme could be appreciated by readers who had emerged from both a Jewish and Greek environment supports our suggestion that John was writing with the needs of both in mind (see further the introduction, xxiii).
Comment

5. John’s concerns, as we have already seen from the preface to this document (vv 3b–4), are intensely practical, as well as theological. He is confronting a community which is divided in its opinions about Jesus, and seriously inclined to heterodoxy, with a message concerning the life and unity which may be found in God through Jesus. On this basis all the members of the Johannine circle are challenged to “live in the light”—to examine their faith, and to practice it.

The section begins, in this v, with an affirmation. The conditions, both negative and positive, for proper Christian living will be discussed in due course (1:8—5:11). Meanwhile John makes a bold and summary statement which takes his audience back to the fundamental basis of all Christian belief and experience. He reminds them of the nature of God himself, and of the truth that “God is light.”

καὶ ἔστιν αὓτη ἡ ἀλλελία . . . ἀναγγέλλομεν ὑμῖν. The first v of this section is associated with the preceding paragraph by the use of similar language, which was perhaps well-known in the Johannine community. Thus the verb ἀπαγγέλλομεν (“we are declaring”) in vv 2 and 3 is picked up here by the similar verb ἀναγγέλλομεν (“we are proclaiming”), and by the noun ἡ ἀγγελία (“the news”).

The formula καὶ ἔστιν αὔτη (literally, “and this is”) points forward to the statement, prefaced by ὄτι (“that”), in the second part of this v (“God is light”). Such a formula occurs frequently in the letters of John; although it is not always clear whether the reference is retrospective or prospective (cf. 2:25; 3:23; 5:4, 11, 14; 2 John 6; note also the appearance of the formula with a personal predicate in 5:6). Here John is obviously introducing material which follows: the leading theme of his first main division, and its subsequent explication.

The term ἡ ἀγγελία (“the news”) appears in the NT only here and at 3:11. In vv 2 and 3 the writer speaks of the revealed life (of God in Christ) which “we” (associating himself with the orthodox members of the Johannine community, who were committed to the preservation and propagation of the apostolic message; see the comment on 1:1) are “proclaiming” (ἀπαγγέλλομεν). Now, in association with a similarly community witness (ἀναγγέλλομεν, “we are proclaiming”), John declares to “you” (ὑμῖν, the Johannine circle as a whole) the “news” that God is light as well as life. Such a message, in Christian terms, is good news, and the virtual equivalent of “gospel.”

This “news,” it is affirmed, has been “heard from him” (ἀκηκόαμεν ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ). For this verb see the comments on 1:1 and 3. Perhaps for the benefit of some gnostically inclined members of his congregations, John emphasizes the reality of the Incarnation. For those with ears to hear, he implies, the “word of life” (1:1) was audible in the life and teaching of the historical Jesus; and it is this gospel (already outlined in the preface, 1:1–4) which is now being transmitted afresh. It has been heard, says the writer, “from him” (ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ). The use of pronouns in 1 John is not always precise; but the allusion to “hearing,” which in the preface is associated with Jesus, and the fact that the news is said to concern God, suggests that in this context the author of the message is Jesus himself (cf. also John 3:32; so Marshall, 108 n. 1). Even if the description of God as “light” is not found in the teaching of Jesus as reported in the Gospels (but see Matt 6:22–23 = Luke 11:34–36), it is noteworthy that the advent of Jesus is seen by other writers than John as a revelation of light (cf. Matt 4:16; Luke 2:32).

ὂτι ὀ θεός φῶς ἐστιν . . . οὐδεμία (“that God is light” etc.). The background to the image of God as “light” is both Hellenistic and Jewish; indeed, “the categories of light and darkness belong to the universal language of religious symbolism” (Stott, 70). It was, for example, a feature of Zoroastrianism. Gnosticism itself, furthermore, was in effect a “religion of light” (developed in the dualist systems of Manichaeism and Mandaism), in which light and darkness stood over against each other as hostile and independent powers (cf. further H.-C. Hahn, NIDNTT 2 [1976] 490–91). For the Hellenistic sources in general, especially in Plato and Philo, see Dodd, 201–205.

Such associations of the term “light” in Greek thought would clearly have appealed to John’s ex-pagan readers. But the context most readily available to the writer of 1 John was probably Judaism; and the use of the image of light in the OT and Qumran would therefore be appreciated in addition by the ex-Jewish members of his community.

In the OT “light” is used in an intellectual sense to symbolize truth (where “darkness” is error), and in a moral sense to represent righteousness (where “darkness” is evil). So, for example, Ps 119:130; Isa 5:20; Mic 7:8b; see also Ps 27:1. (In the NT cf. Rom 13:11–14; 2 Pet 1:19.) This language is also familiar to us from the literature of Qumran (cf. 1QS 1:5, 9–10; 5:19–21; 1QH 4:5–6; 1QM 13:15; and note the description of God as “perfect light” in 1QH 18:29). See further the association between Jesus and light in the Fourth Gospel (he is the carrier of divine illumination), and the contrast between “light” (symbolizing life) and “darkness” which characterizes its teaching (John 1:4; 8:12; 9:5; 12:35–36 and 11:9–10; in the last text a moral element emerges, while in the other passages the stress is on light as the revelation of truth).

The declaration, “God is light” (ὁ θεός φῶς ἐστιν), is a penetrating description of the being and nature of God: it means that he is absolute in his glory (the physical connotation of light), in his truth (the intellectual) and in his holiness (the moral). See Westcott, 16. The Logos of God is “the light” (τό φῶς, 2:8; cf. John 1:7–9); but God himself is “light” (φῶς, anarthrous), just as he is love (1 John 4:8, 16) and spirit (John 4:24). As such, God is infinite, transcendent and “wholly other”; the source of all life and renewal (cf. 2 Cor 4:4–6).

But this does not mean that John is being speculative in his thinking at this point. To say that God is “light” is not a purely conceptual representation of his nature, detaching the being of God from his historical activity in creation; even if those from a Greek environment might have been inclined to think in this way. There are, once more, practical implications in this description. To describe God as absolute “light” presupposes that God and darkness (error, or evil) are mutually exclusive. Indeed, as the writer says here (typically following a positive statement with a contrasting negative), in God there is “no darkness (σκοτία) of any kind” (οὐδεμία, literally “no,” is intensive; hence the addition in our translation of the phrase “of any kind”). This amounts to the claim that God as light (truth and righteousness) reveals darkness (error and evil) for what it is (cf. 1:1–2; John 1:4). The statement “God is light” carries with it an inevitable moral challenge: “his followers must walk in the light”; and this is precisely the writer’s present subject (see vv 6 and 7). For the intertwining of faith and duty in this section of 1 John, with special reference to 1:6, 8 and 10 (cf. 2:4, 6 and 9), see J. L. Houlden, ExpTim 93 (1981/82) 132–36.

For εἶναι ἐν (“to be in”), which occurs in the phrase σκοτία ἐν αὐτῷ (“there is no darkness in him”), see the comment on v 8.

6. The practical and ethical implications of John’s leading statement in v 5, “God is light,” are immediately taken up and discussed. God has disclosed himself to man through Jesus Christ as the source of all life (vv 2, 5), and this determines our relationship to him; indeed, it makes such a relationship both possible and desirable.

But personal “fellowship” (vv 3, 6) between man and God depends on a clear recognition of our (human) nature, as well as a proper estimate of the nature of God himself, revealed in his Son Jesus Christ. Evidently, however, the Johannine community had been troubled by false views of man’s nature, and claims to be free from sin. It is to such claims that John addresses himself in the following vv.

Ἐὰν εἴπωμεν (“if we claim”). Whom did John have in mind? The people most likely to be maintaining a detachment from sin would be those who were inclined to gnostic views. Their dualist understanding of existence would mean that, in some cases at least, they could despise the material level of being and thus claim to be sinless (see the introduction, xxiv–vii). But this position, the writer now demonstrates, implies that its proponents had failed to come to terms with man’s actual nature. The heretics were denying the fact and results of sin because they had not accepted the truth that the goodness of God demands the goodness of man, if fellowship is to exist between them.

It is possible that heterodox opinions of this kind were held by extremists who still remained within John’s circle. But it is more probable that such ideas were characteristic of the secessionists, who had withdrawn from the community, and “defected into the world” (see the comment on 2:19; cf. also 2 John 7). They would not have claimed explicitly that sin was unimportant; but their behavior, together with the claims they were making (vv 6–10), amounted to the assertion that sin does not matter because righteousness and right conduct are unimportant. John is concerned that such heretically inclined views should not influence the orthodox members of his church. He therefore identifies himself closely with his readers (“if we claim”), as often when speaking of common Christian experience, and distinguishes three claims from which all believers should be dissociated. For the use of the first person plural, εἴπωμεν (“we claim”), see further the comment on 1:1.

The three assertions which John cites, in order to refute them, are really variations on a single theme (“sin does not affect me”), which may in some form have become an heretical catchword among the Johannine secessionists. However, the precise formulation differs in each case; and the writer’s response is also varied (especially in the third instance, 2:1–2, which answers the claim mentioned in v 10a). All three claims are introduced by an ὲάν (“if”) clause, followed by a consequential statement (such as, “we are lying”). This is followed in turn by a positive contrast (also prefaced by ἐάν, now with an adversative force, “but if”), setting out the truth which corresponds to the error. John thus uses six ὲάν clauses, in three pairs:

6. If we claim to share fellowship with him, while living in darkness, we are lying and not practicing the truth.
	

7. But if we live in the light as he himself is in the light, then we share fellowship with each other; and the blood of Jesus his Son purifies us from every sin.

8. If we claim to be sinless we are deceiving ourselves, and the truth has no place in us.
	

9. But if we acknowledge our sins, he is faithful and righteous, so that he will forgive our sins and purify us from every kind of unrighteousness.

10. If we claim that we have not sinned we are making him out to be a liar, and his word has no place in us.
	

2:1. But if anyone should sin, we have an intercessor with the Father: Jesus Christ, who is righteous.

The first error to be treated concerns the belief that sin is unimportant; that it is possible to be in a right relationship with God while behaving unrighteously. Again, probably no heretic would have said as much. The claim of this group was simply “to share fellowship with God.” But in practice their conduct belied this claim. Christian behavior cannot be separated from Christian experience, nor ethics from religion. All such spiritual assertions must be tested: in relation to the truth that God is light, and in relation to the way of life which accompanies these claims (cf. Stott, 72). One of John’s concerns throughout this document is to show that Christian belief and behavior belong inextricably together. Hence his teaching in this section.

In each of the three examples which he gives of the heterodox viewpoint about sin, as here, the writer faces the problem before moving to a positive assessment of the remedy. This lies in the forgiveness which God has made possible through the death of Christ (vv 7, 9; 2:1–2). In the present v the problem is the contradiction between “sharing fellowship with him” and “living in darkness.” The heretically inclined secessionists had been falsely claiming the former (the privilege of the Christian) while in reality being committed to the latter (the fate of the heretic). Ἔχομεν (literally, “we have”) denotes in this context a possession which is intimate and secure; hence our translation, “share.” For κοινωνία (“fellowship”) see the comment on v 3. The phrase μετʼ αὐτοῦ (“with him”) means “with God,” who is light (v 5).

The image of “walking” (περιπατῶμεν, which we have translated as “living”) is derived from the OT cf. the Hebrew halakah (“rule”; literally, “walking”). See Gen 17:1; 1 Kings 2:4; 2 Kings 20:3; cf. also 1QS 5:25; 7:18; 8:5; 1QH 16:7. It is fairly common in the Johannine literature (e.g. John 8:12; 1 John 2:6, 11; cf. 2 John 4). The present tense of ὲὰν περιπατῶμεν (literally, “if we walk,” subjunctive) denotes continuity. “Living habitually in darkness” suggests an attitude of mind, which issues in specific acts of wrong behavior, but also goes beyond these; it implies a determination to choose sin (darkness) rather than God (light) as one’s constant sphere of existence (cf. Isa 9:2; Rom 2:19; also John 3:20). On “darkness” (σκοτία), in relation to “light” (φῶς), see the comment on v 5.

ψευδόμεθα καὶ οὐ ποιοῦμεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν (“we are lying and not practicing the truth”). The writer is resisting the attitude of antinomianism (“lawlessness”), which was adopted by those who believed that the spirit could not be affected by bodily acts, and who permitted license as a consequence. Such an attitude was characteristic of some early gnostics (cf. the later position of Carpocrates); and it is fairly certain that John has in mind at this point those whose ideas were gnostically inclined. (For some primitive forms of antinomian teaching see Irenaeus, Adv. Haer. 1.1–12; Clement of Alexandria, Strom. 3.5; cf. Jude 4.)

In answer to this form of “living in darkness” John points out that sin and God are mutually exclusive, as are darkness and light (cf. Isa 59:1–2; John 12:35–36). To claim otherwise has a double significance. Positively it means that “we are lying” (ψευδόμεθα; cf. Jer 9:3, where those who “shoot lies” are equated with those who do not acknowledge God. See further 2:4.) Negatively it signifies that “we are not practicing the truth” (οὐ ποιοῦμεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν), an expression which is probably Semitic (cf. 2 Chr 31:20; Neh 9:33; 1QS 1:5; also John 3:21). There is a strong emphasis in 1 John on “lying,” as the opposite of “doing the truth” (cf. 2:4; 4:20). For the devil as the origin of falsehood see John 8:44.

This is the first appearance in 1 John of the term ἀλήθεια (“truth”), which occurs twenty times in the Johannine letters and denotes the revelation of God’s nature and salvific purposes in Jesus his Son. The primary background for the writer’s use of the concept is undoubtedly Hebraic, although the members of John’s audience who came from a Greek environment would have understood “truth” in Platonic terms as “heavenly reality.” See further the comments on 1:8 and 2:4.

7. A contrast follows. The writer pursues the image of “light versus darkness,” and offers his readers the first in a series of “light” statements (cf. also 2:8–10). The result of “living in darkness” is that it makes fellowship with God impossible, since human sinfulness inevitably interrupts a personal relationship between man and God. Conversely, “living in the light” issues in contact with God, who is himself light (see v 5).John goes on to demonstrate that it is ultimately God who makes such contact possible.

ἐὰν δὲ ἐν τῷ φωτι περιπατῶμεν . . . ἐν τῷ φωτί. John introduces the contrast to v 7 by using a balancing ἐάν (“if”) clause. The adversative δέ (“but”) underlines the contrast. The expression “if we live in the light” employs an image which may be derived from John 12:35 (“walk while you have the light”).

The present tense of the verb, περιπατῶμεν (literally, “we are walking”), denotes a continuous attitude of mind (for the verb and its use here cf. the comment on “living in darkness,” v 6). “Living in the light” thus implies a “conscious and sustained endeavour to live a life in conformity with the revelation of God” (Brooke, 15). In his being and in his activity God has made himself known (finally through his Son) as absolute in glory, truth and holiness (see the comment on v 5). The resulting demand on his creatures is that they should seek to make these qualities their own. In Pauline language this means living in complete openness to him who “searches our hearts” (Rom 8:27), and meeting the challenge to behave morally while living “a new lift” (Rom 6:4; cf. Matt 5:14–16; Luke 16:8b; Eph 5:8–14; Col 1:12–14; Phil 2:15; 1 Thess 5:5). It is in God’s light that “we see light” (Ps 36:9).

Beyond the NT the idea of “walking” in light or darkness was transposed into the allegory of the “two ways” (so Did. 1–6; Barn. 18–20; cf. also 1QS 3:20–21).

John’s thought in the present v is consistent. To “live in the light” is to be true to God, as he is true to himself (cf. 2 Tim 2:13). For God is “light,” and “he himself” (which is the force as well as the meaning of the reflexive) is therefore in the light. John’s imagery is sufficiently flexible for the symbol of “light” to be applied to God in these two directions without confusion. A further significant contrast is suggested by the statement that God is “in the light.” Men “live” in the light, but God “is” (ἐστιν) in it (Brooke, 15). Note the careful balance of the repeated phrase ἐν τῷ φωτί (“in the light”) in both contexts.

κοινωνίαν ἔχομεν μετʼ ἀλλήλων (“we share fellowship with each other”). Two positive consequences of living in the light are now set out, corresponding to the negative results of living in darkness mentioned in v 6. It might be expected that the first would be “we share fellowship with God,” which was the claim made by the heretics and just refuted (see v 3b). Instead, the thought moves on: to live in the light is to share fellowship with God’s people. But this is an entirely complementary thought, and one which is implied in the challenge to live in the light. It is axiomatic for John that fellowship with God involves fellowship with his people. Perhaps the secessionists had claimed to have a relationship with God while at the same time neglecting to love their fellow-Christians. This attitude, which would have been typical of those with gnostic inclinations, existed as a further mark of the heretics’ falsehood (see further the comment on v 6).

John has already stated a desire for “fellowship” (κοινωνία) with his readers (1:3; for the term itself see the comment on that v). In 1:3 he uses the pronouns ὑμεῖς (“you”) and ἡμῶν (“us”). Now he identifies himself even more closely with the orthodox (“apostolic”) members of the Johannine circle by using the first person plural alone: “we share (ἔχομεν, literally, “we have”) fellowship with each other.” For this usage see further the comment on v 5; see also 1:1.

καί τὸ αἴμα . . . ἁμαρτίας. John suddenly widens his theological terms of reference by his announcement that “the blood of Jesus purifies us from every sin” (cf. 5:6). The second result of living in the light follows from the first: “we share fellowship with each other, and (καί) the blood of Jesus purifies us.” This is because the achievement of fellowship with God, however remote it may be, immediately produces an awareness of God’s holiness and man’s unholiness, or sin. The author therefore reassures his readers that God has anticipated this need. In the death and resurrection of Jesus exists the possibility of purification from “every sin” (πάσης ἁμαρτίας). This refers to man’s sinful nature in general, although it may include the wrong acts which can occur even when a Christian is living “in the light.”

    Note on “Sin”

    The Gr. terms for “sin,” like the Hebrew, are varied. The most common is ἁμαρτιχα, which is defined in 1 John as both “lawlessness” (ἀνομία, 3:4) and “wrongdoing” (ἀδικία, 5:17). This is the normative understanding of the concept of sin throughout the Bible; for in both the OT and the NT “sin” describes “every departure from the way of righteousness, both human and divine” (BAG, 42). In characteristic Johannine usage, sin also appears as that which is opposed to the truth (1:8; cf. John 9:41). Westcott (38) distinguishes (perhaps too sharply) in the use of the singular noun ἁμαρτία (sin in general, or an act of sin) and the verb ἁμαρτάνειν (to present a sinful character, or commit a sinful act); between the principle of sin, and the manifestation of this in the sinful acts of the individual (cf. John 16:8–9; 1 John 3:6; 5:16–18). In the plural use of the noun, ἁμαρτίαι (“sins”), there is no ambivalence (cf. 1:9).

    In 1 John sin is regarded as a universal condition (1:10), resulting, apart from Christ, in alienation from God (2:15) and spiritual death (3:14). But, in Christ, sin (and sins) can be removed (2:2; 3:5; cf. John 1:29). See further Westcott, 37–40.

τὸ αἷμα Ἰησοῦ τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ. John says that “the blood of Jesus” purifies us from every sin. The term αἷμα (“blood”) is a symbol for the crucifixion of Christ, and its background is to be located in Jewish sacrifice. In the OT blood (Hebrew dām) was regarded as the seat of life (Lev 17:11). In terms of sacrifice, as a means of atonement (“at-one-ment” between man and God), the “blood” of a victim was thus its life yielded up in death; and the “sprinkling” of that blood guaranteed for the worshiper the effectiveness of any sacrifice (cf. Exod 30:10; Lev 16:15–19).

The “blood” of Jesus occupies an important place in NT thought, and must be interpreted above all against the specific background of the cultic observances on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16; but cf. also the Passover story and ritual, Exod 12). In his suffering and death, the NT writers claim, Jesus in perfect obedience offered the true and lasting sacrifice for sin (cf. Rom 3:25; Heb 9:12–14; 10:19–22; Rev 1:5; also 1 Cor 5:7). Thus to say here that the blood of Jesus “purifies us from every sin” means that in the cross of Christ our sin is effectively and repeatedly (καθαρίζει, “purifies,” is a continuous present) removed; although John does not explain how this happens. The covenant between God and Israel, sealed and restored by sacrifice, is also related to the NT idea of Christ’s offering for man’s sin (cf. Jer 31:31–34; Heb 8:7–13).

On the basis of this v Westcott (34–37) argues, with particular reference to Lev 17:11, that the “blood” of Jesus in the NT, rather than signifying his sacrificial death, always includes the thought of his life, “preserved and active beyond death” (36). A similar position is adopted by Dewar, JTS ns 4 (1953) 203–208. Against this view, and for further consideration of the biblical idea of “blood,” especially in association with the death of Jesus, see Morris, Apostolic Preaching, 112–28; J. Behm, TDNT 1 (1964) 172–76. cf. also Taylor, Atonement, 130–61; F. Laubach, NIDNTT 1 (1975) 220–24, especially 223.

The sacrifice which “purifies us from every sin” is described here as that of “Jesus his (God’s) Son” (Ἰησοῦ τοῦ υίοῦ αὐτοῦ). Such a designation may have been carefully chosen by the writer. For the christology which is implied in this phrase could provide a counterbalance to the views of those who were making, or were inclined to make, heretical claims about Jesus. Those with too high a view of his person are reminded that Christ was genuinely human (hence the use of the earthly name, “Jesus”), and therefore able to be the sacrifice for human sin. On the other hand, those whose estimate of Jesus was too low are recalled to the truth that he was also God’s own Son, so that he could offer the sacrifice perfectly. (See the introduction, xxvi–vii.) On “Son” see further the comments on 1:3 and 3:8.

O’Neill (Puzzle, 10) regards the whole phrase τὸ αἷμα Ἰησοῦ τοῦ υίοῦ αὐτοῦ (“the blood of Jesus his Son”) as a gloss, introduced after the writer had completed his work. (Similarly Bultmann, 20, sees it as a later redaction.) O’Neill’s argument is that confession in the total passage 1:5–10 is the only condition of forgiveness (see v 9). However, as he admits, there is no textual evidence in favor of this view. Moreover, the reference to the “blood of Jesus” is a logical and indispensable step in John’s argument at this point, and consistent with the other sacrificial references to the death of Jesus in 1 John (cf. 2:2; 4:10). It is true that references of this kind are explicit in the letters and not the Gospel of John. But (i) sacrificial ideas are implicit in the Gospel (e.g. John 1:29, 36; 3:14–16; 10:11; 11:50–52; 12:24; see also 6:53–56, using αἷμα); (ii) in 1 John the sacrificial description of Christ’s death develops the understanding of atonement in the Fourth Gospel, for the special benefit of those readers (from a Greek background) who may have been prepared to play down or even deny the reality of the cross; and this is no doubt one further reason for the distinctive formulation of John’s argument here.

The verb καθαρίζειν (“to cleanse,” or “to purify”), as used here, denotes the removal of sin’s entail, and not merely the forgiveness of sin (cf. Eph 5:26; Titus 2:14; Heb 10:2; also John 13:10; and see 1:9). The “purified” (καθαροί) in heart are elsewhere promised by Jesus the ultimate blessing of “seeing God” (Matt 5:8).

The secessionists from the Johannine community who were maintaining that sin was unimportant (vv 6–7), and whose views the writer is especially attacking at this point, may have appealed to the Fourth Gospel to support their position. For example, they might have interpreted John 3:16–18a to mean that present salvation is all, and that the believing sinner therefore need not fear condemnation. Similarly John’s reply, that salvation is a universal need which has been met in the sacrificial death of Jesus, may also hark back to elements of teaching preserved in the Fourth Gospel. See 2:4–9. cf. further Brown, Community, 124–27; also the introduction, xxvi–vii.
Explanation

The section 1:5–7 forms an introduction to the first main division of 1 John. It leads into the subject of the true character of Christian living by stating a theme, “God is light” (v 5). This acts almost as a text to a sermon, since what follows immediately is a meditation on it.

A practical motive is involved here. John exhorts his readers not merely to see that God is light, but also to “live in the light.” To this end he counters those who claim union with God while really living in darkness (v 6), and demonstrates the effect of “living in the light” (v 7). His message thus contains both an ethical and a theological thrust; and these emphases are closely related. John refutes the heretical argument that it is possible to be true to God, while living in sin, by reminding his audience of the divine character itself. God’s essential holiness (or “wholeness”) excludes man’s unholiness. At the same time the writer asserts that the problem of sin, which is borne in upon us as we live habitually in the light, has been anticipated by Jesus. As Son of God he really lived and died to make possible the removal of human sinfulness (v 7).

In both arguments, the theological and the practical, John no doubt has in mind the views of those from a Greek background, who denied the reality of the Incarnation and Atonement, and also believed that right conduct was immaterial. In his insistence on the true Sonship of Jesus, however, the writer would also have furnished a response to those who had emerged from a Jewish background, and whose understanding of Christ’s divinity was impoverished.

Form/Structure/Setting

The subject matter in this section of 1 John has determined the present division of vv. After propounding his thesis that “God is light” (v 5), the writer proceeds to show his readers how it is possible to “live in the light.” Four conditions are set out, and the first of these (renunciation of sinai forms the topic of this passage.

The literary form of each verse here is balanced, and John follows carefully the structural pattern which has been established in the previous section (see vv 6 and 7). He uses two more pairs of ἐάν (“if”) clauses in order to present a false assertion made by the heretics, introduced with the words “if we claim” (the first ἐάν clause), followed by an explanatory statement, followed by a positive assertion of the truth (the second ἐάν clause). Two such claims, which are roughly two versions of the same claim (see the comment on v 6), are set out and commented upon now. Thus:

8. If we claim to be sinless, we are deceiving ourselves.
	

9. But if we acknowledge our sins, he will forgive our sins.

10. If we claim that we have not sinned, we are making him out to be a liar.
	

1,2. If anyone should sin, we have an intercessor, our sin-offering.

After each version of the heretical error presented in vv 8 and 10 the explanatory comment is repeated in a roughly “parallel” form: “we are deceiving ourselves/the truth has no place in us” (v 8); “we are making him out to be a liar/his word has no place in us” (v 10). Compare v 6, the first version of the claim that sin is inconsequential: “we are lying/we are not practicing the truth.”

O’Neill (Puzzle, 13–15) regards vv 1b and 2 (with 2:6) as Christian glosses on a Jewish hymn about obedience (the rest of 2:1–6). Similarly Bultmann (23) sees v 2 as an ecclesiastical redaction. But there is no evidence for treating these vv as secondary; and, on the contrary, it is possible to show (as we shall do) that they are integral (both theologically and in terms of practical exhortation) to the structure and content of John’s argument at this point.
Comment

8. The mention of “sin” at the end of v 7 (that sin from which the blood of Jesus purifies those who live in the light) gives rise to a statement of the second version of the affirmation about the unimportance of sin which was being made by the heretically inclined secessionists (see v 6). They had already claimed fellowship with God while actually living in darkness. Now they go further and claim to be sinless. These Christians did not need “purification from every sin” because, they said, they were without sin. What John regarded as sinful did not appear to them as such. Whereas the claim in v 6 could be true if those who made it were “living in the light,” this assumption of sinlessness seems to be false in any situation.

ἐάν εἴπωμεν ὄτι ἁμαρτίαν οὐκ ἔκομεν, “if we claim to be sinless.” Using the same formula as in v 6a, ἐάν εἴπωμεν ὄτι (“if we claim that”; literally, “if we say that”), John introduces a variation of the basic assertion which was being made by the gnostically inclined secessionists from John’s community (cf. further the comment on v 6). This was the assertion that sin could not affect their relationship to God (v 6), because (in their eyes, at least) they were sinless (vv 8 and 10). Their “gnostic illumination,” that is to say, made sin a matter of moral irrelevance to them. For the philosophical basis to this dualist view see further the introduction, xxiii–iv; cf. also the comment on 2:3.

The version of the heretical claim to be sinless mentioned in this v (ἁμαρτίαν οὐκ ἔχομεν; literally, “we have no sin”) is more clear-cut than in v 6. The assertion now reported may have been a response to the accusation levelled at the heretics by the orthodox Johannine Christians: that those who boasted of living in the light while walking in darkness were in practice committing sin (being unrighteous) without acknowledging it. The explicit reply of the heretics, that they were “sinless” (cf. also v 10), may have depended on the discussion in John’s Gospel (8:31–47), misapplied in their own case, about sinfulness as a characteristic of those who do not believe (see the introduction, xxvi).

The expression ἁμαρτίαν (“to have sin”) is found in the NT only here and in the Fourth Gospel (e.g. John 9:41; 15:22; cf. 5:26, ζωήν ἔχειν, “to have life”). To “have sin” is the equivalent of possessing a sinful character or disposition. Cf. the use of the phrase πιστιν ἔχειν (“to have faith”) in Matt 17:20, to describe the active principle of faith in the life of the believer. Thus “ ‘sin’ is the principle of which sinful acts are the several manifestations” (Brooke, 17). The latter, sinful acts, are distinguished as such in v 10 (οὐκ ἡμαρτἠκαμεν, “we have not sinned”). However, perhaps the contrast should not be drawn too sharply; for man’s sinful nature is not, in fact, easily discerned apart from his sinful actions.

Law (Tests, 130) interprets the phrase ἀμαρτίαν ἔχειν judicially, to mean “having guilt or responsibility for wrong actions.” But although the idea of responsibility for one’s sin is certainly involved in the use of this phrase (rather than the verb, ἀμαρτάνειν, “to sin”) by the fourth evangelist (e.g. John 15:22), it is in any case only one aspect of the notion of sin in both Hebrew and Christian thought (see above, 24). For sin in biblical terms is basically a departure from right; and guilt or responsibility is therefore an inevitable consequence of sin, but not its inclusive character. In this v it is precisely a denial of sin altogether to which the writer is alluding.

ἐαυτούς πλανῶμεν . . . ἐν ἠμῖν, “we are deceiving ourselves. . . .” The result of the heretical claim to sinlessness is described in two parallel ways. Positively, “we are deceiving ourselves”; negatively, “the truth has no place in us.” The emphatic ἐαυτούς πλανῶμεν (“we are deceiving ourselves”), rather than the single verb, πλανώμεθα (“we are deceived”), suggests a deliberate refusal to face the facts. To deny that human nature is sinful is actively to practice self-deception. The verb πλανᾶν (“to deceive”) has an OT background (cf. Deut 13:6, LXX; also Wis 5:6; 1QH 4:16), and includes the notion of “leading astray” (cf. 2:26; 3:7; see also John 7:12, 47, where Jesus is accused of deceiving people). The verb is also used in Rev (e.g. 2:20; 12:9) to describe the deception of the faithful by the servants of evil.

The fact that the truth has “no place” in the secessionists (and those who are like them) means not merely that they are lying, but also that they do not and cannot share, as they suppose, in the reality of God, whose “true” nature has been revealed in Jesus as the truth (cf. John 1:4; 8:31, 32; 14:6). Like the “word” of God (v 10), the truth of God has no place in such people.

The abstract, “Truth,” was eventually to become hypostatized by gnostic thinkers. In the developed system of the second century Alexandrian Valentinus, for example, the Godhead was made up of thirty “aeons.” These were regarded as male and female pairs; and among them Intellect and Truth produced Word and Life, who in turn produced Man and the Church.

On ἀλήθεια (“truth”) see further the comment on v 6; cf. also A. C. Thiselton, NIDNTT 3 (1978) 889–94, especially 890. See also Büchsel, Der Begriff. For the characteristically Johannine formula εἶναι ἐν (“to be in”), which is used in the phrase “the truth has no place in us,” see Malatesta, Interiority, 27–34. This expression can be used to describe the mutual indwelling which exists between the believer and the Godhead (e.g. 2:5; 5:20); although the more regular expression for such mutual indwelling is μένειν έν (“to abide in”), as in 2:6. In the present v εἶναι ἐν refers to the moral quality of “truth” which can be found “in” (ἐν) believers, but not in their opponents. Cf. 2:4; see also v 10 (God’s word [not] “in us”) and 2:15 (the Father’s love [not] “in him”). In 1:5 there is a parallel, but inverted, usage: darkness is in God’s enemies, but not at all “in” (ἐν) him.

In this v John denounces those who claim to be without sin, whereas in 3:6, 9; 5:18 he appears to argue that the Christian does not and cannot sin. On this basis Bogart, Perfectionism (especially 1–5, 25–49), maintains that two distinct types of perfectionism (sinless behavior) are to be found in 1 John: “heretical” (as in 1:8, 10) and “orthodox” (as in 3:6). In his view this accounts for the seeming paradox whereby John condemns “perfectionism” here, but commends it later. However, the paradox may not be real, if in each case John has in mind what is normally expected of the believer (that he will not sin habitually), and what actually happens (that he sometimes falls short of God’s standards). Cf. 2:1; and see further the comments on 3:6, 9; 5:18. For “sin” in 1 John see the comment on 1:7.

9. The writer now sets out, in balanced contrast, the truth corresponding to the second statement of the error which was being propagated by the heretics. The claim to be sinless is a self-deception. For sin is a fact of life, and characteristic of those who are Christians, as well as those who are not. Indeed, the believer is likely to be more sensitive than the worldly person (cf. 2:15–17) to the real nature and seriousness of sin. But its reality does not prevent fellowship with the “faithful and righteous” God, provided that the sin in question is acknowledged before him. In this affirmation (as in v 7 and 2:1b)John may be replying to a misreading by the secessionists of the fourth evangelist’s teaching in John 8:31–47; see the comment on v 8.

ἐάν ὁμολογῶμεν τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν, “if we acknowledge our sins.” The contrast with v 8 is again introduced by ἐάν (“if”); this is the fourth use of the formula in the three pairs of ἐάν clauses which occur in 1:6—2:1. In the present v ἐάν has an adversative force (hence our translation, “but if”); although the opposition between vv 9 and 8 is not so deliberate as it was between vv 7 and 6 (using ἐάν . . . ἐάν δὲ, “if . . . but if”).

Although the verb “we acknowledge” (ὁμολογῶμεν) appears in a conditional clause (beginning with ἐάν), the whole phrase has the force of a command (so Marshall, 113). We ought to acknowledge our sins; and, if we do, God responds. The manner of the acknowledgment is not specified. However, Westcott (23) may be right to suggest that an element of public confession before others, as well as God, is involved; for elsewhere in the Johannine corpus the verb ὁμολογεῖν (“to acknowledge”) is used in the sense of open “witness” (see 2:23; John 1:20; Rev 3:5; cf. also Matt 10:32; Rom 10:9).For the cognate verb ἐξομολογεῖν (“to confess”), used in a baptismal context, see Matt 3:6 = Mark 1:5; Did. 4:14. The use of the plural, “sins” (τὰς ἁμαρτίας), probably indicates that the confession of particular acts of sin is meant in this context, rather than the acknowledgment of “sin” in general. The exact phrase, ἐάν ὁμολογῶμεν τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν, occurs only here in the NT (but cf. Mark 1:5; James 5:16, “confess your sins to each other”). For “sin” see the comment on 1:7.

πιστός ἐστιν καὶ δίκαυος, “he is faithful and righteous.” Although the adjective δίκαιος (“righteous,” or “just”) can be used of Jesus in Johannine thought (cf. 2:1; also John 5:30, referring to the judgment of Jesus as “just”), the subject of the present phrase, “he is (ε) faithful and righteous,” is obviously God (the “he” of vv 6 and 7). To acknowledge sin, John says, brings into focus these two separate qualities in God’s character: his faithfulness, and his righteousness. The Gr. construction, πίστος ἐστιν καὶ δίκαιος . . . (“faithful he is and righteous . . . .”), suggests the way in which both divine qualities are called forth by the open acknowledgment of human sin.

God’s “faithfulness” is regularly associated in the Bible with his covenant promises (cf. Ps 89:1–4; Heb 10:23); and these include forgiveness when man breaks the covenant relationship (cf. Jer 31:34; Mic 7:18–20). So here forgiveness is a part of his faithful nature. Furthermore, God’s righteousness is closely linked at this point in the text with his faithfulness. Brooke (19) interprets δίκαιος (“righteous”) in this context as a virtual synonym for πίστος (“faithful”); that is to say, God shows his “justice” by remaining true to his promise of forgiveness, even when man has failed to fulfill his covenant obligations. Dodd (22–23) regards God’s “forgiveness” in this passage (= πιστός) as a function of his righteousness (= δίκαιος); cf. Rom 3:21–26; 2 Tim 2:13. In other words, God’s righteousness (as in Isa 51:5) is a salvific action, not merely an attribute. Because by nature God is forgiving, he forgives.

Neither of these two interpretations brings out John’s meaning fully. While (with Brooke and Dodd) it is important not to oppose the love and justice of God (as happens in some conservative theories of the atonement), it remains true that the NT, and indeed the Bible as a whole, finds a place for both. God’s attitude to man is one of faithfulness, and the desire to forgive (πιστός). Moreover, he can forgive because, while remaining righteous in being and action (δίκαιος), he has made it possible for us to become righteous (cf. the last phrase of this v also John 17:25 and Rom 3:26). That this is a probable exegesis of the present passage is supported by the fact that God’s activity, as described in v 9, follows and interprets more completely the concept of divine “purification” from sin mentioned in v 7 (note the use of the same verb, καθαρίζειν, “to purify”). Again, we are not told how this happens; but acknowledgment of sin, John claims, inevitably results in God’s forgiving and purifying response; and the possibility of this is grounded in the death of Jesus. For δίκαιος see further the use of the cognate noun, “righteousness” (δικαιοσύνη), at 2:29; 3:7, 10; and note John 16:8, 10. The first two texts also use δίκαιος.

ἴνα ἀφῇ ἡμῖν . . . ἀπὸ πάσης ἀδικίας. The sphere of God’s “purification” from sin (v 7) is now explained. The clause is introduced by ἴνα (“so that”), which depends on the preceding two adjectives, “faithful and righteous.” The faithfulness and righteousness of God are such “that” he will forgive/purify (purpose), and does so (result). Cf. Moule, Idiom-Book, 142, who notes the lack of a sharp dividing-line in Semitic thought between intention and consequence.

God’s saving action, in response to acknowledged sin, operates in two directions: forgiveness of sin, and purification from “every kind of unrighteousness.” The images are expressive; for sin is an offense which God expiates, and also a stain which he removes (cf. Stott, 77). The verb ἀφιέναι (“to forgive”) means literally “to release,” or “to let go.” Its background is forensic (cf. Luke 7:43, concerning release from debt); although in the LXX the verb is also used in a cultic setting (cf. Lev 4:20; 19:22). For the verb καθαρίζειν (“to purify”) see v 7. The two verbs, “forgive” and “purify,” are not entirely synonymous; for καθαρίζειν may mean the removal of sinful desire in general, as well as of the guilt attaching to actual sins (cf. Westcott, 25). But the primary significance of this passage is in any case related to the divine pardon which is available for every believer who confesses to sinful actions (hence the plural, τὰς ἁμαρτίας, “sins”; see v 7a).

John no doubt chose the term ἀδικία (“unrighteousness”), instead of ἁμαρτία (“sin”), because it forms a contrast with δίκαιος (“righteous”), used of God earlier in the sentence. The only other occurrence of the word in the Johannine letters is at 5:17, where ἀδικία is identified with ἁμαρτία. The singular phrase, “every kind of unrighteousness” (πάσης ἀδικίας), refers to the confession of sin in detail.

God’s salvific actions in this v are regarded as constant. The verb ἡμῖν (literally, “he forgives”) is thus in the present tense, suggesting his daily forgiveness. (At 2:12 the verb appears in the perfect tense, where the complementary sense is that of the initial experience of divine forgiveness on becoming a Christian.) The pronouns ἡμῖν (literally, “to us”) and ἡμᾶς (“us”) are repeated for emphasis; God’s healing is essentially personal, and concerned with the individual.

10. This v sets out the third version of the heretical affirmation about the unimportance of sin. For its possible background in the teaching of John’s Gospel see the comment on v 8. The present expression of the heretics’ claim (“we have not sinned”) is the most clear-cut in the series; and the description of its consequence (we make God out to be a liar) is the most grave.

ἐὰν εἴπωμεν ὄτι οὐκ ἡμαρτήκαμεν, “if we claim that we have not sinned.” To introduce the claim to be without sin, and its counter in 2:1b, John uses the final pair of ἐάν (“if”) clauses in this passage. The conditional clause (“if we claim”) is followed (as in vv 6 and 8) by a ὄτι (“that”) clause, containing a statement in indirect speech (“that we have not sinned”).

The statement about the absence of sin in the life of the believer may have been suggested by the reference in v 9 to the need for our “sins” (τὰς ἁμαρτίας) to be acknowledged. Cf. the similar connection between vv 7 and 8. Moreover, this explicit assertion on the part of the heretics (“we have not sinned”) may have been initially a response to John’s accusation (v 8b) that the claim to sinlessness was a piece of self-deception (cf. the link between vv 8a and 6b).

It is difficult to see any real difference between the affirmations “we are sinless” (v 8) and “we have not sinned” (v 10); although Westcott (25) argues that in the earlier v the heretics are represented as denying the “permanence of sin as a power,” whereas here they are said to be denying absolutely the practice of sin in their own lives. A broader, and more likely, distinction possibly exists in these two vv between the principle of sin, ἁμαρτίαν οὐκ ἔχομεν (literally, “we do not have sin,” using the present tense of the verb), and its expression in sinful acts (οὐκ ἡμαρτήκαμεν, “we have not sinned,” using the perfect).

We should not lose sight of the tact that at this point John is still drawing out the ethical implications of his thesis, “God is light” (v 5). He has already declared that “living in the light,” and thus sharing fellowship with God and God’s people, cannot be compatible with “living in the darkness” (vv 6–7). The writer later urges those of his readers who were inclined to heresy to renounce sin altogether (2:1a), especially in the face of their claims to be sinless (vv 8, 10). In other words, God’s nature as light not only invites us to become like him by living in the light, thus sharing his fellowship; he also makes possible the first vital step toward this transformation, as we confess our sin (when we recognize our natural unlikeness to God) and acknowledge our consequent need of the salvation that is through him.

ψεύστην ποιοῦμεν αὐτόν, “we are making him out to be a liar.” Further consequences of the heretical claim to be sinless are now specified. In vv 6 and 8 the result of such a claim has been described as falsehood on man’s part: “we are not practicing the truth” (v 6); and “the truth has no place in us” (v 8). In this v John defines the outcome more strongly, with reference to God. The main subject of 1:5–7 and 1:8—2:2 is clearly God, although he is mentioned by name only in v 5. Thus the pronoun αὐτόν (“him”) here must refer (as in vv 6 and 7) to God himself; similarly with αὐτοῦ later in this sentence.

The declaration “we have not sinned,” John claims, has a double consequence. One is positive, and the other negative. First, the claim to be without sin suggests falsehood on God’s part; it “makes him out to be a liar.” The universality of human sin is a common biblical doctrine (cf. Ps 14:3; Isa 53:6; John 2:24–25; Rom 3:22–24); and so also is the theme of the mercy of God, who forgives the sinner (Jer 31:34; Eph 4:32; among the Johannine writings the actual verb ἀφιέναι, “to forgive,” occurs only in this letter). Thus, to deny the tact of sin in one’s own life is to deny the holy and forgiving nature of God; it is to impute falsehood to him, and to challenge his own verdict on man’s guilt as a sinner (see John 16:8–9; Rom 8:1; cf. Schnackenburg, 88). The use of the expression ψεύστην ποιεῖν τίνα (“to make someone a liar”) is characteristically Johannine (cf. 5:10, where the same formula occurs; also John 5:18).

καὶ ὁ λόγος αὐτοῦ οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν ἡμῖν, “and his word has no place in us.” The second (negative) outcome of the claim to be without sin is an extension of the first. It is that “his word has no place in us.” The “word” (λόγος) of God may refer to the personal Logos (as in John 1:1–14). Equally, it could refer to the message of the gospel, the proclamation about the Word (see the comment on 1:1; cf. also 2:14; John 17:6; Acts 4:31). The meaning in this latter case would be that those, like the heretics, who claim that they have not sinned cut themselves off from all that God has said to man in Christ, and from all that he continues to say through the Christian preaching of the apostles.

However, as in 1:1, a deliberate ambivalence may be included in the reference to λόγος at this point. To make God out to be a liar, the writer may be saying, is only possible either for someone who is not listening clearly to the good news about Jesus, or for someone in whom Jesus, by his Spirit, does not dwell (cf. John 14:23; 15:4; Col 1:27). We may notice in passing the extent to which, as here, John and Paul are at one in their profound understanding of the Christ-Christian relationship; see Smalley, “Christ-Christian Relationship,” especially 96–100.

The phrase “(his word) has no place in us” (οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν ἡμῖν) is parallel to that in v 8, “(the truth) has no place in us.” For the use of εἶναι ἐν (“to be in”) see the comment on v 8. The significance in both places is roughly synonymous. However, if the term λόγος in the present context is ambivalent, and includes a reference to Christ (see above), it could be argued that John regards the indwelling of God’s word in more personal terms than the interiority of divine “truth.” Nevertheless, see John 17:17, where Jesus describes God’s “word” as “truth.”

2:1. John now turns to answer the third version of the basic error which the secessionists from his church, and their followers, were perpetrating (the claim to be without sin, as outlined in 1:10). In so doing he declares one of his chief objects in writing this document, and comes to the heart of his purpose in this section. The first condition for living in the light, he says, is to renounce sin (“I am writing that you may not sin”).

Τεκνία μον, ταῦτα γράφω ὑμῖν ἴνα μὴ ἁμάρτητε, “I am writing these things to you, my dear children, in order that you may not sin.” The formulation of this reply to the heretical position varies slightly from the first two. Whereas vv 7 and 9 began immediately with the word ἐάν (“if”), the contrast here, introduced by καὶ ἐάν (“but if,” v 1b), is held up for a moment. This gives the writer an opportunity to appeal to his readers in direct and pastoral terms.

John’s personal appeal contains two striking features. First, he uses the address τεκνία μου (“my dear children”), which occurs in the NT only here (but see 3 John 4; Gal 4:19). The diminutive τεκνίον (meaning “a little, or young, child”) is used, in the plural, as a term of endearment and intimacy (hence the translation, “my dear children”). Cf. 2:12, 28; 3:7, 18; 4:4; 5:21; note also John 13:33. Elsewhere John’s readers are simply referred to as παιδία (“children”). Cf. 2:14, 18; also John 21:5. When believers are described as God’s children (as in 3:1 and 5:2) the word τέκνον (θεοῦ), “child (of God),” is used. But see 3:10 (“the devil’s children”), and the general use in 2 John 1, 4, 13; 3 John 4 (“children,” or “offspring”), all of which use τέκνον in the singular or plural. We may compare Paul’s similar way of addressing his congregations (e.g. 1 Cor 4:14).

The second noticeable feature of the writer’s personal appeal in this parenthesis is to be found in his use of the first personal singular, γράφω (“I am writing”), and in the reference to his audience as “you” (ὑμῖν cf. 1:2, 3, 5). John appears to use the first personal singular for direct address or exhortation; as here, when he abandons the first person plural which he has employed throughout the first chapter, and distinguishes his audience as “you.” He tends to use the first person plural (“we”) either when he is associating himself with those who are the guardians of the apostolic faith in the transmission of the kerygmatic message (so 1:1–3), or when he is identifying himself with his readers in terms of basic Christian experience (see v 1b).

John will revert to the inclusive “we” form of address in 2:3. Meanwhile he turns for a moment from the problems posed by his heretical opponents, which have been particularly in mind since 1:6, to urge upon his immediate adherents behavior which is suitable for the Christian.

John’s teaching in the preceding vv (1:6–10), with its emphasis on purification and forgiveness, may have been interpreted as an encouragement to be sinful: both by the secessionists, who regarded sin as unimportant anyway (cf. vv 8, 10), and even by some orthodox members of the Johannine community (cf. Rom 6:1). So the writer seizes this chance to call upon all his followers to repudiate sin entirely. John is not suggesting by this the possibility of a completely sinless existence; rather, he is pleading for a renunciation of the disposition toward sinfulness which issues in specific acts of wrongdoing. The use of the aorist subjunctive, ἴνα μὴ ἁμάρτητε, “in order that you may not sin,” supports this interpretation, since it refers to “definite acts of sin rather than the habitual state” (Brooke, 23). In other words, John wishes his readers to recognize the fact and seriousness of sin, and also to “walk in the light” by living, so far as is possible, blamelessly. (See, however, the comment above on v 8; cf. also 3:6, 9; 5:18.)

It is for this reason that John is writing “these things” (ταῦτα). The reference of ταῦτα may be to the passage 1:6–10; or to the first chapter as a whole, in which the main themes of 1 John are outlined; or to the complete letter (cf. 5:13).

καὶ ἐάν τις ἁμάρτῃ, “but if any should sin.” John has been encouraging his readership to renounce sin. But he also recognizes that sin is an everpresent possibility for the believer. Thus for the third time (see vv 7, 9) he comes to the question of forgiveness, and in so doing replies once more to the heretical claim to be free from sin. John’s answer is again introduced by an adversative ἐάν (“if”; here καὶ ἐάν “but if”); cf. the parallels in v 9a, answering the claim of v 8a, and v 7a, providing a reply to the assertion of v 6a. For the use of ἐάν in this passage see further the comments on vv 6 and 9.

The writer has already put the negative point: he is writing to discourage sinful behavior of any kind. Now, in typically balanced style, he deals with the problem of sin positively: if anyone should sin, God has made provision for this. Whereas in the first part of this v John is anticipating too lenient an attitude toward sin, in the second half he is countering the possibility of too harsh a view. The fact that once again the aorist tense is used (ἐάν τις ἁμάρτῃ, “if any should sin”) indicates that acts of sin are in mind, not the state of sin (cf. the comment on ἴνα μὴ ἁμάρτητε, “that you may not sin,” in v 1a; and contrast the use of the present tense ἁμαρτάνει, “sins,” in 3:6, 8; 5:18).

At no point does the author of this letter argue for the possibility of sinlessness as such in the life of the Christian on earth. He is well aware of the reality of sin, even in the believer. He has been resisting the heretical view of its unimportance; and twice (vv 7 and 9) he has mentioned its remedy. Now, as a direct answer to the claim in v 10a (“we have not sinned”) and its result (“we are making God out to be a liar,” v 10b), and also as an extension of his message, John returns once again to the subject of God’s salvific activity on behalf of sinful mankind. He specifies two ways in which this has been manifested; and in so doing he develops his theology of atonement, set out in vv 6–10.

παράκλητον ἔχομεν πρὸς τὸν πατέρα, “we have an intercessor with the Father.” The first way in which God’s forgiving nature has been revealed is in the provision of an intercessor (παράκλητον ἔχομεν, “we have an intercessor”). Westcott (42) notes a significant change of construction in the sentence at this point. John writes “if any should sin, we have an intercessor” (not, for example, “if you . . . you”). This emphasizes the individual character of sinful activity (“if anyone”); and it also draws attention to the existence of the Christian society with which the writer is identified and to which “Christ’s promises are assured” (Westcott, ibid.). The change is the more marked after the author’s use of “I . . . you” in the first part of this v. For the appearance of the third person plural (“we”) in this context see the comment on v 1a.

The noun παράκλητος, “intercessor,” is uniquely Johannine; and it appears only here and in the Fourth Gospel (at 14:16, 26; 15:26; 16:7). For its use and meaning in John’s Gospel, and for the difficulty of translating the term exactly, see Smalley, John, 228–33. The word is passive in form (cf. κλητός, “called”), and comes from the verb παρακαλεῖν, which originally meant “to summon to one’s aid,” corresponding to the Latin advocare. In classical literature and also in Philo the term παράκλητος is thus used to mean “one who is called alongside (to help).” (For the background see Brooke, 23–25.) The “aid” in question varies according to the context; but the sense of acting as an advocate or intercessor, particularly in the setting of a law-court, is often present. In the LXX the term παράκλητος as such is not used; but cognates, the verb παρακαλεῖν (“to call to one’s side”) and the noun παράκλησις (“encouragement”), occur quite frequently. There, as in the NT, the sense is predominantly the provision of “comfort” (cf. Job 21:2, using παράκλησις, “consolation,” to translate the Hebrew Ṯanḥumet; also Acts 9:31). Nevertheless, the idea of acting as an advocate, or interceding on behalf of others before God, is fairly common in the OT (cf. Exod 32:30–32, Moses for the people; Job 42:7–10, for his friends).

It is precisely this latter meaning which attaches to the use of παράκλητος in the present v. In our need of divine forgiveness, says the writer, we have an effective intercessor to act for us, and to present our case to God the Father (cf. Matt 10:32). We cannot do this for ourselves, since we are sinners; but the risen Jesus does so on our behalf (cf. Rom 8:34, Jesus intercedes for us; 8:26, the Spirit intercedes for us; Heb 7:25, Jesus makes intercession for those who draw near to God). As Son, he pleads for the sinner “with” (πρός, Latin apud) the Father. This means that Jesus intercedes in the presence of God. But he does so actively: “with” the Father, he asks for the penitent’s forgiveness when anyone sins (cf. the use of πρός, “with,” at 1:2, “eternal life existed with the Father”; also in John 1:1, “the Word was with God”). For the characteristically Johannine description of God as “Father” see the comment on 1:2.

Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν δίκαιον, “Jesus Christ, who is righteous.” The direct association between Jesus and the παράκλητος is significant, and should not be passed over lightly. For it may have a bearing on the identity of the Paraclete in the farewell discourse of the Fourth Gospel, where (although this is debated) the term seems to refer to the Spirit, as the alter ego (“other I”) of Jesus. See Smalley, John, 228–30, and the literature there cited, including the work of O. Betz, R. E. Brown and G. Johnston; note especially Brown, NTS 13 (1966/67) 113–32. Certainly the term παράκλτος in this v must be interpreted in the light of its appearance in John’s Gospel; although here it refers to Jesus and there (as in 14:16) to the Spirit. This variation was pointed out by C. H. Dodd (BJRL 21 [1937] 146–48) as further evidence for driving a wedge between the Fourth Gospel and 1 John. However, the ultimate significance of παράκλητος in the two Johannine contexts may not differ very greatly. We note, for example, the following: (a) If we allow that the Spirit of God is also the Spirit of Jesus (cf. Acts 16:7; Rom 8:9; Phil 1:19), then theologically the word “Paraclete” can refer to the ministry of either the Spirit or Jesus. (b) John 14:16 refers to “another Paraclete,” which suggests that Jesus is the first. (c) Both here and at John 14 (vv 12–14, 16, “I will ask the Father”), Jesus is said to act as an “intercessor.” And as Jesus intercedes for his own (v 1b), so the Spirit pleads the cause of Jesus before an unbelieving world (John 16:8). See further the comment on 2:2.

The intercessor we have with the Father is referred to as “Jesus Christ” (cf. 1:3). The double name almost certainly describes Jesus in his relation to man and God: as one with both, and therefore qualified to act for us. Although John is at this point still dealing primarily with the problems raised by the gnostically inclined secessionists, for whom he stresses the humanity of “Jesus,” he is also sensitive, probably, to the “low” christology typical of the Ebionitically inclined group within his church; and for these readers the exalted title of “Christ” would be eminently appropriate. See further the introduction, xxiii.

Jesus Christ is also called here δίκαιος (“righteous”). This predicate (“being righteous”) heightens the description of his ability to act as the sinner’s intercessor. He shares God’s nature and ours. But also, and because of this, he is the one who has “fulfilled all righteousness” (Matt 3:15; cf. also the formulation in Rom 10:4, Christ is “the end of the law,” which may have been used by John’s opponents—see 3:4—to justify their “lawlessness”). Therefore, on the basis of his own righteousness, manifested above all in the righteous act of the cross (cf. v 2), Jesus is supremely able to ask for that righteousness to be extended to all God’s children who are in fellowship with him. On that ground, also, God (who is himself “righteous,” v 9a) can “purify us every kind of unrighteousness” (v 9b; cf. Rom 3:26).

The term δίκαος, as applied to Jesus, is characteristic of John, especially in the context of Jesus considered as an example (cf. 2:29; 3:7; note also John 17:25; using πάτερ δίκαιε, “righteous Father”). There is a primitive and traditional background to this description, stemming from the use of ὁ Δίκαιος (“the Righteous One”) as a title for Jesus in the apostolic preaching (cf. Acts 3:14; 7:52). On the embryonically high christology implied in the kerygmatic usage of this title in Acts see Smalley, ExpTim 73 (1961/62), 359. On δίκαιος generally see further H. Seebass, NIDNTT 3 (1978), 360–65, especially 362; cf. also on 3:7–10.

2. God’s salvific work in the interests of sinful man has been disclosed in the first place, John says, by providing us with an intercessor (v 1). He now goes on to explain a second way in which God’s activity of salvation has been shown to man: the intercessor is also “the offering for our sins.” This extends the description of the risen Jesus Christ as (righteous) Paraclete (note the connecting καί, “and,” at the start of the sentence); and it also provides some further elucidation of John’s theology of atonement in the passage 1:6–10.

καὶ αὐτὸς ἱλασμός ἐστιν περὶ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν, “and he himself is the atoning sacrifice for our sins.” How is Jesus able to plead the cause of the sinner? John has already specified one qualification: his divinely righteous character (v 1). Now he mentions another: Jesus is also “an atoning sacrifice” (ἱλασμός) for our sins.

The term ἱλασμός occurs in the NT only here and at 4:10; although cognate words are used elsewhere: ἱλάσκεσθαι, “to propitiate” or “to forgive” (cf. Luke 18:13; Heb 2:17); ἱλαστήριον, “propitiation” or “expiation” (Rom 3:25; Heb 9:5); and ἴλεως, “propitious” or “forgiving” (Matt 16:22; Heb 8:12). Exact translation in the case of each of these terms is difficult, because of the theological and attendant semantic problems involved (see below on ἱλασμός).

The setting of this language is sacrificial; and to that extent the appearance of ἱλασμός in this v recapitulates and expands the reference to the “blood” (αἷμα) of Jesus, which purifies us from every sin, referred to in 1:7b. As in the earlier v, John’s allusion here to the sacrificial nature of Christ’s death (present, but only marginally, in the Fourth Gospel) may have included an antiheretical thrust. Against those with gnostic inclinations who believed that the life and death of Jesus were unreal, or who were concentrating on the cross of Christ as a glorified exaltation alone (and thus misreading the fourth evangelist’s evidence), the writer insists that the death was a reality, and that its effect constituted an “atoning sacrifice.” See further the introduction, xxvii, and the comment on 1:7; cf. also 3:16 and 4:10.

The primary background to the concept ἱλασμός in 1 John (and of its cognates elsewhere in the NT) is Jewish and OT. But its precise meaning is debated. When the word ἱλασμός appears in pagan writing (e.g. Plutarch), it clearly denotes an offering made by worshipers to “propitiate,” or placate, the anger of the gods (ἱλασμὸς τῶν θεῶν, “propitiation of the gods”; sometimes it occurs in the singular, ἱλασμὸς τοῦ θεοῦ “propitiation of the god”) whom they have displeased.

However, its use in the LXX, and thus in 1 John, has called forth two major interpretations from scholars. For example, Westcott (85–87), followed by Dodd (Bible and Greeks, 82–95, especially 94–95), favors a “subjective” meaning, which is in fact suggested by the RSV translation of ἱλασμός at this point as “expiation.” See further Lyonnet, “Noun hilasmos,” 148–55. This signifies that God is not the object of an offering for sin, but its subject; in NT terms, he has brought about the ἱλασμός in Christ Jesus (cf. Rom 3:25); and this amounts to saying that he has removed the defilement of sin (which is now the object of the action), and caused it to be “covered” (the probable root meaning of the Hebrew verb kipper, translated in the LXX as ἱλάσκεσθαι). This accords with the meaning of ἱλασμός (= the Hebrew seliḥah, a “sending away”) in some parts of the OT (cf. in the LXX Ps 130:4, “with you there is forgiveness”; Dan 9:9, “the Lord our God is merciful and forgiving”). Dodd (25) suggests that such a meaning was also present, if rarely, in pagan writers. The significance of ἱλασνός here, on this showing, would be that sin is “neutralized” by the offering of Christ’s life, once the believer is united with him (Westcott, 87).

On the other hand, scholars such as Hill (Greek Words, 23–48; see 36–37 on 1 John 2:2 and 4:10) and Marshall (117–19) regard ἱλασμός in a more “objective” light, taking account of the element of “propitiation” (the word used in this v by the AV translators) which they find in this term. See also Nicole, WTJ 17 (1955) 117–57. According to this interpretation God is the object of an offering for sin, rather than its point of origin. The grounds for following such an interpretation are that in the OT “the idea of placating the wrath of God or some other injured party is often present when the word-group in question is used” (Marshall, 118). The meaning here would then be that “Jesus propitiates God with respect to our sins” (note the triple use of περί, “for,” or “concerning”; and cf. 4:10; Heb 5:3; 1 Pet 3:18). Certainly it is possible to find passages in the LXX where ἱλασμός is used in the context of an offering made by the worshiper to God, rather than the other way round (cf. Ezek 44:27, “he shall offer his sin offering”; note also 2 Macc 3:33, “the high priest was performing the rite of atonement”). For a survey of the views held by this school see N.H. Young, EvQ 48 (1976) 67–78.

Possibly these two interpretations of the term ἱλασμός, one in which God is the subject of the action of sin-offering, and one in which he is the object, need not be regarded as mutually exclusive. In the OT, as we have seen, both senses prevail; and the same is true when, in the LXX, ἐξιλάσκεσθαι (“to atone”) is used (cf. Zech 7:2; 8:22; Mal 1:9, the only occasions where this verb is used with an “objective” meaning; and 2 Kings 5:18, one of several “subjective” uses of the verb ἱλάσκεσθαι). Theologically it is in any case true that God is the initiator of the Jewish principle and pattern of sacrifice for sin, as he is of the surrounding framework of the Law (cf. Lev 16). But he also receives that sacrifice, so that atonement may be made “for all the sins of the Israelites” (Lev 16:34). Similarly, in the NT, it is possible to argue from such passages as Rom 3:21–26 that God both “presents” the “offering” of his Son (v 25, using ἱλαστήριον) as a means of the sinner’s justification, and also “receives” it, so that he can justify “the man who has faith in Jesus” (v 26).

Perhaps we should not use Paul to interpret John. Nevertheless in 1 John 2:2 (cf. 4:10) both senses of ἱλασμός also seem to be present. Jesus is regarded in this passage as a heavenly intercessor (v 1), pleading the cause of the sinner and asking for his pardon. He does this, and can do it effectively, because he is righteous, and is “himself” (αὐτός) the offering (not the offerer!) for our sins (cf. v 7b). To this extent God is the object of the saving action. But he is also the subject, since in v 9 we learn that the forgiveness and purification of the sinner ultimately stem from the Father: he is righteous, and on the basis of the Son’s offering he will forgive our sins. Here, then, is not contradiction but complementarity. John is declaring that the source of the offering is God the Father, but that the means are to be found in God the Son; and our translation, “atoning sacrifice,” attempts to capture both senses. In his life, but supremely in his death and resurrection, in the “entire work of Christ” (Dodd, 27), salvation (initiated by God) is achieved for the believer. Moreover, this work possesses a continuing quality. Jesus “is” (ἐστιν), now and always, the offering for our sins. See further Thornton, ExpTim 80 (1968/69) 54–55, who argues for a balanced interpretation of ἱλασμός in 2:2, dependent on the exegesis of παράκλητος (“intercessor”) in v 1 as “pacifier” (then ἱλασμός includes a propitiatory meaning) or “spokesman” (when ἱλασμός conveys an expiatory connotation).

On “sins” in this v, denoting particular sinful acts, see the comments on vv 8 and 9; see also 1:7.

οὐ περὶ τῶν ἡμετερων . . . περὶ ὄλου τοῦ κόσμου. As in the Gospel of John, the scope of divine salvation is ultimately regarded as all-inclusive. The fourth evangelist describes Jesus as the “Savior of the world” (4:42; cf. 3:16); and here John refers to him as the one whose “atoning sacrifice” relates to the sins of “the whole world.” The adjective “whole,” (περὶ) ὄλου, is intensive. The sacrificial offering of Christ is effective not just for the sins of the “world” (which could refer to a section of it), and still less for “our” sins (those of John’s immediate circle) alone; it embraces the sins of the whole world. (Note the construction οὐ μόνον . . . ἀλλὰ καὶ, “not only . . . but also”.) On the term κόσμος (“world”), meaning “the earth” as the habitation of mankind and the sphere of God’s salvific activity, see the comment on 2:15; also Haas, Handbook, 56–57.

The repeated περί (“for”) in this final phrase makes it clear that the sins of the world are the concern of the “atoning sacrifice” made by Jesus, rather than “the world” in general (against Westcott, 45). John’s teaching provides an encouragement for the believer who sins, but also a challenge to the heretic who claims to be sinless.
Explanation

In the section 1:8–2:2 John is dealing very practically with his first main purpose in this letter, which is to urge his readers to respond to the good news of God as light (v 5) by living in the light (v 7). The primary method of achieving this, he demonstrates, is by renouncing sin; and, indeed, to encourage such a renunciation is one of the chief purposes of this document (2:1). In all this, however, particular notice is taken of John’s gnostically inclined opponents, who were minimizing the effects of sin or denying its presence altogether. By way of reply to these heretics, the writer completes a trilogy of heterodox claims and orthodox counterclaims (1:6—2:1) by indicating the reality of both sin and its remedy. In the face of God’s righteousness, human unrighteousness is a fact of life. But God has not deserted us in our moment of need. Even while the believer tries genuinely to “live in the light” sin is a possibility; and in that case God has provided in his Son, our intercessor and atoning sacrifice, the possibility of forgiveness and new life (2:1–2). This is not an excuse for sin, but rather a universal and challenging assurance that we can be delivered from its power, if not its presence, through the offering of Jesus.

Here is the essence of the gospel which, as indicated in his preface (1:1–4), John wished to share with all his readers, and in particular perhaps with those who, influenced by the heretical group in his church, were trying to distort the truth by espousing an inadequate christology (including “high” or “low” views of the person of Jesus), or by practicing a wrong ethic (in this instance, insisting that righteousness is unimportant).

There are timeless implications in John’s teaching here. He speaks to any non-Christian who wishes to discount the importance of sin, and to any Christian who maintains the possibility of becoming sinless in this life. In both cases the Johannine position is a thoroughly biblical and sensible one. We cannot avoid the fact of sin, but we can avoid its practice. If from time to time we do not, there is hope for the sinner—since God’s forgiveness has been made possible through his Son, Jesus Christ. He is the one who enables us continually to “live in the light.”

Form/Structure/Setting

There are definite links between this section and the previous one. In the first place, the subject matter is continuous. The author is explaining to the members of his church, in answer to developing heretical tendencies, the nature of true Christian belief and practice, and the way in which these interact. To do this he first chooses as his theme and for his exhortation the necessity of “living in the light” (1:5–7). The first (negative) condition required for a genuinely Christlike existence, the writer suggests, is the renunciation of sin (1:8—2:2). The second (positive) condition he now proceeds to discuss: it is obedience, especially to the law of love (2:3–11). “Though the immediate effect of the light is to expose sin, its primary purpose is to reveal duty” (Law, Tests, 209).

In addition to the connections in terms of content, between 2:3–11 and what precedes it, there are literary points of contact. The motif of “light,” for example, with its contrast of “darkness,” is introduced at 1:5 (which begins the complete section 1:5—2:29), and appears again, for the last time in 1 John, at 2:8–11. Similarly, John cites and refutes in this passage (vv 4, 6 and 9) claims which were apparently being made, without justification, by ex-members of his community. The statement of these claims parallels, even in points of language, the claim to sinlessness mentioned in the preceding section (cf. 1:6, 8, 10). See further the comments below.

At the same time there are changes, in both thought and tone, between this section and the last. The notion of “fellowship” with God and God’s people, and how this can be created (1:8—2:2), gives place to a presentation of the importance of “knowing” and “loving” God and one’s fellow-Christians (see vv 3–4, 5, 10). Furthermore, there is a change in the writer’s approach to his readers, as he becomes increasingly hortatory (cf. especially v 7) and less polemical. For the literary structure of these verses see also the comments on vv 7 and 8.

O’Neill, Puzzle, 16–19, sees v 8 as a “Christian comment” which contradicts v 7 and modifies vv 9–11, where light is presented as an alternative option to darkness, rather than its conqueror. When v 8 is removed from 2:7–11, O’Neill argues, we are left with a clearly defined piece of (Jewish) teaching “which can be split up into regular lines” (18). There is, however, no evidence to support this theory; and although v 8 provides a contrast to the vv surrounding it, its theology (against O’Neill) is consistent with the overall teaching of this passage (see the comment below). Nevertheless, we may agree with O’Neill that the general structure of vv 9–11 is quasi-poetic.
Comment

3. Obedience is necessary if the believer is to “live in the light.” As Law says (Tests, 209–10), “to have fellowship with God, we must not only acknowledge what the light reveals as true; we must realize in action what it reveals as right.” Here is one “test” by which the profession of Christian commitment may be judged.

Evidently some of John’s former adherents, especially those with docetic inclinations, were in error doctrinally (they thought of Jesus as divine, but not human); and ethical error followed as a result (they regarded righteousness as unimportant). For the connection here see the introduction, xxiii–iv. Against such an antinomian position the writer is advancing the thesis that Christian behavior must be consistent with all that God has disclosed to us of himself: his absolute nature as “light” and “life,” and his self-giving in the historical Jesus (cf. 1:1–2, 5). In the first four vv of this section (vv 3–6), therefore, John takes up the question of moral obedience, and its importance, as the first standard by which spiritual integrity may be measured. Law (Tests, 210, n. 3) regards this opening paragraph as logically formulated: with a thesis (v 3), antithesis (vv 4–5a), and a restatement of the thesis (vv 5b–6).

καὶ ἐν τούτῳ γινώσκομεν, “and this is how we can be sure.” The phrase τούτῳ γινώσκομεν (literally, “in this we know”) is a typical formula in 1 John, where it appears, with variations, on ten occasions (cf. v 5; 3:16, 19, 24; 4:2, 13; 5:2; also 2:18; 4:6).

In this v we find the first of 25 occurrences in 1 John of the verb γινώσκειν (“to know”). The noun γνῶσις (“knowledge”), as such, is not used in this letter or in the Fourth Gospel; although in both documents the verb “to know” (γινώσκειν) is common, and the concept of “knowledge” (of God) is pervasive (cf. 1 John 2:13; John 17:3). This linguistic usage is probably not significant theologically, and it may simply reflect the influence on John’s Gr. of the Semitic tendency to employ verbs wherever possible in place of the cognate noun. At the same time, however, John may have wished to avoid a technical term which could be misused or misunderstood (cf. Smalley, John, 48).

During the first Christian centuries a wide range of religious movements sought to provide an answer to the basic problem of man and the world by concentrating on salvation through a secret γνῶσις (gnōsis, or “knowledge”). The term “Gnosticism” is used to describe these early, philosophical preoccupations with “knowledge.”

Gnosticism is, in fact, not so much a single religious system as a climate of abstract, speculative thought. Until this century its ideas were known to us solely through the writings of the early church fathers, who regarded gnostic movements as heretical corruptions of the Christian gospel which arose from a Greek (dualist) environment. Christian Gnosticism in its developed form (as we know it, for example, from the complex and sophisticated systems of the second century thinkers Basilides and Valentinus) certainly appears to be entirely Hellenistic in origin and character. But while it is true that the Graeco-Roman environment in which the gospel was cradled would have fostered gnosticizing tendencies once they had penetrated Christianity, modern scholarship is now suggesting that the movement itself entered the Hellenistic world from the Orient, even before the Christian era, and then proceeded to influence many cults and philosophies within the Mediterranean civilization, including Judaism (Qumran) and emergent Christianity.

These earlier forms of gnostic thinking (sometimes called “pre-gnosticism”; so Reicke, NTS 1 [1954/55] 141) seem to have infected some primitive Christian believers. Paul, for example, was forced to resist tendencies of this kind in Corinth and Colossae (cf. 1 Cor 1:22–23; Col 1:15–20); and the writer’s choice of language at this point in 1 John suggests that he was also encountering a similar kind of outlook in his church. On Gnosticism/Gnōsis see further R. Bultmann, TDNT 1 (1964) 689–714, especially 711–13; E. D. Schmitz, NIDNTT 2 (1976) 392–406; Smalley, John, 49–51, and the literature there cited; Schnackenburg, 95–101. See also Yamauchi, Pre-Christian Gnosticism.

Apparently the majority of the secessionists had been attracted by gnosticizing tendencies in some form (see on 1:6). The background to this group, like that of Gnosticism itself, may have been either Greek or Jewish (cf. Col 2:8, 16). But the predominant character of Christians who would have been drawn to such speculative religious thinking in first century Asia Minor is likely to have been Hellenistic, and therefore (in this case) docetic. The error of these people was twofold. Theologically they were pursuing intellectual “knowledge” for its own sake; and ethically they regarded sin as unimportant. (Gnosticism in any form is seldom concerned with moral behavior; redemption for the gnostic involves a release from ignorance, not sin.) In both respects, the theological and the ethical, John’s teaching provides an orthodox counter.

γινώσκομεν ὄτι ἐγνώκαμεν αὐτόν, “we can be sure that we know him.” The writer begins by using, and emphatically repeating, the language of the gnostics themselves: literally, “we know that we know.” The reason for this choice of terminology is not that John shared the assumptions and tendencies of Gnosticism (so Houlden, 65). Rather, as with Paul on occasions (cf. Col 2:9, answering those who denied the reality of the Incarnation), John takes up the terms and ideas of his heretical readers and fills them with a truly Christian content. “Knowledge” of God in the biblical sense is not intellectual and speculative, but experimental and dynamic. Moreover, the context in which man’s knowledge of God becomes possible is not abstract, but historical (cf. Jer 31:34; Matt 11:27; John 1:18). In other words, to “know” God is not a matter of correct thought-processes, but of a genuine spiritual relationship. The knowledge of God, and fellowship with him, are complementary aspects of Christian experience. Here is the starting point for something which is intended to grow and develop; thus when the writer speaks of “knowing God” in this v he uses the perfect tense (ἐγνώκαμεν; literally, “we have known”), which implies a past experience with continuing effects. When John says “we can be sure that we know him (ἐγνώκαμεν αὐτόν),” the pronoun αὐτόν presumably refers to God (cf. “fellowship with him,” μετʼ αὐτόυ, in v 6). But the sense of “God in Christ” (as a lawgiver) need not be excluded from this context; for Jesus has been in mind in the preceding two vv, and he is responsible for the “new law” of love (vv 7–11).

ἐὰν τὰς ἐντολὰς αυτοῦ τηρῶμεν, “if we obey his orders.” But how can we be “sure that we know him”? To answer this question John moves from a theological issue (the proper aim of “knowledge” should be a right relationship with God) to an associated question of ethics. A simple test by which we may all measure our faith, our knowledge of God (and John is closely identifying himself with his readers at this point), is the extent of our moral obedience. Do we obey God’s orders? A related question is whether our obedience is continuous (note the use of the present tense, τηρῶμεν, “we obey,” in this phrase).

Here is the test; and it is expressed conditionally: “we can be sure that we know him if we (habitually) obey his orders” (cf. Hos 4:1–2). But just as the “amount” of one’s knowledge of God is less important than the direction of that knowledge (whether one knows God at all), so the “amount” of one’s obedience to God’s orders cannot determine the extent of the believer’s knowledge of him. John is not saying that the Christian must be perfectly obedient before he can in any way know God, and be rightly related to him; for he has already claimed that no one is sinless (1:8—2:2). Obedience is not the condition for knowing God; but “obeying orders” should characterize that knowledge (normally, if not without exception), and may be the means of testing it (cf. Bultmann, 25).

The moral law of God as a whole is represented by the “orders” (ἐντολάς) to which John refers, and to which (he says) obedience is necessary. The background to the concept of God’s “commands” is to be found in the OT. The law of God (given to Moses) is contained in the Decalogue (cf. Exod 20:2–17), which epitomizes the Jewish Torah. In the NT this is summarized positively by Jesus in terms of love: for God, and for other people (Matt 22:37–40; cf. Rom 13:8–10; Gal 5:14, and the “new commandment” of love referred to in John 13:34; see also v 8, and 2 John 4–6). The expression τηρεῖν τὰς ἐντολάς (τὸν λόγον) (“to keep the commandments (or the word)”) is characteristically Johannine; in the NT it is found elsewhere only in Matt 19:17; 1 Tim 6:14 (cf. 1 Cor 7:19). In 1 John, see v 4; also 3:22, 24; 5:3. On “command” (“law”) in John, see further H. -H. Esser, NIDNTT 1 (1975) 337–39. See also Maurice, 69–86; Haas, Handbook, 45. For the term ἐντολή (“command”) itself cf. 2:4, 7, 8; 3:22, 23, 24; 4:21; 5:2–3.

4. This v introduces the first of three claims which, the writer indicates, may be made by any Christian. Unlike the three versions of the heretical assertion about the unimportance of sin, specified in 1:6, 8 and 10 (using ἐὰν εἴπωμεν, “if we claim”) these claims (in vv 4, 6 and 9, employing in each case the formula ὁ λέγων, “anyone who says”) are positive assurances which may be adopted by the true believer. All three statements are presented in the third person singular (“anyone who says”); and the first two (vv 4 and 6) are preceded by the use of the first person plural (γινώσκομεν, “we know”) in vv 3 and 5. This switch from the plural to the singular suggests that a principle (such as “we know God,” or “we exist in him”) is being applied, and tested, in the life of the individual believer.

The claims in question may be set out in this way:

4. Anyone who says, “I know him,”

(must be obedient, v 5).

6. Anyone who claims to abide in him,

must live as Jesus lived.

9. Anyone who claims to be in the light,

(must love his brother, v 10).

Two points are evident from the table above. One is that these three statements are really three versions of the single Christian claim to know God, in the sense of being properly related to him (through Christ). To this extent they develop the thought about the true “knowledge” of God introduced (in opposition to gnostic error) in v 3. Thus the potentially true statements in vv 4, 6 and 9, dealing with the right relationship between man and God, parallel the false statements in 1:6, 8 and 10, dealing with the reality of sin which may interrupt that relationship.

Secondly, to each version of the claim about knowing is attached a condition which is also a test. Once more, the conditions are versions of the primary Christian obligation of obedience (similarly introduced as characteristic of a right relationship with God in v 3). To obey God necessarily involves Christlikeness (v 6), and also love, which is the summation of God’s moral law (v 9).

It is possible, as we shall see, that all three versions of the claim to know God were deliberately taken up by members of John’s church from the Fourth Gospel itself, and used without regard to the need for obedience in the spiritual life. These Christians may have arrived at their view of Jesus by distorting aspects of the Fourth evangelist’s christology (see the introduction, xxvi–vii); and the same may have been true of their ethical application of Johannine theology.

ὁ λέγων . . . ἐντολὰς αὐτοῦ μὴ τηρῶν. V 4 clarifies the statement in v 3, and expresses it in negative terms. Knowledge of God necessarily implies obeying his orders; therefore to claim such a relationship, without the determination to obey God’s moral demands, axiomatically indicates that the claim is false. This appears to have been the position of the secessionists from the Johannine community and their like-minded followers. The first version of the claim to know God uses the typical formula ὁ λέγων (“anyone who says”), followed by a citation of the slogan in direct speech (ὅτι, “that . . .”; in vv 6 and 9 ὁ λέγων is followed by an infinitive). The slogan echoes the pattern and content of the assertion in 1:6 (“if we claim to share fellowship with him, while living in darkness, we are lying”). But sins of omission may be in mind here; whereas in 1:6 sins of commission are being attacked by the writer (so Marshall, 124).

The cry, “I know him,” possibly derives from such a description of Christian experience as the one given by Jesus, according to John 17:3, in his prayer of consecration: “this is eternal life: that they may know you (γινώσκωσιν σέ), the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom you have sent.” On this basis a Christian might well say, “I know him”; but, without the necessary consequence of moral obedience, the claim would be a false one (cf. Matt 7:21). The pronoun αὐτόν (“him”) may refer to God, or Christ, or God in Christ (cf. αὐτόν in v 3; note also the theological formulation in John 17:3).

The phraseology at this point in v 4 is closely related to the statement about knowing God in v 3 (even to the use of the perfect tense ἔγνωκα, literally, “I have known,” denoting a past experience with present results). However, the claim to “know him,” especially if it is derived from the Fourth Gospel, may well have given rise to the positive statement in v 3, rather than simply being an echo of that v here. At the same time the expression καὶ τὰς ἐντολὰς αὐτοῦ μὴ τηρῶν (“but disobeys his orders”) clearly inverts the phrase in v 3, ἐὰν τὰς ἐντολὰς αὐτοῦ τηρῶμεν (“if we obey his orders”; note the repeated present tense of the verb, indicating habitual obedience or disobedience). For the terminology here see the comment on v 3.

ψεύστης ἐστίν, καὶ ἐν τούτῳ ἡ ἀλήθεια οὐκ ἔστιν, “is a liar, and the truth has no place in this person.” The assessment of the worth attaching to a claim to know God, when obedience is not present, is devastating. To assert that man is sinless makes God out to be a “liar” (ψεύστην, 1:10). But to profess a relationship to God without morality is to be a “liar” (ψεύστης) oneself. This judgment is intensified, as in 1:8 (cf. vv 6, 10), by the addition of the phrase καὶ ἐν τούτῳ ἡ ἀλήθεια οὐκ ἔοτιν (“and the truth has no place in this person”). Presumably the linguistic echoes of the earlier trilogy, 1:6, 8 and 10, are deliberate.

The demonstrative ἐν τούτῳ, “(the truth has no place) in this person,” is typically Johannine (cf. v 5; John 5:38). Certainly the placing of ἐν τούτῳ is emphatic and specific. In such a (disobedient) man, the writer seems to be saying, the truth as an “active principle. . . .regulating his thoughts and judgments” (Westcott, 48) cannot be present. Cf. 2 John 4; 3 John 3–4. As in 1:6 and 8, and perhaps again in opposition to gnosticizing tendencies, the word “truth” (ἀλήθεια) itself is deliberately chosen by the writer to describe a “genuineness” which is neither philosophical nor abstract, but rather practical and concrete. (Cf. the description of God in this sense at Exod 34:6–7.) On “truth” in John, as a soteriological concept, see Aalen, SE II (1964) 3–24; see also the comment on 1:6, 8.

5. The proper response to the Christian’s knowledge of God is obedience to God’s commands. Without this an individual’s claim to know God may be disputed. In setting out this train of thought in vv 3–6 John preserves a careful symmetry. He has already made it clear that right behavior is an indispensable part of Christian experience; and he has done this in terms which are both positive (v 3) and negative (v 4). Now he restates his theme positively (v 5a; cf vv 5b–6), and in contrast to the suggestion of disobedience m v 4 (note the adversative δέ, “but”).

ὃς δʼ ἂν τηρῇ αὐτοῦ τὸν λόγον, “anyone who obeys his word.” As usual, the writer is not content merely to repeat what has been said already. In v 3 the test of knowing God is described as “obeying his orders” (ἐὰν τὰς ἐντολὰς αὐτοῦ τηρῶμεν). Here the reference is widened to include obedience to God’s word: αὐτοῦ τὸν λόγον (“his word”). The subject of the pronoun αὐτοῦ (“his”) is probably God. However, as throughout this passage, Christ cannot be excluded from its full sense; especially in view of John 14:21–24, where the expression “keeping my commandments (ἐντολάς)” is similarly broadened to “keeping my word (λόγον),” and the subject throughout is Jesus himself.

The true Christian is one who responds positively and continuously (note the present tense of τηρῇ, literally, “keeps”) both to God’s moral law and to the totality of his self-disclosure in Jesus Christ. The placing of the pronoun αὐτοῦ (τὸν λόγον), “his (word),” compared with the phrasing in v 3, (τὰς ἐντολάς) αὐτοῦ, “his (orders),” may underline the personal associations which John is giving to the concept of God’s λόγος (cf. Westcott, 48). In thus expanding the object of Christian obedience (God’s word, and not only God’s law), John may have had an eye to those of his readers with a Jewish background (Ebionites?) who were exalting the place of the law, and reverting to legalism in the process. (On the use and meaning of “word,” see the comments on 1:1 and 10.)

The sense of this phrase in v 5 (ὃς δʼ ἂν τηρῇ αὐτοῦ τὸν λόγον, “anyone who obeys his word”) is more inclusive than its parallel in v 3; and the sentence as a whole is also introduced in an indefinite, and therefore in a broader, manner. John does not associate himself with his readers, as in v 3 (γινώσκομεν “we can be sure”), or specify an individual case, as in v 4 (ὁ λέγων literally, “the one who says”). Rather, he makes the statement in a completely general form: ὅς δʼ ἄν . . . ,” anyone (who obeys his word).”

ἀληθῶς ἐν τοὐτῳ ἡ ἀγάπη τοῦ θεοῦ τετελείωται, “God’s love has really reached fulfillment.” The writer now moves forward the thought of v 3 (echoed in v 4) even further. The actively obedient Christian does not simply know God; in him God’s love really reaches its fulfillment. John possibly uses ἀληθῶς (“really”) at this point to distinguish sharply a false claim to the knowledge of God (such as he discovered in v 4) from its positive counterpart. In this person (ἐν τούτῳ again; see v 4), who obeys God’s word, God’s love is really fulfilled.
