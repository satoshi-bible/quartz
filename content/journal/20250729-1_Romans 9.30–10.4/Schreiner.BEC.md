B. Israel’s Rejection of God’s Saving Righteousness (9:30–11:10)
V. 	God’s Righteousness to Israel and the Gentiles (9:1–11:36)
   	A. 	God’s Saving Promise to Israel (9:1–29)
   	➤ B. 	Israel’s Rejection of God’s Saving Righteousness (9:30–11:10)
   	C. 	God’s Righteousness in His Plan for Jews and Gentiles (11:11–32)
   	D. 	Concluding Doxology (11:33–36)

In verses 6b–29 Paul has argued that God’s word and promises do not fail, because they depend on him alone. He is fulfilling his promises in saving gentiles and a remnant in Israel. In the first part of this section (9:30–10:21) Paul examines the matter from another perspective: Israel’s failure to believe. Here he cites the Scriptures extensively to demonstrate that Israel’s unbelief was part of God’s plan (R. Longenecker 2016: 835). From this section we learn that Paul viewed divine sovereignty and human responsibility as complementary rather than contradictory truths. They are not mutually exclusive but are compatible. Paul provides no philosophical resolution as to how they correlate, and it is probably best to acknowledge that they relate mysteriously, in a way that exceeds our finite understanding. In any case, it would be a serious mistake to appeal to divine sovereignty as if it diminished the genuineness of human freedom and responsibility.

At this juncture in salvation history God’s saving righteousness manifests itself in the salvation of many gentiles, while only a few from Israel believe. Paul asks why the gentiles have obtained right standing with God in such large numbers, while so many in Israel have fallen short (9:30–33). His answer is that the gentiles have drawn near to God by faith, while Israel sought to keep the law and thereby attain righteousness by works instead of faith. Romans 10:1–4 continues and expands on similar themes. The people of Israel have a genuine zeal for God, but they tried to establish their own righteousness instead of submitting to God’s righteousness. In verses 5–8 Paul argues that righteousness by the law is fundamentally opposed to righteousness by faith. The former consists fundamentally of doing, while the latter believes what God has done in Christ. Thus those who belong to the people of God, whether Jews or gentiles, believe the gospel message that Jesus is Lord and has been raised from the dead. This message is universally available and universally proclaimed so that all who call on the Lord’s name will be saved.

What is necessary to believe the gospel of Christ? Paul rehearses the steps in 10:14–17. One must hear the message to be saved, hearing does not happen without proclamation, and proclamation depends on one being sent to proclaim. Even though faith depends on hearing the message, hearing alone doesn’t guarantee belief. Some who listen to the message fail to believe. Indeed, much of Israel (vv. 18–21) falls into that category. They have heard the message and disbelieved as the gospel has been proclaimed to the ends of the earth, while gentiles have believed and become part of God’s people. The inclusion of the gentiles fulfills the prophecy that God would provoke Israel to jealousy by another nation and that he would reveal himself to those who did not seek him. Nonetheless, God continues to stretch out his hands to Israel, imploring them to be saved. Still Israel obstinately resists God’s gracious advances. By citing these OT prophecies, Paul reveals that it was always God’s plan to provoke Israel to jealousy by including gentiles within the realm of his blessing. The events of history are not surprising to God.

But if Israel is stubborn and recalcitrant, can we conclude that God has rejected his people (11:1)? Paul emphatically rebuffs such an inference (11:1–6). The presence of a remnant testifies that God has not cast off the people on whom he set his covenant love. By God’s electing grace, there has always been a remnant throughout history. The very presence of a remnant indicates that God will fulfill his promises to Israel in the future. Verses 7–10 summarize the argument of 9:30–11:6. What the majority of Israel has sought—right standing with God—has not been given to them. They have not received it because they tried to be right with God by works rather than by faith. God’s electing work has brought some to salvation from both Jews and gentiles, but the majority of the Jews have been hardened by God. This hardening, Paul maintains, is in accord with what the OT teaches. Paul presently ministers in an era in which Israel is hardened and only a remnant is being saved.


1. Israel’s Unbelief in Christ (9:30–10:13)

The outworking of salvation history is unexpected because gentiles are being included in God’s saving purposes, whereas the Jews are largely left outside. This is all the more surprising, since the Jews possess the law to instruct them about God’s will. Israel has wrongly used God’s law, however, thinking that they could exploit it to establish their own righteousness instead of believing in Christ and submitting to the saving righteousness of God (9:30–10:4). Indeed, Paul posits a radical discontinuity between doing and believing (10:5–8). Vindication before God comes from believing in Christ; it is not based on doing the prescriptions of the law. This message of faith is universally available to all, both Jews and gentiles (10:9–13). All those who call on the Lord will be saved, whether they are Jews or gentiles. Since there is only one Lord, there is only one way of salvation for all peoples: through faith in Jesus Christ.


a. Israel’s Failure to Obtain Righteousness (9:30–10:4)

Paul summarizes the state of affairs from 9:6b–29 and draws a conclusion. Surprisingly, the gentiles who did not seek right standing with God have received it by faith. By contrast, Israel, which zealously pursued God’s law to have a right relation with him, did not realize their goal. Why has Israel failed? Paul answers this question in verse 32: they pursued the law in the wrong way, “as from works” instead of by faith. Therefore they fell over God’s stone (Christ) by failing to believe in him. Romans 10:1–4 continues the argument, despite the chapter division.1 In verse 1 Paul reiterates (cf. 9:3) his desire for Israel’s salvation. He acknowledges that they are zealous for God but faults them because this zeal is not informed by knowledge (v. 2). Verse 3 explains (γάρ, gar, for) why their ignorance is culpable. Israel refuses to submit to God’s saving righteousness for two reasons. First, they lack knowledge about God’s saving righteousness. That this lack of knowledge is not merely the lack of necessary information is communicated in the second reason. Israel rejects God’s saving righteousness because they desire to establish their own righteousness. An implied proposition (see below) probably connects verses 3–4. Israel should have subjected themselves to God’s saving righteousness, since Christ is both the goal and the end of the law, with the result that righteousness belongs to all who believe.

Exegesis and Exposition

9:30What shall we say then? That gentiles, who did not pursue righteousness, obtained ⌜righteousness⌝, even the righteousness that is by faith, 31but Israel, although it pursued the law for righteousness, did not attain the ⌜law⌝. 32Why? Because they did not pursue the law by faith but as from ⌜works⌝. They fell on the stone of stumbling, 33just as it is written, “Behold I am laying in Zion a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense, and the ⌜one who believes⌝ in him will not ⌜be put to shame⌝.” 10:1Brothers and sisters, the desire of my heart and my prayer to God for ⌜them is⌝ for their salvation. 2For I testify about them that they have a zeal for God but not according to knowledge. 3For they did not subject themselves to the righteousness of God, because they were ignorant of the righteousness of God and because they sought to establish their ⌜own⌝. 4For Christ is the goal and end of the law resulting in righteousness for everyone who believes.
[9:30]

The first major movement in defense of the trustworthiness of God’s word is completed.2 The second movement is contained in 9:30–11:10.3 It commences with the diatribal question τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν (ti oun eroumen? What shall we say then?)4 This phrase (6:1; 7:7; 9:14) or a similar one (9:19) is used to anticipate and counter mistaken inferences one could deduce from Paul’s preceding comments. The phrase is also used as a transitional device to move to the next stage of the argument (4:1) or to establish conclusions from the preceding context (8:31). Both of these uses seem to fit here. The argument moves to the next stage, yet the conclusions that can be drawn from the preceding verses, especially verses 24–29, are elucidated. In other words, Paul wants to investigate further the implications of what he has just stated, that God has elected many gentiles unto salvation while choosing only a remnant of Jews. Now he contemplates the exclusion of Israel, which is his primary concern, and the inclusion of the gentiles from the perspective of the gospel (rightly D. Moo 1995: 244–45; against E. Johnson 1995: 227). The rest of verses 30–31 could be interpreted as a question, since the question τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν; is often followed by another question (cf. 4:1; 6:1; 7:7; 8:31; 9:14; cf. 6:15; so Fitzmyer 1993c: 577). In this case, however, identifying 9:30b–31 as a question is unlikely, for the question would then be terribly long. That verses 30b–31 constitute a statement rather than a question is strengthened by the question διὰ τί (dia ti? Why?) at the beginning of verse 32, since this question would most sensibly follow an assertion.

In verse 30b Paul considers the situation of the gentiles, and then in verses 31–33 he explains the condition of the Jews; the latter is Paul’s main concern in this context. The statement in verse 30b is rather surprising. Gentiles who did not pursue a right relationship with God have obtained a right standing with him.5 The verbs διώκειν (diōkein, to pursue) and καταλαμβάνειν (katalambanein, to obtain, seize) derive from the race course, signifying the pursuit toward a goal and the procurement of the same.6 Philippians 3:12–14 is an instructive parallel, for there the verbs διώκειν (pursue) and καταλαμβάνειν (obtain) describe Paul’s pursuit to obtain his eschatological goal (cf. 2 Tim. 2:22). The assertion that the gentiles did not pursue righteousness (μὴ διώκοντα δικαιοσύνην, mē diōkonta dikaiosynēn) doesn’t imply that all gentiles were devoted to moral debilitation. Many gentiles may have been zealous for moral excellence, but they were not striving for a right relationship with the one true God.7 How is it that those who did “not pursue” a right relation with God obtained it? An additional phrase is appended (δικαιοσύνην δὲ τὴν ἐκ πίστεως, dikaiosynēn de tēn ek pisteōs, even the righteousness by faith) to clarify how gentiles obtained right standing before God: by faith.8 It would be misleading, however, to interpret this answer in such a way that Paul leaves behind the previous discussion in Rom. 9.9 The ultimate reason why gentiles, who were not even pursuing a right status with God, exercised faith is the electing word of God that cannot fail (9:6) since election is not based on the will or effort of the one chosen (9:16).10 The reference to faith shows that human response is imperative, but the ultimate source of such faith is God’s merciful election.
[9:31]

In verses 31–33 attention shifts to the Jews, who pursued the law for righteousness but did not attain righteousness via the law. The verbs “pursue” (διώκειν) and “attain” (φθάνειν, phthanein) extend the race imagery from the previous verse.11 Gentiles who did not engage in the race have obtained the prize of a right relationship with God, whereas the Jews, although they energetically pursued the law for righteousness, did not attain to what the law demanded and fell short of reaching the goal.12 The precise wording of the verse is unusual, for we expect Paul to say that the Jews pursued “the righteousness of the law” (δικαιοσύνην νόμου, dikaiosynēn nomou), but instead the text says “the law of righteousness” (νόμον δικαιοσύνης, nomon dikaiosynēs; see the additional notes). Some interpreters conclude that in any case the meaning is not affected, and that the phrase is an example of hypallage and is equivalent to “righteousness of law” (e.g., Calvin 1960: 217; RSV; Schmithals 1988: 363; Hofius 1990: 24–25; 2002b: 162).13 But this suggestion is unpersuasive since it skirts the precise wording of the verse and rewrites its grammar, and Paul could have easily written δικαιοσύνην νόμου (righteousness of the law; cf. Rom. 10:5; Phil. 3:9; so Cranfield 1979: 507; Badenas 1985: 103).

How then should the genitive δικαιοσύνης be understood?14 Does the text refer to “the righteous law,” a law that promises righteousness, demands righteousness, results in righteousness? Or to a law that is falsely understood as a means of righteousness? The adjectival translation “righteous law” can be set aside, for it is unlikely that δικαιοσύνη is used in a different sense in verses 30–31 than elsewhere in the context. Since the word δικαιοσύνη (righteousness) refers to a right relation with God in verse 30, it likely has the same meaning in verse 31 as well. Similarly, the notion that the phrase refers to a false way of righteousness can be rejected, since the notion of falsity would need to be supplied by Paul if he intended such a complex thought, but here the expression is terse. It is difficult to believe that Paul would say that the law promises or results in righteousness, since elsewhere he argues (cf. 1:18–3:20; cf. Gal. 2:16–21; 3:6–4:7) that the law exposes and reveals sin.15 Saying that the law demands or requires righteousness makes sense. We could also translate the phrase as “for righteousness” (cf. B. Martin 1989: 137), which means Israel was seeking the law to obtain a right relationship with God.16 Still, this isn’t much different from saying that the law demanded righteousness to stand in the right before God.

Although Israel pursued the law for a right relation to God, they “did not attain the law” (εἰς νόμον οὐκ ἔφθασεν, eis nomon ouk ephthasen). It seems probable that δικαιοσύνην (righteousness) is the object of the verb ἔφθασεν (attain) and that εἰς νόμον (the law) is a predicate accusative.17 Verses 30 and 31 stand in antithetical parallelism to each other, and the verbs καταλαμβάνειν (obtain) and φθάνειν (attain) are rough synonyms. The gentiles obtained righteousness, and Israel failed to attain the law. The imagery of the race and the failure to attain the goal (cf. Schreiner 1991b: 213, 220) suggest that in verse 31 Paul faults Israel for not keeping the law.18
[9:32]

In verse 32 Paul continues to examine what was going on with Israel by asking, “Why is it that Israel did not attain the law?”19 The answer is communicated with a causal ὅτι (hoti, because). Israel didn’t attain to the law’s righteousness “because” they pursued the law “not by faith but as from works” (οὐκ ἐκ πίστεως ἀλλ᾽ ὡς ἐξ ἔργων, ouk ek pisteōs all’ hōs ex ergōn). Verse 32 contains an ellipsis, and thus the verb must be supplied. Most commentators agree that the verb “pursue” (διώκειν) should be inserted, since it occurs in verse 31.20 Käsemann (1980: 277) inserts the verb “live,” but this is improbable, since it does not even appear in the preceding context; it is probably an example of reading 10:5 into 9:32. Wilckens (1980: 212) suggests the verb φθάνειν (attain), which has the virtue of being the verb that immediately precedes verse 32. But this term would suggest that they “attained” righteousness by works, which would contradict Paul’s theology. T. Gordon (1992) posits that the copula should be understood, and thus the verse would say that Israel did not obtain righteousness because the law is characterized by works rather than faith.21 He observes an interesting parallel with Gal. 3:12, for there Paul says that “the law is not of faith” (ὁ δὲ νόμος οὐκ ἔστιν ἐκ πίστεως, ho de nomos ouk estin ek pisteōs). The identical words οὐκ ἐκ πίστεως are present in Rom. 9:32, and the phrase ἐξ ἔργων (ex ergōn, of works) occurs in both Gal. 3:10 and Rom. 9:32. Still, Gordon’s suggestion should be rejected because the parallel he establishes between Rom. 9:32 and Gal. 3:12 breaks down: in the Romans verse the word “as” (ὡς, hōs) is added. Käsemann (1980: 278) rightly comments that the presence of ὡς (as) is a striking feature in the verse, denoting the subjective attitude of Israel toward the law.22

The point is not that the law is deficient in that it is intrinsically characterized by works rather than faith. Instead, Israel is faulted for the way and manner in which they approached the law. Thus the verb “pursue” is the most fitting verb to include. Israel was not misguided to pursue the law, and no criticism of the object of their pursuit is forthcoming, since keeping the law in the OT was not contrary to faith but an expression of faith in Yahweh, who had established a covenant with Israel and redeemed them from slavery (cf. Exod. 19:3–6; 20:1–17). What is problematic is the way in which they pursued the law, that is, “as from works” so as to establish their own righteousness (cf. Rom. 10:3). Those who pursued the law in faith would, therefore, believe in Christ, since they would perceive that the law pointed to him.

This text is disputed in scholarship as part of the recent debate on Paul’s view of the law. Some scholars contend that here Israel is criticized only for failing to believe in Christ,23 but this effectively mutes the voice of verse 32 and is guilty of reading the text only through the lenses of salvation history (rightly Gundry 1985; cf. Hübner 1984a: 71–74; D. Moo 1996: 619). Nor is it persuasive to limit the critique to sociology, so that the Jews are criticized for excluding gentiles from the covenant by erecting the boundary markers of Sabbath, circumcision, and food laws.24 Paul uses ἔργα (works) generally here, and there is no hint that the discussion is restricted to or focuses on those segments of the law that separated Jews from gentiles.25 Israel is not censured for trying to impose the law on gentiles but for pursuing the law “as from works” (v. 32); that is, the fault of Israel is that they were trying to gain righteousness via works.26

Verse 32b is asyndetically connected to verse 32a.27 Contrary to those who perceive only a salvation-historical critique of Israel, there is probably an implicit “therefore” joining verses 32a and 32b. The reason Israel fell on the stone of stumbling, that is, failed to exercise faith in Christ, is that they were trying to achieve righteousness by works (so Hübner 1984a: 66). The Jews who heard the gospel and rejected it focused on achieving instead of believing. The identification of the “stone” as Christ is not immediately evident in verse 32, but it is confirmed by the citation of the OT in verse 33.
[9:33]

The OT citation is a conflation of Isa. 8:14 and 28:16, although it does not match either of those verses exactly (for a more extensive discussion see C. Stanley 1992: 119–25). The MT of Isa. 28:16 doesn’t say that one will avoid shame but that one “will not be in haste,” and thus Paul depends on the LXX tradition.28 The use of the word τίθημι (tithēmi, put) in the Pauline version (cf. also 1 Pet. 2:6) emphasizes divine predestination. God is the one who appointed the stone over which the Jews would fall and be scandalized.29 The disbelief of the Jews, then, is placed under the umbrella of God’s sovereignty, without any intimation that their responsibility for unbelief is lessened (cf. Müller 1964: 36; Murray 1965: 42; Käsemann 1980: 276). The text of Isa. 8:14 that Paul uses is closer to the revisions of Aquila and Theodotion than to the LXX, although it does not match any of them precisely.30

Paul’s use of both Isaiah citations is in line with the general meaning of the OT text. Those who trust in Yahweh will find him to be a shelter and security in the midst of judgment (Isa. 8:11–15; 28:14–22), but those who place their trust elsewhere will face judgment (so also Oss 1989: 192). Paul identifies the stone as Jesus, and this is reflected elsewhere in Christian tradition (1 Pet. 2:6–8; Barn. 6.2–4; cf. Mark 12:10–11 = Matt. 21:42 = Luke 20:17; Acts 4:11). Some scholars have suggested that the common use of this theme supports the idea of a written collection of texts that identified the stone with Jesus (cf. Selwyn 1981: 268–77; Lindars 1961: 177–79), but the variations among the texts cited support an oral collection at most (Käsemann 1980: 279). In any case, the idea that the stone here refers to the Torah can be confidently rejected.31 The very same citation of Isa. 28:16 in Rom. 10:11 obviously refers to faith in Jesus (rightly Rowe 2000: 142–44), and the tradition of the stone referring to Jesus in Christian tradition points to the same referent. There is some evidence that the “stone” texts were understood messianically in Jewish circles. The Targum of Isaiah is adduced by some as evidence (Dunn 1988b: 583; Jeremias, TDNT 4:272), but the dating of that targum (fourth century AD) is too late to be persuasive (Fitzmyer 1993c: 580).32 More promising is the reference to the stone in Dan. 2:34, probably interpreted by Josephus in messianic terms (Ant. 10.10.4 §210), which made Josephus reluctant to convey to the Romans his understanding of the text. A messianic understanding may also be reflected in 1QS 8.7, since the Qumran covenanters perceived Isa. 28:16 to be fulfilled in their community. The placement of Jesus as a stone in Zion suggests that he is the new temple, the place where God dwells with his people, and at the same time there is a hint of Christ’s resurrection (Seifrid 2007: 651).

The assertion that the one who believes in Christ “shall not be put to shame” (οὐ καταισχυνθήσεται, ou kataischynthēsetai) should not be interpreted psychologically. It means that the one who puts his faith in Christ will be vindicated in the judgment (Käsemann 1980: 279; Byrne 1996: 314). Thus the πρόσκομμα (proskomma, stumbling) and σκάνδαλον (skandalon, offense) of verse 33 refer to the eschatological judgment (so Jeremias, TDNT 4:276), and the two lines of the citation are an example of antithetical parallelism. Those who detect a salvation-historical slant in this text rightly discern that Israel is faulted for failing to believe in Christ, but this disbelief cannot be separated from the anthropological error of trying to gain righteousness by works (so Aageson 1987: 61–62).33 Indeed, the failure to discern the significance of salvation history is rooted in anthropology. It is precisely because they desired to achieve their own righteousness that they failed to believe in Christ, for believing in Christ gives all the glory to God, while observing the law means that glory and praise accrue to human beings.
[10:1]

The connections between 9:30–33 and 10:1–4 should be traced. Israel’s “zeal for God” (v. 2) corresponds to pursuing the law for righteousness in 9:31. The attempt to establish their own righteousness (10:3) parallels the effort to gain righteousness by works rather than by faith (9:32).34 The stumbling over the stone of stumbling (9:32–33) is described as the refusal to subject themselves to God’s righteousness. Before reiterating and augmenting the themes of 9:31–33, Paul interjects his personal response to the plight of Israel. The desire of Paul’s heart and his prayer on behalf of Israel is that they would experience salvation. These words evoke 9:1–3, reminding us that the issue that informs these chapters is the salvation (or lack thereof) of Israel.35 Some have contested that the word εὐδοκία (eudokia) can properly be rendered “desire” here (Sanday and Headlam 1902: 282; Schrenk, TDNT 2:746), but ample evidence supports this translation, and it is the most suitable rendering in this context (Ps. 145:16 [144:16 LXX]; Sir. 39:18; cf. Sir. 18:31; 2 Thess. 1:11; so Cranfield 1979: 513; Dunn 1988b: 585; Fitzmyer 1993c: 582).

It is also said that praying for the salvation of Israel undercuts a strong predestinarian interpretation of Rom. 9 (Leenhardt 1961: 264; Dunn 1988b: 586). This hardly follows logically, since prayer is one of the means God has ordained to accomplish his purposes. Moreover, as the unfolding argument reveals, Paul’s prayer for the salvation of Israel is based on the irrevocable promise that God would ultimately save Israel (11:26). The promise that Israel would surely be saved did not move Paul to forsake prayer. Rather, the promise that Israel would be saved was the foundation and bulwark for his prayers. Similarly, Daniel prayed fervently for release from exile (Dan. 9) precisely because God had promised to liberate Israel after seventy years (Jer. 25:11–12; 29:10). The idea was foreign to Paul and Daniel that one could abjure prayer since God had already predicted what would occur. God’s promises provide the encouragement and stimulus for prayer (cf. also Schlatter 1995: 212). Divine sovereignty and human responsibility are correlative rather than antithetical.
[10:2]

The reason (γάρ, gar, for, v. 2) Paul prays for the salvation of Israel is that they have a zeal for God.36 Paul was doubtless reflecting on his own life as a Jew, a life fueled by zeal for the Torah and for the one God of Israel (Gal. 1:13–14; Phil. 3:4–6; cf. Acts 9:1–5; 22:3–5; 26:4–5).37 In this regard Paul and other Jews were following a venerable tradition of zeal for God that was illustrated in the life of Phinehas (Num. 25:6–13; Sir. 45:23–24; 1 Macc. 2:26, 54; 4 Macc. 18:12), Simeon and Levi (Jdt. 9:2–4; cf. Jub. 30.4–20), Elijah (1 Kings 19:10, 14; Sir. 48:2; 1 Macc. 2:58), and the Hasmoneans (1 Macc. 2:19–27, 50, 54, 58; Josephus, Ant. 12.6.2 §271). Such zeal for the Torah also characterized the Qumran community (1QS 4.4; 9.23; 1QH 6 [14].14). Paul does not dispute the authenticity of Israel’s zeal for God. He faults them for lack of knowledge, and the deficiency of knowledge is explained in the next verse. Note the γάρ connecting verses 2–3 and the word ἀγνοοῦντες (agnoountes, not knowing) beginning verse 3.38
[10:3]

The defect in the Jews’ knowledge is explicated with the two participial clauses of verse 3. Both the participles “not knowing” (ἀγνοοῦντες) and “seeking” (ζητοῦντες, zētountes) are causal, modifying the main verb “they subjected themselves” (ὑπετάγησαν, hypetagēsan) and explain why Israel did not subject themselves to God’s saving righteousness.39 The first reason given is that they were ignorant of “the righteousness of God” (τὴν τοῦ θεοῦ δικαιοσύνην, tēn tou theou dikaiosynēn). The righteousness of God here refers to the gift of righteousness given to believers. The parallels between Rom. 10:1–5 and Phil. 3:2–9 support a forensic reading, since the texts are so similar. (1) Both refer to God’s righteousness. (2) In both Paul contrasts righteousness by law and righteousness by faith. (3) According to Philippians, Paul had tried to establish his own righteousness; in Romans, Israel tries to do the same. (4) Paul renounces “a righteousness of my own that comes from the law” (Phil. 3:9), and Israel is trying to establish its own righteousness (Rom. 10:3), a “righteousness that is based on the law” (Rom. 10:5; cf. here Byrne 2000: 302). (5) Romans 10:3 speaks of τὴν τοῦ θεοῦ δικαιοσύνην (the righteousness of God), while Phil. 3:9 appeals to τὴν ἐκ θεοῦ δικαιοσύνην (tēn ek theou dikaiosynēn, the righteousness from God). The only material difference between the two phrases is the ἐκ (from). It is quite doubtful, given the remarkable parallels between the two texts, that God’s righteousness has a distinct meaning in Rom. 10. The use of ἐκ in Phil. 3:9 clarifies (given the parallels) the meaning of the genitive τὴν τοῦ θεοῦ δικαιοσύνην (the righteousness of God) in Romans. In both instances, righteousness is a gift from God.

Israel was ignorant that God’s saving righteousness was a gift of his grace, and thus instead of trusting God in Christ for their righteousness they sought to establish “their own” (ἰδίαν, idian) righteousness. Some have argued that the word ἰδίαν signals the Jewish preoccupation with their own righteousness over against the gentiles.40 Thus it is asserted that the critique is directed against a nationalism and ethnocentrism that excludes gentiles by erecting boundary markers (such as circumcision, Sabbath, and food laws) so that gentiles cannot be coheirs in the people of God. Israel surely had a sense of national superiority, since they were specially elected to be God’s people out of all the peoples of the world, and it could easily lead to pride. But this text doesn’t concentrate on Israel’s unique status among the nations. Instead, Paul indicts Israel because it “sought to establish” (ζητοῦντες στῆσαι, zētountes stēsai) its own righteousness. This is most naturally interpreted to mean that Israel continued to devote itself to all that the Torah commanded to prove itself worthy before God.41 Remarkably, Paul doesn’t mention any of the boundary markers here. Thus Israel didn’t subject itself to the saving righteousness of God, since it failed to grasp that righteousness is a gift of God’s grace and mistakenly thought it could secure righteousness by observing the Torah.42
[10:4]

Romans 10:4 is one of the most controverted verses in Pauline literature.43 The word τέλος (telos, end) is a predicate nominative, even though it precedes the word Χριστός (Christos, Christ), and it is moved up to the front of the verse for emphasis. The meaning of the term τέλος has been the subject of considerable debate. The two main options are to translate it as either “goal” or “end.”44 Badenas has mounted a vigorous defense of the interpretation “goal,” maintaining that this was the nearly universal view in the history of the church (1985: 7–37) and that lexically the meaning “goal” is demanded by the evidence (1985: 38–80).45 On this reading the text would say that Christ is the goal of the law so that righteousness is available to all who believe. The translation “goal” is also defended from the racing illustration in 9:30–33, yielding the conclusion that Christ is the goal of the race, and the Jews stumbled over him but the gentiles obtained the goal.46 The translation “goal” certainly yields a coherent sense in the context. Israel attempted to establish its own righteousness by works, but it should have perceived that Christ was the goal to which the law pointed.

We have seen that the word τέλος may mean “goal,” but there are also good arguments for the definition “end.”47 One Pauline text indisputably yields the meaning “goal” (1 Tim. 1:5), and in two texts the meaning is “fully,” “completely,” or “to the end” (2 Cor. 1:13; 1 Thess. 2:16), while on three occasions it denotes the end of history (1 Cor. 1:8; 10:11; 15:24). In four instances the translation “outcome,” “final destiny,” or “result” is appropriate, referring to the end result of evil works (Rom. 6:21; 2 Cor. 11:15; Phil. 3:19) or of a life of righteousness (Rom. 6:22). Both “end” and “goal” would fit in 2 Cor. 3:13, and the meaning of τέλος there is disputed. I conclude that an either-or isn’t necessary in Rom. 10:4, and thus both “end” and “goal” are probably intended.48 If one thinks of a race, when one reaches the goal, then one also has reached the end. Jesus Christ is the goal and purpose of the law in that the law points to him.49 At the same time, he is the end of the law. When Christ came, the goal of the law was met in salvation history, and the law was set aside as a covenant. Believers are no longer under the provisions of the Mosaic covenant.50 The result of Christ being the goal and end of the law is that now right standing with God is available for all who put their faith and trust in him.

It is unlikely that Paul intends to say here that the exclusive and nationalistic dimension of the law is set aside with the coming of Christ.51 The passing away of the Mosaic covenant certainly means that the nationalistic character of that covenant is no longer in force, but since Paul doesn’t refer to this dimension of the covenant in context, it is unlikely that he has this sense in mind. Others have argued that the law has ceased with the onset of the messianic era.52 Certainly, this is one dimension of what Paul teaches here, though it is disputable whether Paul is in harmony with other Jewish traditions that make this point, since the Jewish evidence adduced is contested. We can say that Paul believed the law was set aside with the coming of the Messiah since he is the goal to which the law pointed.

Some scholars make a general statement about the law ending as a way of salvation, and now salvation is through Christ. It is often unclear whether the intention is to say that there is a salvation-historical shift between the Testaments or whether two different ways of salvation are taught, one based on the law and the other based on the gospel. The latter idea should be excluded, since Paul teaches that salvation has always been by faith (cf. Rom. 4:1–8; Gal. 3:6–9). However, it is certainly the case that a salvation-historical shift is taught in the Pauline Letters to the effect that the Mosaic covenant is no longer in force (cf. Schreiner 1993b: 124–33).53 Moreover, the text implies a salvation-historical shift, since faith in Christ is the means of salvation (cf. 9:32–33; 10:4).

The salvation-historical reading doesn’t rule out the anthropological dimension of the text. The Jews refused to submit themselves to God’s righteousness because they were ignorant of it and attempted to establish their own righteousness. Verse 4 is attached to verse 3 with a γάρ, demonstrating that there is a logical relationship between the two verses. An implied proposition must be supplied, since it does not make sense to say “The Jews did not subject themselves to God’s righteousness” because Christ is the end and goal of the law. Nor does it work much better to read the text to say “The Jews were ignorant of God’s righteousness and sought to establish their own righteousness” because Christ is the end and goal of the law. Supplying an understood proposition is not that difficult. Paul probably suggests that the Jews were wrong not to submit to God’s righteousness, since Christ is the end and goal of the law (cf. Sanday and Headlam 1902: 284; S. Williams 1980: 283–84).54

The interpretation proposed here understands εἰς (eis) as introducing a result clause.55 We see a similar use of the preposition twice in Rom. 10:10, where εἰς clearly signifies result, and the parallel is quite striking, since he uses the exact phrase εἰς δικαιοσύνην (resulting in righteousness). Some object that in the sentence εἰς δικαιοσύνην is closer to Χριστός (Christos, Christ) than it is to τέλος γὰρ νόμου (telos gar nomou, for the end of the law; Cranfield 1979: 519–20; Sanders 1983: 61; Badenas 1985: 116). But the latter phrase is moved up front for emphasis, and Seifrid (1985: 9) argues rightly that εἰς δικαιοσύνην (resulting in righteousness) is related not to all that precedes but only to the predicate nominative τέλος νόμου.

Additional Notes

9:30. The inclusion of τήν before δικαιοσύνην specifies the righteousness in question, but it is clearly secondary, being attested only by 𝔓46 and G.

9:31. The scribal insertion of δικαιοσύνης (ℵ2, F, Ψ, 1881, Majority Text) after the second use of νόμον could be explained by an accidental reversion to the previous νόμον δικαιοσύνης in the verse or an attempt to harmonize with the first phrase. The textual evidence also solidly favors NA28 (𝔓46vid, ℵ*, A, B, D, G, 6, 81, 945, 1506, 1739, 2464, pc). Some have conjectured that δικαιοσύνην should be read instead of νόμον. The word “righteousness” is what we would expect in this context, and thus, on the principle of lectio difficilior, the conjecture is quite improbable. No scribe would have inserted the difficult νόμον to replace the easily comprehensible δικαιοσύνην.

9:32. The insertion of the word νόμου after ἔργων (ℵ2, D, Ψ, 33, Majority Text) accords with Rom. 3:20, 28; Gal. 2:16; 3:2, 5, 10, and is what we would expect in a discussion of the relationship of the Jews to the law. Its omission here is the more difficult reading and is also well attested (ℵ*, A, B, F, G, 6, 629, 630, 1739, 1881, 2464, pc), and thus the reading of NA28 is certainly original. The omission of νόμου here is exegetically significant, for it indicates that the emphasis here is on “works” instead of the “law” (see the exegesis and exposition).

9:33. The inclusion of πᾶς before the participle ὁ πιστεύων is an assimilation from Rom. 10:11, where Isa. 28:16 is again cited and no manuscript omits πᾶς.

9:33. Some Western witnesses (D, F, G) assimilate the text to the LXX of Isa. 28:16 with the reading οὐ μὴ καταισχυνθῇ.

10:1. The Majority Text clarifies the text by substituting τοῦ Ἰσραήλ for αὐτῶν (see my translation) and by inserting the verb ἐστιν. Other manuscripts (ℵ2, P, Ψ, 33, 1505, 2495, pc) clarify the grammar by inserting ἐστιν after αὐτῶν. In both instances the changes represent later alterations by scribes, for the external evidence favoring αὐτῶν alone is overwhelming (𝔓46, ℵ*, A, B, D, F, G, 6, 365, 1506, 1739, 1881).

10:3. The manuscript evidence is evenly divided as to whether to retain δικαιοσύνην (𝔓46, ℵ, F, G, Ψ, 33, Majority Text) or omit it (A, B, D, P, 81, 365, 629, 630, 1506, 1739, 1881). It was probably added later for clarification, but the meaning is not affected either way, since the word is implied even if it is a later addition.
