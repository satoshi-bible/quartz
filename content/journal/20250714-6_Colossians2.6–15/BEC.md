
B. Believers Should Live Only on the Basis of Christ (2:6–15)
III. 	Letter Body: Christians Should Strive for Maturity (1:24–4:6)
   	A. 	Paul Rejoices in His Struggle to Proclaim Christ (1:24–2:5)
   	➤ B. 	Believers Should Live Only on the Basis of Christ (2:6–15)
   	C. 	Believers Should Not Be Required to Submit to Extraordinary Religious Practices (2:16–23)

Here is a fuller statement of the main idea of this unit: Believers should live only on the basis of Christ and not any other belief system, since Christ came in the fullness of deity in bodily form, resulting in his redemption of them through his death, resurrection, and triumph over their enemies.

This section, anticipated by 1:24–2:5, circles back to the main point of 1:3–23, which is to “walk . . . worthy of the Lord” (v. 10a). The goal of 1:24–2:5 is that believers be “complete in Christ” (1:28), which is achieved by them not being “deluded” by false teaching (2:4), so they can maintain “good discipline and the stability of faith” (2:5). And this is the basis of “walking in” Christ in 2:6, which explicitly expands on “walking” worthily in 1:10a. This expansion is observable from noticing that both 1:10–12 and 2:6–7 have a series of four adverbial participles (beginning with an agricultural metaphor) that modify “walk” and conclude with a mention of thanksgiving (D. Moo 2008: 179–80; likewise O’Brien 1982: 106; Sumney 2008: 128; Pao 2012: 156). Here in 2:6–15 we will see that Paul refers back in other ways to 1:10–2:5 to elaborate on what he has said in that context.

Paul begins this section by commanding the listeners to “walk” in Christ in the same way (by faith) that they received Christ. They are able to “walk” in Christ because they have been initially “rooted . . . in Him” at the inception of their belief, and on that foundation they continue to be “built up in Him and established” (v. 7a). When they first believed, they were taught these truths and “overflowed with thanksgiving”* (v. 7b) for them.

They are to “walk worthily” (1:10a; 2:6) not only by focusing on the truth (2:7) but also by not being led astray by “philosophy and empty deception,” which is based on false worldly views and not on Christ (v. 8). Instead, they should base their lives (their lifestyle “walk”) only on Christ (or be “captivated” by Christ instead of false teaching; v. 8b). From various angles everything else in the remainder of verses 9–15 supports this proposition of “walking only on the basis of Christ” (vv. 6b and 8b) as the main logical point of all of verses 6–15.

The readers are to found their lives on Christ because he is the only one in whom “Deity dwells in bodily form” (v. 9). Verses 10–11 explain the threefold effects of Christ being the one in whom “all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form” (v. 9b): (1) believers “have become filled in Him”* (v. 10a), (2) “He is the head over all rule and authority” (v. 10b), and (3) “in Him [they] were also circumcised” (v. 11a).

Then the entire remainder of the segment (vv. 11b–15) flows out of the last effect of being “circumcised.”1 Christians were circumcised “by the circumcision of Christ, which occurred by “having been buried with Him in baptism” (vv. 11b–12a). By means of this baptism they were also “raised up with” Christ (v. 12b). Then the next verse adds that, despite their sin, their resurrection (being “made alive”) was a direct result of God “having forgiven them” their “transgressions” (v. 13) and “having canceled out the certificate of debt” against saints (v. 14a). Verse 14b explains further what it means that this “certificate of debt” has been “canceled”: God “has taken it out of the way [by] having nailed it to the cross.” Verse 15 concludes the segment by progressing on to mention an effect of forgiveness at the cross (vv. 13–14), explaining also an effect of the earlier point from verse 10b that Christ “is the head over all rule and authority”: because Christ is over all rulers, through the cross he was able to defeat and triumph over the evil, satanic “rulers and authorities.”

Thus everything in Col. 2:6–15 revolves around the main point of “walking only in Christ” (v. 6b, reiterated in v. 8b). The “in Him” (Christ) in verse 6 sets the tone of the Christ-centered discussion throughout verses 6–15 (generally observed by commentators), where the phrases “Christ” (v. 8b), “in Him” (vv. 6, 7, 9, 10, 11), “in whom”* (vv. 11, 12 [?], 15b [?]), and “with Him” (vv. 12 [2×], 13; and three σύν [syn] verbs)2 occur. These references are a strand that links all of these verses together, not only to focus on Christ but also on the believers’ corporate identity with Christ. This emphasis on Christ is set in antithesis to the non-Christ-centered false teaching.

Exegesis and Exposition

6Therefore as you have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him, 7having been firmly rooted and now being built up in Him and established in your faith, just as you were instructed, and overflowing with gratitude. 8See to it that no one takes you captive through philosophy and empty deception, according to the tradition of men, according to the elementary principles of the world, rather than according to Christ. 9For in Him all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form, 10and in Him you have been made complete, and He is the head over all rule and authority; 11and in Him you were also circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, in the removal of the body of the flesh by the circumcision of Christ; 12having been buried with Him in ⌜baptism⌝, in which you were also raised up with Him through faith in the working of God, who raised Him from the dead. 13When you were dead ⌜in⌝ your transgressions and the uncircumcision of your flesh, He made ⌜you⌝ alive together with Him, having forgiven ⌜us⌝ all our transgressions, 14having canceled out the certificate of debt consisting of decrees against us, which was hostile to us; and He has taken it out of the way, having nailed it to the cross. 15When He had disarmed the rulers and authorities, He made a public display of them, having triumphed over them through Him.
[2:6]

The initial “therefore” (οὖν, oun) indicates that the preceding context provides the basis for the command to “walk.” The focus of this basis is Paul’s desire that the Colossians become “complete in Christ” (1:28) by disregarding false teaching and maintaining “stability of faith” (2:5) in true Christian teaching. They have “received Christ Jesus the Lord.” The word “receive” (παραλαμβάνω, paralambanō) often has the idea of believers receiving a Christian tradition that has been “delivered” to them (e.g., 1 Cor. 11:23; 15:1, 3; Gal. 1:9, 12; Phil. 4:9; 1 Thess. 2:13; 4:1; 2 Thess. 3:6; following Bruce 1984: 93). Many commentators insist that this is the thought here.3 On this basis, some contend that the idea primarily is not “referring to receiving Christ Jesus as one’s personal Lord” (Pao 2012: 155). The “receiving,” however, does seem to include committing themselves personally to Christ as their Lord (so also O’Brien 1982: 105). The element of personal commitment is reflected by 1:10, where “to walk worthy of the Lord” is “to please him in all respects,” though 1:7 says that they “learned” the gospel tradition “from Epaphras,” teaching that has come indirectly through Paul (cf. 1:23–28). The giving of “thanks” in 1:12 and 2:7 also focuses on a personal relationship with Christ. Of course, in the context of Col. 1–2 (e.g., 1:6–7, 23, 28), what they believed about Jesus has been passed on to them and is part of the apostolic tradition about Jesus (as confirmed from such parallel passages as 1 Cor. 15:1–2; Phil. 4:9; 1 Thess. 4:1, on which see further below). That in “receiving Christ,” they were also receiving the “tradition” about him is evident from 2:7, where reference to their “faith” is directly followed by “just as you were taught.”* And this received tradition is set in immediate contrast to the false “tradition of men” (2:8; cf. Abbott 1897: 244). Thus, “receiving Christ” is equally a personal and an intellectual commitment (cf. Schweizer 1982: 124–25; MacDonald 2000: 88), though the notion of a passed-on tradition is “subordinated to the . . . idea that what the missionaries had passed on to their converts was Christ himself” as a person (Caird 1976: 189; likewise Barth and Blanke 1994: 300–301, 311; Lincoln 2000: 620).

As we concluded in 1:1, “Christ Jesus” is no mere name without interpretative significance but reflects to some degree the OT expectation of a Messiah (cf. also Barth and Blanke 1994: 302), who is now identified as Jesus of Nazareth (the article before “Christ” may enhance this point; Harris 2010: 80; contra Sumney 2008: 127n1). Some think that the words “the Lord” that immediately follow receive greatest emphasis in this verse. It is argued that when the title comes after a transitive verb, it tends to function predicatively: “Christ Jesus as Lord” (so O’Brien 1982: 106; D. Moo 2008: 178–79; followed by several translations: NLT, NIV, NET; HCSB; cf. NJB). The parallels of 2 Cor. 4:5 and Phil. 2:11 are offered in support (though the latter has a different order of words and both lack the article before “Lord”). Some translations, however, render merely “Christ Jesus the Lord” (KJV, RSV, NRSV, ESV).4 Regardless of which translation is best,5 it is likely correct to identify this name for Christ as part of a reference to the early confession “Jesus is Lord.” Colossians so far has identified this confession as Christ being “the image of the invisible God” (1:15), “the firstborn of all creation” (1:15), the creator of “all things” (1:16), the sustainer of “all things” (1:17), “[the] head of the body, the church” (1:18), “the firstborn from the dead” (1:18), the new temple (1:19), the “reconcile[r]” of all (1:20, 22), and “God’s mystery” (2:2; following in part D. Moo 2008: 179).

This early Christian confession is based on “Jesus” being the Messiah (“Christ”) and being the divine “Lord.” In 1:3 we have already observed that referring to “Jesus” the “Lord” is not a mere title of respect, applied to kings or masters, but to some degree a reference to “Lord” (κύριος, kyrios) in the LXX, which was the usual Greek translation of Yahweh in the Greek OT (cf. Fee 2007: 20–27, 179, 577–78, 631–38) and was a word also used by pagans of their gods. This is thus a reference that includes identifying Jesus with Yahweh of the OT.6 In other explicit NT expressions of “Christ” as the “Lord,” the emphasis also is on confessing Christ as Yahweh,7 so that he is identified with the unique God of the OT. “For Paul, to confess that Jesus is Lord is to confess that he is Yahweh” (Wallace 1996: 188). This part of the early confession is important to observe here, since most of what has been mentioned about Jesus in 1:15–2:2 has focused on his divine identity, and his deity will again be developed in 2:9 below. In light of this preceding and following context, it is probably correct to see “Lord” as the emphasis in 2:6.8 This emphasis on Jesus’s deity is likely a counter to the false teaching (mentioned in 2:4, 8), which focuses on other religious elements in addition to Christ in order to find and experience true wisdom and knowledge. Authentic wisdom and knowledge can be found only in the inexhaustible “fullness” of the divine Christ (see 2:3), which trumps all other philosophies, which are “empty” (2:8).

“Walk” is an obvious figurative expression for “lead one’s life” (a marginal reading in the NASB). The manner by which (ὡς, hōs) they must continue to “walk” is by the same faith in “Christ Jesus the Lord” by which they began their Christian existence (“as you have received”). Their initial trust in Christ brought them into union with him, and they are to continue to “walk in him,” that is, continue to live by faith in union with him (the ἐν, en, focuses on sphere).9 Thus the faithful manner by which they “received” Christ is the manner by which they are to continue to live. This continues the theme from earlier, where Paul repeatedly says that his readers have begun by “faith” (Col. 1:2, 4; 2:12) and continue to live by “faith” (Col. 1:2, 4, 23; 2:5, 7). Paul’s only other imperative of περιπατέω (peripateō, 4:5) in the epistle may indicate that part of the way believers are to “walk” is by having “wisdom toward outsiders,” “so that they may know how they should respond to each person” (4:6), including unbelieving false teachers.

As we have suggested, this verse is a direct development of 1:10a, where Paul exhorts believers to “walk in a manner worthy of the Lord.” The point there is that the saint’s ethical character is “to be fitting with” or matching the character of their “Lord” (Jesus) in order to “please” him. This enforces the notion that the manner of the “walk” in 2:6 is also the focus, which we have seen to be the function of the introductory ὡς (hōs). The manner by which Christ’s followers are to continue their lives is by the same faith as they began with Christ. The same notion of encouraging Christ’s followers to live now in line with the way “you” first “received [second-person aorist active indicative, παρελάβετε, parelabete] Christ” appears in Paul’s other epistles.10 The closest of these parallels (outside of Col. 1:10) is 1 Thess. 4:1, which calls believers “to walk” in the manner of faith they have exercised earlier. The parallel of Phil. 4:9 includes the same notion. These parallels reinforce that Col. 2:6 expresses the idea of walking in the same manner of faith. Perhaps not coincidentally, the parallel of 1 Cor. 15:1–2 is part of a defense against false teaching (15:12–19), which is also part of the context here.
[2:7]

That the hearers must be “firmly rooted and now being built up in him and established in . . . faith” reiterates that “stability of . . . faith” in true Christian doctrine (2:5) is the basis for the “walk” of verse 6.11 They have been “rooted” (perfect passive participle) in their Christian faith in the past (a past yet ongoing condition), and they are continuing to be “built up in him and established (present participles) in their faith.”12 “Rooted,” “built up,” and “confirmed” reflect a continuation of temple imagery from 1:23 (“firmly founded and steadfast and not moved”*; see the additional note on 1:23a).13 This may also anticipate the temple reference in 2:9 (see below). The point appears to be close to that in Rev. 3:11–12: “I am coming quickly; hold fast what you have, so that no one will take your crown. He who overcomes, I will make him a pillar in the temple of My God, and he will not go out from it anymore; and I will write upon him the name of My God, and the name of the city of My God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down out of heaven from My God, and My new name.” The point in Revelation, and here, is that the person who “overcomes” by faith becomes a part of the end-time temple and will be eternally secure in the salvific presence of God and Christ (see Beale 1999a: 294–96). The passive mood forms of the first three participles in verse 7 shows that it is God, not the Colossians, who is making them part of his temple, as in Rev. 3:12.14

There is also development of 1:23, where if the Colossians “continue in the faith firmly established and steadfast, and not moved away from the hope of the gospel,” they will be presented “holy and blameless and beyond reproach” on the last day (1:22). This anticipates the onslaught of the false teachers now explicitly mentioned in 2:4 and 2:8, by which the readers are not to be “moved” away from the truth, since the “gospel” is “truth” (1:5) in contrast to the false gospel of “deception” (2:8; on this latter contrast, see D. Moo 2008: 186). While “faith” here could well refer to the object of belief (O’Brien 1982: 108), it is perhaps preferable, as in 1:23 and 2:5 (on which see), to view “faith” here to refer to the exercise of a person’s own faith, and yet, of course, that faith is in true Christian doctrine about Christ.

The phrase “just as you were taught” refers not only to the directly preceding participles of 2:7 but also includes verse 6. Paul reminds the hearers that they were “overflowing with thanksgiving” in response to their “instruction.” This continues the theme of “thanksgiving” from 1:3 and 12, a theme that will be reprised in 3:15 and 17 and 4:2. This is thus a major idea in Colossians, conveying the notion of gratitude to God, especially because God is the source of their faith (e.g., 1:3–4; 2:6–7), their inheritance and redemption (1:12–14), and the other blessings that they possess.
[2:8]

In contrast to the reliable instruction summarized in 2:6–7, the hearers are to be wary (“see,” “be on the lookout for”) of anyone espousing any teaching or “philosophy” that contradicts the true teaching they “have received.”15 They are not to be “taken captive” by such people. They have been “delivered from the authority of darkness”* (1:13), and they are to guard against being taken captive again (Wall 1993: 105; likewise Garland 1998: 141–42). “Take captive” is probably the best translation of συλαγωγέω (sylagōgeō), since this verse is so parallel with Gal. 4:3, where people are said to have been “held in bondage under the elemental things of the world.” This last phrase also occurs in Col. 2:8 (Barth and Blanke 1994: 308). Commentators agree that Paul is not condemning all “philosophy” but only that which does not base itself on and line up with16 this faithful teaching. Such “philosophy” is “empty deception”17 for three reasons: (1) because it is based on “the tradition of men” and not on the “word of God” (1:25) or true “instruction” (2:7), which they have “received” (2:7); (2) because it is based on worldly doctrine (“the elements of the world”) and not on that which proceeds from heaven (cf. 3:1–2); (3) because it is not based on Christ. Rather, all knowledge is to be seen through the lens of Christ and him alone (Garland 1998: 159).

The content of this “philosophy” is “in conformity with” and “based on” (κατά, kata)18 “the tradition of men” and “the elementary principles of the world.” These sources of knowledge are now, at the end of verse 8, defined by what they are not: they are not based on and do not conform to the content of the teaching about Christ (not “according to Christ”). But what more can be said specifically about the content of these two sources of false knowledge? Paul stops short of further description in verses 9–15, where instead he wants to concentrate on the basis of the true teaching: Christ and his redemptive work. Then in verses 16–23 he returns to the deceptive philosophy that he has begun to introduce here in 2:8. In this concluding section to chapter 2, Paul will explain what he means by “the tradition of men” and “the principles of the world,” which are both mentioned again verbatim. We will find that the first phrase is part of an OT allusion (see on 2:22), and the second is best understood against an extrabiblical Hellenistic background (see on 2:20). Both backgrounds will unfold the defective content of the “philosophy” that Paul has introduced here.

Thus the verse ends by highlighting that this false teaching is not “in conformity with” nor “based on” (κατά, kata) the true teaching about “Christ.” This means it is a “distortion of the gospel” (Gal. 1:7) and is ultimately a “different gospel,” like the false gospel taught in Galatia (Gal. 1:6; so also 1:9; cf. Dunn 1996: 136). The vain philosophy based on “the tradition of men” and “the elements of the world” has replaced Christ as the source of truth, which, as we will see, is idolatry (see on 2:22).
[2:9]

The verse starts with “because” (ὅτι, hoti) to indicate that a reason is now beginning to be given about what the philosophy lacks by not being based on Christ. Positively, verse 9 implies an explanation of why believers should “walk in Him [Christ]” (v. 6) and be “captivated by” Christ instead of the false philosophy. In this respect, verse 9 introduces the remainder of the segment up through verse 15, which is distinct in that it contains either repeated reference(s) to Christ or the cross, thus showing the centrality of Christ in this explanation (O’Brien 1982: 111). The implicit repetition of “walk” from verse 6 at the end of verse 8 (walk “according to Christ” in contrast to walking according to the false teaching) is the main logical point of verses 6–15. This main point in verses 6 and 8 is supported by the large section of grounding in verses 9–15.

The main reason given for why the “philosophy” is so deficient and, implicitly, why believers should “walk” not according to that philosophy but “according to Christ” is that “in Him all the fullness of Deity dwells in bodily form” (v. 10). This clause has been the subject of much discussion.

The first observation in verse 9 is that the wording builds on Col. 1:19, most of which is word-for-word reproduction in Greek of 1:19:
Colossians 1:19 	Colossians 2:9
because in Him He was well pleased for all the fullness to dwell* 	because in Him all the Divine fullness dwells in bodily form*
ὅτι ἐν αὐτῷ εὐδόκησεν πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα κατοικῆσαι 	ὅτι ἐν αὐτῷ κατοικεῖ πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα τῆς θεότητος σωματικῶς
hoti en autō eudokēsen pan to plērōma katoikēsai 	hoti en autō katoikei pan to plērōma tēs theotētos sōmatikōs

We saw that 1:19 was an allusion to Ps. 67:17–18 LXX (68:16–17 Eng.): “God was well pleased [εὐδοκέω, eudokeō] to dwell [κατοικέω, katoikeō] in it [ἐν αὐτῷ, en autō (Zion)]; . . . the Lord will dwell [there] forever, . . . in the holy place [קֹדֶשׁ, qōdeš].”19 This allusion was applied to Christ in 1:19. The Ps. 67:17–18 LXX allusion carries over from 1:19 to 2:9.20 God’s holy of holies presence on earth was the most preeminent reality in all of Israel. So also the reason (ὅτι, hoti, 1:19) why Christ should “come to have first place in everything” (1:18b) is that he is the inaugurated end-time temple in which God’s fullness has begun to dwell (1:19). He is the escalated form of God’s presence, the true holy of holies, and as such he himself is identified with the unique tabernacling presence of God and is the most preeminent one together with God in the church and in all the cosmos. Now Paul applies this truth about Christ’s preeminence as the temple from 1:18–19 and places it in contrast to the false teachers, who do not base their pseudo “philosophy and vain deception”* on Christ. This is the first suggestion that the false teaching in Colossae has to do with not focusing on Christ as the true temple. We shall see that this will soon become explicit.

The idea in 1:19 is not that God’s presence is in Christ but rather “that all that God is dwells in Christ” (Sumney 2008: 74); and in 2:9, “it is not merely godlikeness that dwells in Christ, but actually the divine nature itself” (Sumney 2008: 133). In this light, since 2:9 is so verbally parallel with 1:19, the point of 2:9 is a reiteration of the point that Christ is the end-time temple in21 whom “the fullness of Deity dwells.” Whereas “Deity” was not mentioned in 1:19, it is expressed explicitly here to make more explicit “the fullness” referred to in 1:19. The phrase πλήρωμα τῆς θεότητος (plērōma tēs theotētos) is an attributive genitive: “Divine fullness” (possibly a genitive of content, “fullness which is deity”).22 And it is not just part of the “Divine fullness” that dwells in Christ, but “all” of it dwells in him (BDAG 829, s.v. πλήρωμα 3, renders the phrase as “the full measure of deity”). There is a complete “union between God and Christ” (Campbell 2012: 432). This is not only an ontological union but also a full identity with the unique God of the OT who inhabited Israel’s temple and would fully fill the end-time temple,23 the latter of which is the focus here. Christ is so identified with the complete tabernacling divine presence that he must be considered to be fully Deity himself. The repeated notion of God’s “glory filling the temple [or tabernacle]” is found in the Greek OT,24 and πλήρης (plērēs, full)25 is employed in some of these passages, where the temple is “full of the glory” of God (Isa. 6:1; Ezek. 43:5; 44:4; cf. Isa. 6:3). This background probably supplements the above-noted Ps. 67 LXX (68 MT, Eng.) temple allusion.26

The reference to “bodily form” also highlights that Christ’s divine presence has not taken up abode in a physical architectural structure, as in the OT, but in Christ’s own body as the new form of the temple (so D. Moo 2008: 193–94). Thus the reason why the false teaching of 2:8 is so defective is that it leaves out Christ, who is “the complete Divine fullness in bodily form.” That Christ is the one in whom “Deity dwells in bodily form”27 is also the reason why the Colossians should not pay attention to the false teaching and should “walk” (lead their lives) only on the basis of Christ. The false teaching is “empty,” in contrast to “all the fullness of Deity dwelling in” Christ. The present tense “dwells” underscores the enduring reality and condition of the tabernacling divine presence that is fully expressed in the incarnate Christ (cf. D. Moo 2008: 196), both during his earthly ministry, at his death, and in his resurrection and exalted state. The focus here, however, is on the latter state, since that is the time referent of all the “in him” statements in verses 9–12 (though it is true that Christ began to be the temple during his earthly ministry, this was escalated in his death and especially in his resurrection).28 This also means that the divine Christ, the Son of God, will forever exist in a human body (Harris 2010: 89). In contrast to the errorists’ denigration of the physical body (see 2:16–23), the point here is that Christ’s physical body was and is the sacred abode of deity and not a detriment to his divine status; in fact, his fleshly body was necessary for the eschatological presence and power of God to be expressed, especially in reclaiming people from sin and the malevolent powers that held them captive (which is part of the point in v. 10a; Gupta 2013a: 92).

It is possible to translate σωματικῶς (sōmatikōs) as “substantially,” which would indicate that Christ as the divine presence in the new temple is the fulfillment of the partial presence of God in Israel’s old temples, which pointed forward to the “full measure” of the divine presence in the end-time temple. The use of σῶμα (sōma) in Col. 2:17 could support such a use here, since there it refers to Christ being the “substance” (or “reality”) of what the OT types of the OT law foreshadowed.

On balance, the translation of “bodily” (referring to the incarnation) may be preferable over “substantially” (referring to typological substance replacing the shadows), though the latter is a viable option because of its fit with a concept of temple typology, especially in line with the typological use of σῶμα (sōma) in 2:17.29
[2:10a]

Now in verses 10–11, three effects or results of Christ being the very tabernacling presence of God are stated. The first part of verse 10 says that Christ’s divine temple presence has resulted in believers “in him” (possibly “through him”)30 having “been made full” or “having become filled” (for the latter, see ESV, NET). Viable also is the NIV 1984’s rendering, “You have been given fullness in Christ.” The perfect tense focuses on the “full” condition that believers are in. Some have acknowledged the possible problem if this means that Christians “have all the fullness of deity within them, just as Christ does” in 2:9–10 (Lincoln 2000: 623; cf. Peake 1970: 524). But commentators generally equate the “filling” here with the reality that the fullness of salvation is only in Christ (e.g., O’Brien 1982: 113) or that it meets all their spiritual needs in contrast to the false teachers’ proposals (Peake 1970: 524). These general, vague proposals are understandable, since Pauline theology would not let the interpreter think the believer could be divine like Christ! But these proposals are too general and do not satisfactorily answer the problem just addressed. However, if the “fullness” that is “in Him” (v. 9) expresses Christ’s divine presence as the true temple, then it is likely that “[believers who] have been filled in him”* (2:10) has something to do also with the filling of the temple by Christ, which is borne out by the verbal parallelism in Greek between verse 9 and verse 10a:31
Colossians 2:9 	Colossians 2:10a
because in him all the divine fullness dwells in bodily form* 	and in him you have been filled*
ὅτι ἐν αὐτῷ κατοικεῖ πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα τῆς θεότητος σωματικῶς 	καὶ ἐστὲ ἐν αὐτῷ πεπληρωμένοι
hoti en autō katoikei pan to plērōma tēs theotētos sōmatikōs 	kai este en autō peplērōmenoi

Believers’ union with Christ means that, just as Christ has become the divine temple presence of God, so they have begun to be identified with and to experience the effect of being “filled” with that tabernacling presence of Christ’s divine “fullness,”32 just as their union with him in verses 11–13 means they have been circumcised in him, buried with him, and raised with him (Campbell 2013: 35–36). Thus verse 10a is to be understood in line with other Pauline passages about the church being identified with the temple because believers are in Christ. This is similar to 1 Cor. 3 and Eph. 2, where Christ is the foundation of the new end-time temple and believers are built on him as a part of that temple (cf. 1 Cor. 3:10–17; Eph. 2:19–22). In Eph. 1:22–23 “the church” is “the fullness [τὸ πλήρωμα, to plērōma] of Him [Christ] who fills [τοῦ . . . πληρουμένου, tou . . . plēroumenou] all things [not only the church] in every way.”* This is developed in Eph. 3:16–19, where God’s “glory” is given to believers, and “the Spirit’s” work in them results in Christ “dwelling,” which leads to saints “comprehending” that they are part of the temple in Christ (v. 18) and “know[ing] . . . the love of Christ, in order that you should be filled [πληρωθῆτε, plērōthēte] unto all the fullness [τὸ πλήρωμα, to plērōma] of God.”* Ephesians 3:16–19 is a development of the temple text in Eph. 2:20–22, so that, in light of its parallel with Col. 2:9–10, it should not be surprising that the “filling” language in the latter passage should also be about Christ filling the temple of believers33 and that this “filling” is equivalent to the glory of deity filling the church.

Many translations of verse 10 have “You have become complete in him,” but this conveys the possible notion of final eschatological perfection. Instead, the idea here is that of the church beginning to be identified with and thus filled with Christ’s tabernacling divine presence as the end-time temple,34 as is the case in the 1 Cor. 3 and Eph. 2 passages. In the light of the Ps. 67 LXX temple allusion in 1:19, the continuation of that idea in 2:9 and on into verse 10 here suggests that the church has begun to be identified with the latter-day temple because of its union with Christ, being “in Him.”35 Accordingly, this does not refer to an eschatologically consummate completeness, as does Col. 1:28 (“that we may present every man complete in Christ”).
[2:10b]

I think there is solid footing for discerning the temple notions in verses 9 and 10a, which continue the temple idea from 1:19, itself already subtly continued in 1:22 (temple sacrifices); 1:23; and 2:7; yet a link with believers and the temple has occurred early on already in 1:9 (see my comments, including the additional note, on that verse). We will now speculate a bit further on the possible continuation of this idea into 2:10b–11. But at this point let us ask, Why would Paul be concerned with this theme and why is it worth investigating the possible continuation of this idea in verses 10b and 11? We believe that Paul has been focusing on the true temple of Christ, of which believers are a part, in order to counter the errorists’ claims that they experience visions of and participate in the worship of the heavenly temple. These purportedly elite so-called Christians are claiming to have unique experiences of the heavenly temple that enhance their religion in a way that leaves other Christians disenfranchised. They believe that ascetic practices prepare them for such temple experiences and visions, which they regard as necessary to be a part of the true temple. It is this false claim on which verses 16–23 elaborate. And it is this claim that 2:9–15 (esp. vv. 9–11) is countering by affirming that believers have already achieved eschatological fullness in the new, true temple in Christ, which fullness will be consummated at Christ’s return (cf. 3:1–5). It is important to recall that 2:6–23 is the heart of the epistle, so it is significant that the focus on true versus false temple worship occurs in this crucial section of the epistle. The burden of this proposal will be to show that false heavenly temple worship is, in fact, what is primarily in mind in verses 16–23. Thus the point of verse 10a is not merely that “fullness” (defined in various vague ways) can be found only in Christ and not in the false teachers’ extra regulations (so many commentators), but that their participation in Christ as the true temple is all they need to experience the eschatological tabernacling presence of God.

The second part of verse 10 expresses a second result of Christ’s divine presence as the true temple: “He is the head over all rule and authority.” This develops two earlier notions from chapter 1. First, 1:16 has affirmed that because Christ is the divine Creator (v. 16a) of “all things” (v. 16c), then it is deducible that “thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities” (whether good or evil) are part of the creation, which is not equal to him but under him as Creator. The same words for “rule” (ἀρχή, archē) and “authority” (ἐξουσία, exousia) found in 2:10 also appear in 1:16 as part of what Christ has created. Now, what was implicit in 1:16 is brought to the fore: since Christ is the divine Creator, he is ruler “over all” the creation, including “all rule and authority.” In 1:15 we have found that, in light of the emphasis on “firstborn” being about “supremacy over,” the genitival phrase “firstborn of all creation” (πρωτότοκος πάσης κτίσεως, prōtotokos pasēs ktiseōs) was best taken as a genitive of subordination, “firstborn over all creation,” instead of more generally as a genitive of reference, “firstborn with reference to all creation.” Here also in 2:10 the phrase ἡ κεφαλὴ πάσης ἀρχῆς καὶ ἐξουσίας (hē kephalē pasēs archēs kai exousias) is the same kind of genitive and should be translated, “head over all rule and authority.” We shall see in verse 15 below that “the rulers and authorities” there are evil spiritual forces, which Christ defeated at the cross. Here the same reference anticipates verse 15 by generally saying that because Christ is divine (v. 9), he is the absolute ruler even over evil, unseen heavenly powers.36 Then verse 15 shows how in particular he exercised that divine rulership when he came as “the fullness of deity in bodily form” to earth and conquered these powers at the cross.

Not surprisingly, the reference to “head” (κεφαλή, kephalē) is connected to the meaning of the same word for “head” in Col. 1:18: “He is also head of the body, the church.” In 1:18 we concluded that Christ as “head” indicated primarily the notion of headship, yet suggesting that the authority was expressed through directing and causing growth of the church body into a unified whole. However, here in 2:10 only the idea of authority is expressed, as confirmed from 2:15, since Christ is “head over” evil forces as a victor and the church is not in mind, as it was in 1:18. Accordingly, Christ is in a position of authority over what is good, the church, and over what is evil, the invisible satanic powers. Of course, 1:15–17 has affirmed his divine authority over every single thing in creation.

Verse 10 is parallel to Eph. 1:21–23, where also Christ is said to be “above all rule and authority” and “head” (though of the church), and the church is “His body, the fullness of the one filling all things in every way.”* There, in context, it is through Christ’s sovereignty over the bridgehead of the church that the evil heavenly powers will continue to be subdued.

But how could Christ as the “head” relate to the central temple notion in Col. 2:9–10a, which we have been proposing? Since Christ has become the divine ruler in the end-time temple, he has authority not only over his people but also over pseudo-members of the covenant community, the false teachers, behind whom stand the evil angelic “rulers and authorities” (2:15). In fact, the continuation of the Ps. 67:17–18 LXX (68:16–17 Eng.) allusion from 1:19 in 2:9 also suggests this, since in the psalm the climax of God’s tabernacling in the temple occurs directly following his defeat of the enemy (in LXX: Ps. 67:19; also 67:21–25, 29–30; in Eng.: 68:18, 20–24, 28–29).37 The same connection occurs here in Col. 2:10b.

Generally the OT mention of God dwelling in his temple includes the idea that he is the king enthroned there in a victorious position over his people, after defeating all enemies (cf. Exod. 15:1–16 with 15:17–18; cf. also Pss. 78:65–69; 132:13–18).38 Some of these temple portrayals include God enthroned above angelic or heavenly powers.39 In this light, it is natural that Paul sees Christ in his latter-day temple as the “head” of the evil powers.
[2:11a]

Overview of the verse. Could the idea of the temple still be in mind? Perhaps it is an overreading to think so. But even if the temple is not in mind, the third result is now given of what “all the Divine fullness dwelling bodily” in Christ entails. But if this is so, then Christ’s “fullness” as the new temple may still be in mind, and the verse explains one more result that Christ being the temple has brought about. In particular, Christ as the divine presence of the temple may continue in the introductory phrase “in whom [Christ] also” (ἐν ᾧ καί, en hō kai). The καί (kai) could be translated here as “also” or “likewise” (cf. BDAG 495–96, s.v. b2) and functions adverbially to modify the verb “circumcised.” The adverb may advance on to a completely new and unexpected thought beyond that of 2:9–10, as some contend (e.g., O’Brien 1982: 115), or it may continue the thought of verses 9–10:40 “in Him [Christ]” may thus continue to refer to Christ as the new temple, as in 2:9 and 2:10, which would be best expressed by “in whom likewise you were circumcised” (note that 2:9 and 2:10 each refer to the thought of Christ [“in Him”] as the temple, and verse 11 likewise begins with “in Him”).

If the thought of Christ as the temple continues on into Col. 2:11a in this manner, then the background against which to understand verse 11 may be that of circumcision taking place in the temple, which was the custom in Israel (as evident in Luke 2:22–39). In fact, “made without hands” (ἀχειροποίητος, acheiropoiētos) always refers elsewhere to the new, end-time temple (sometimes the temple in Christ),41 which was built not by humans but by God. The very same word occurs in Col. 2:11, which is the only place where the word is not explicitly accompanied by a word for the temple. The point, at the least, is that circumcision in Christ was done by God and not by humans. The observation, however, that the word elsewhere in the NT (two times) and its synonymous equivalents (only twice in Daniel, twice in Acts, and twice in Hebrews) refer only to a God-built temple may suggest an association with the temple in our present text. The point would be that now spiritual circumcision has taken place “in Christ,” the temple, and since the temple of Christ is “made without hands” (ἀχειροποίητος), so is the “circumcision” that takes place in this new temple “made without hands” (ἀχειροποίητος). This would also fit well with the typology of the physical temple pointing to the spiritual temple in Christ (in the background in 1:19 and 2:9) and the typology of physical circumcision pointing to spiritual circumcision, which we will argue is in mind in 2:11 and 13.

Interestingly, in this respect, Luke 2:21–39 is the only passage in the Bible (and, as far as I can tell, in Judaism) that says that it was a “custom” that a child should be circumcised in the temple “eight days” after the child’s birth. Indeed, in this passage Luke says that Jesus’s parents followed this “custom of the Law” and had him circumcised in “the temple” (2:27), after the prescribed period, “according to the Law of the Lord” (2:39; also 2:22–23). This is not only the sole reference to “circumcision” in the “temple” (cf. 2:27, 37) as a “custom,” but it also is the only reference to “Jesus,” identified as “Christ” (see 2:26), undergoing “circumcision” in the “temple.” This event of Christ’s presence at the time of his circumcision in the temple (2:27) was immediately interpreted to indicate Jesus’s future role in accomplishing “salvation” (2:30), vindication (2:34), and “redemption” (2:38) for Israel. The unique wording and theme (even including the idea of redemption) in common between Luke 2 and Col. 2:11 suggests that Colossians at this point is echoing the Luke passage (in its oral form). For this reason O’Brien (1982: 117), following Bruce (1984: 234), says that Christ underwent circumcision at the cross, “of which his literal circumcision [in Luke 2] was at best a [typological] token by way of anticipation,”42 though Bruce and O’Brien do not comment on the possible common temple theme. At first glance, the phrase “circumcision of Christ” sounds like it could refer to the account of Christ’s infant circumcision in the temple. Apparently because of this, some commentators immediately discount this impression, since they believe it to be obvious that Paul is not referring to this event.43 However, these commentators do not reflect on whether this early event could be a foreshadowing of a greater event in Christ’s career, his death.

The circumcising occurred “in Him” (ἐν ᾧ, en hō, Col. 2:11), which either means by the instrumentation of Christ or in the sphere of Christ, though the focus is on the latter, in the sense of union with Christ (cf. Campbell 2013: 36). This is union with Christ as the temple. Thus the third result of Christ’s being the full latter-day divine presence of God in the temple (v. 9) is his “circumcising” his people in that temple and making them clean to be in that temple.

Exodus 12:48 establishes an ordinance to be observed in following generations: “If a stranger [Gentile, LXX = “proselyte”] sojourns with you, and celebrates the Passover to the LORD, let all his males be circumcised, and then let him come near to celebrate it; and he shall be like a native of the land. But no uncircumcised person may eat of it” (cf. Num. 9:14). The “drawing near” is coming to the temple, where the Passover lamb was sacrificed throughout Israel’s generations.44 Thus Gentiles had to be “circumcised” in order to be clean, to be qualified to enter into the temple courts, and to celebrate the Passover sacrifice.

Intriguingly, Ezek. 44:7–9 says that part of Israel’s sin was to allow Gentiles who were “uncircumcised in heart and uncircumcised in flesh” to enter Israel’s old “sanctuary.” In the future, however, the Ezekiel passage says that no Gentiles “uncircumcised in heart and uncircumcised in flesh”45 will be allowed to enter God’s eschatological “sanctuary.” This text is not saying that no Gentiles will be allowed to enter into the end-time temple, but rather only those who are circumcised will enter. This is the only OT passage suggesting that Gentiles need to be spiritually circumcised in the latter days, and especially circumcised to be clean to enter in the new, end-time temple,46 which I am contending is the point of Col. 2:11 (cf. 2:13). This is the same latter-day temple that the “LORD God” and the “prince” of Israel47 will also enter (Ezek. 44:2–3).

Strikingly, Qumran (1QS 5.5) says that a faithful Israelite must “circumcise the foreskin of his sinful inclination and of his stiff neck in order to lay a foundation of truth,” which is referred to as “the house of truth” (1QS 5.6) that is “founded on truth” and is a “foundation of the holy of holies for Aaron, . . . the precious cornerstone, . . . the most holy dwelling” (1QS 8.5–8). This is a spiritual view of circumcision for members of the end-time covenant community;48 its goal is to establish a spiritual temple for “holiness” (1QS 5.6) and “perfection” (1QS 9.6), in order to form a corporate “holy of holies” (1QS 9.6), much like that in Col. 2:10–11 (following García Martínez 1996).

As noted above, it shall be found in Col. 2:16–23 below that the false “philosophy” was a fundamentally wrong understanding of the meaning and application of OT law for the new age. This appears to be part of the erroneous Jewish doctrine that focused on the continuing validity of the law and its institutions instead of Christ as the epitome of divine revelation (e.g., see N. Wright 1986: 23–30). In response to such false teaching, Col. 1:1–2:11 expresses at various points that Christ is the fulfillment of OT prophecy, types, and the wisdom of the law, so that one should see no need to go back to practicing aspects of the law that have now found their full realization in Christ. In their identification with Christ, believers share in this fulfillment. We have seen in verses 9–11, in particular, that Christ’s divine presence in the end-time temple is the NT reality to which the OT physical temples pointed. If we are correct in tracing the temple theme in these verses, we want again to ask, How might this add further to understanding how Paul is responding to the false teaching? It is likely that 2:18 is referring to people who “delight in asceticism and worship of angels, which things he has seen by entering [into the heavenly temple?], puffed up by his fleshly mind.”* If this translation that includes the “temple” is correct (see further on 2:18), then Paul’s emphasis is a contrast with imperfect heavenly temple worship of angels, with the true temple being completely filled by Christ’s presence, in which believers are sufficiently “filled” also as a part of the temple.49 It is the fullness of Christ’s tabernacling presence only that should be worshiped.

The fulfillment of what the temple institution pointed to is not only introduced in 2:9–11 but also verses 11–13 introduce circumcision as an OT institution that is a type, which finds its fulfillment in Christ and believers. “In Him [they] were also circumcised with a circumcision made without hands,” which is an implied contrast to “circumcision . . . by human hands” in Eph. 2:11, the latter of which refers to ethnic Jews. “A circumcision made without hands” expresses the manner in which the circumcising took place.50 The word “handmade” (χειροποίητος, cheiropoiētos) always refers to idols in the Greek OT and is without exception a negative reference, with overtones of idolatry, in the NT (see further Beale 2004: 224–25). Thus the implied reference to “circumcision made with hands” in Col. 2 indicates that to continue to affirm circumcision as the true identity marker of God’s new-covenant people is idolatrous (Pao 2012: 165 agrees). This further enforces the notion that it is idolatrous to continue to trust in the OT “shadows” once their fulfillment has come.51 Thus the aberrant teaching confronting the Colossians is not only false but also idolatrous. Hence the Colossians should not follow such teaching. Verses 16–23 give further details of the “shadows” (cf. v. 17) in which trust should no longer be placed (see, e.g., dietary laws, special cultic feast days, Sabbaths).

These external rites of the law, of which circumcision is a classic representative, are no longer necessary because their redemptive-historical purpose was to function as a “shadow of things about to come, that is, the [substantial] body of Christ”* (Col. 2:17). In one way or another, Paul understood that the various external expressions of the OT law pointed to the coming Messiah, who has now arrived. Therefore, the law’s preparatory adumbrating function has come to an end, since the messianic “substance” to which it points has arrived. The idea here is very similar to that in Matt. 5:17: Jesus “fulfill[s] . . . the Law [and] the prophets” by fulfilling in his actions and words the OT’s direct verbal prophecies, foreshadowing events (e.g., the Passover lamb) and institutions (e.g., sacrifices and temple), the ultimate meaning of the law, and the true and enduring authority of the OT (for the full argument, see Carson 1995: 140–45).

Accordingly, in 2:11–13 Paul views the external rite of circumcision, where a piece of flesh is stripped off, to be a typological pointer to the greater redemptive reality of the entire body of Christ and his followers being “circumcised” or “cut off” from the old sinful world and set apart to a new one.52 Saints have been “circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, in the putting off of the body of the flesh.” The “circumcision without hands” (ἀχειροποίητoς, acheiropoiētos) refers either to Christ as the agent of the circumcision or, more probably, implies God (through the Spirit) as the agent,53 especially in light of Rom. 2:29, “Circumcision is that which is of the heart, by the Spirit and not by the letter.”
[2:11b]

“The removal of the body of flesh.” The word ἀπέκδυσις (apekdysis) in the dative explains the further manner in which the circumcising was done54 and conveys the notion of unclothing, stripping, or undressing, which will become clearer in 2:15 and 3:9–10, where the verbal form of the word occurs (see my comments on those verses; also cf. BDAG 100, s.v. ἀπέκδυσις). The clothing that is taken off is “the body of the flesh” (τοῦ σώματος τῆς σαρκός, tou sōmatos tēs sarkos).

Is it Christ’s followers who are the objects of the stripping, or is it Christ himself at the cross? Commentators differ on the answer, since the identification is not clear. The answer is that both are stripped. Christ was stripped in his death, but since he was a corporate representative for believers, they were stripped “in Him” (cf. “in Him” at the start of 2:11 and in 2:10, 12). It is apparent that Christ is the object of the stripping, though with believers secondarily in mind. This is evident from the following observations: (1) the phrase “body of flesh” occurs in the NT only in Col. 1:22, where it refers to Christ’s old, earthly, and preresurrection body. The phrase occurs elsewhere in Hellenistic Judaism, where it also refers to a physical body of flesh, pointing further to the reference to Christ’s earthly “fleshly body” in 1:22.55 Paul is referring back to this phrase in 1:22 in order to develop its implications further (so Dunn 1996: 157).

Also there is a parallel in both texts of a redemptive action by Christ: “he reconciled” and “you were circumcised.” In Col. 1:22, it was by means of and in the sphere of his old body “through death” that he “reconciled” the hearers. Now this reconciliation from 1:22 is to be explained by means of the removal of Christ’s old-world body from this world through his death, which makes way for his new, resurrection body. Thus Christ’s death is implied because of the parallel of 1:22 with 2:11. (2) Focus on Christ as the object of the stripping could also be pointed to by its parallelism with the immediate following phrase, “the circumcision of Christ,” which we will see refers to Christ being circumcised in his death (i.e., the flesh of Christ being stripped away at the cross, as O’Brien [1982: 117] contends). Note that both phrases begin with ἐν (en) + the dative article τῇ (tē) + a dative noun + a genitive noun, which shows the parallel syntactical nature, suggesting that they are parallel in content: either both phrases refer to believers or both refer to Christ (the latter we prefer).56 (3) While some think the lack of a pronoun after “flesh” differentiates it from 1:22 and suggests a reference to believers and not Christ, Paul can easily omit it here, since he has repeatedly used pronouns (three times) in the preceding verses to refer to Christ and can assume a pronoun here (these last two observations are from Pao 2012: 166).

As we have said, in Christ’s corporate representative stripping, his followers are also secondarily to be seen as the object of the stripping. Instead of “body of flesh,” some translations have “sinful nature” (NIV 1984, NLT) or “natural self” (NJB) or “body of the sins of the flesh” (KJV, among others, which follow MSS with a scribal addition of “sins” before “flesh”). A more straightforward reading of “the body of the flesh” is preferable because of the parallel with 1:22 and its Jewish parallels, though the genitive construction is best taken adjectivally or, more precisely, attributively, “fleshly body” (note in NET; Wallace 1996: 87).

The point in both 1:22 and here is that the “body of flesh” refers to that which is a part of the old, corrupt world. This clause in 2:11 affirms that it was such a body that was stripped from Christ at his death, and it was such a body in which he died for his people (so also Garland 1998: 147–48; Lincoln 2000: 624), and it is such a body that is “stripped off” the saints through their corporate identification with him, though this is a secondary but included idea in verse 11 (an inaugurated spiritual stripping of saints followed by a consummative spiritual and bodily unclothing at the end of the age). Referring to virtually the same thing, Rom. 7:4 says, “[Brothers], you also were made to die to the Law through the body of Christ, so that you might be joined to another, to Him who was raised from the dead” (cf. Moule 1957: 95, citing also the parallel of 1 Pet. 4:1). We will see that dying to the requirements of the law is a major issue also in Colossians (see on 2:16, 20–23).
[2:11c]

“The circumcision of Christ.” The “circumcising” of 2:11a also occurred according to the manner of (ἐν, en + dative) “the circumcision of Christ.”57 The “circumcision of Christ” could be an objective genitive (“circumcising Christ”) or a subjective genitive (“circumcision by Christ”) or a possessive genitive (“Christ’s circumcision” or “Christian circumcision”), though the third alternative does not preclude the second one: the circumcision belongs to and is carried out by Christ.58 It is difficult to choose which is best. The first option is pointed to by the following (against the objections of Harris 2010: 92): (1) the initial clause of verse 11 tells the readers that they were circumcised in union with Christ. That is, they were corporately identified with Christ’s circumcision, so that what is now made explicit is that they were also considered to have been “circumcised” in the circumcision that happened to Christ, who was their representative in receiving this circumcision (the passive verb indicates that God is the subject performing this circumcision). (2) An objective genitive also may be suggested by observing that Christ’s death is referred to twice in the directly following verse 12. That is, “having been buried” and “death” (end of v. 12) is something that happened to Christ, and not something he performed. Especially the initial phrase of verse 12, “having been buried with him in baptism,” appears to be a further explanation of “the circumcision of Christ,” where again Christ’s “having been buried” corporately represents the saints. Since Christ is the object of being “buried” in verse 12, as are believers who are identified with him, so it is likely that Christ is the object of “circumcision” in verse 11, as are believers who are identified with him. (3) An objective genitive is also pointed to by the observation that in the twelve other occurrences in the Greek of Hellenistic Judaism and the NT, the genitive that follows περιτομή (peritomē) is always objective.59 It is significant that among these objective genitive uses, two are in Paul (Rom. 2:25, 29). (4) Christ being the object of the circumcision, referring to his death, best explains the unexpected redemptive-historical “progression from circumcision to burial to resurrection in 2:11–12; . . . the sequence summarizes the essential affirmations of Christianity: Christ died, was buried, and was raised (see 1 Cor. 15:1–4).”60

Consequently “the circumcising of Christ” refers to his death,61 which Paul could conceive of as taking place in the temple, according to Rom. 3:25.62 Thus, there as here, the new temple is inaugurated in Jesus’s death, not only in his resurrection, as other NT texts affirm (e.g., John 2:19–22), and which is likely included in Col. 2:9–10. Also, the “circumcising of Christ” means that he himself was stripped of his old, corrupt body, so that his part in the old world was removed (as we have seen in the preceding phrase “stripping of the fleshly body”).63 The believers’ union with Christ’s death means the same thing for them (so also Peake 1970: 525), though they are also set apart from the corrupt world’s sin, in which they had participated, but Christ had not.64 The old-world realm of the “flesh” includes not only the “body” of a person but also the “mind.”65 The old “body of flesh” in itself does not necessitate that one be sinful, since Christ had such a body that was physically corruptible but was not inextricably linked to sin, though all fallen humans are under the domination of sin/flesh.66 Christians have “stripped off the old man” from themselves (see on Col. 3:9, using the verbal form of ἀπέκδυσις, apekdysis), and, as we will see, have “clothed themselves with the new man”* (Col. 3:10; see below). It is because they have already been corporately stripped in Christ that they are able to strip themselves. The stripping means that the power of the old sinful world over Christians has been broken (see further on the verbal form of ἀπέκδυσις, apekdysis, in 2:15; so also D. Moo 2008: 200–201). As we will see on Col. 3:9–10, the “old man/self” is their identity with the fallen Adam, who corporately represented them in his action of sinning and with his subsequent condemnation. The “new man/self” is their identity with Christ, the new Adam,67 who has corporately represented them in his act of being put to death (the focus of 2:11c here and v. 12a) and being raised from the dead (the focus of the next two verses, 2:12b–13), resulting in their forgiveness (2:13b–14) and justification (Rom. 5:17–18).68 The stripping of Christ’s fleshly body also involved the removal of his subjection to the corruptible Adamic body, which made way for him as the new Adam, with a new-creational body (cf. Dunn 1996: 158, who makes a similar point).

Also, whereas Christ immediately received a new body, they have not. As noted earlier, their circumcision by Christ has an “already” dimension, by which their spiritual identification with the old world has been stripped off, yet also the full stripping off of their physically corrupt body must await the final resurrection at the end of the age, when also their inaugurated spiritual resurrection existence will be perfected.69

Before leaving this discussion of circumcision, it is necessary to anticipate and address the following phrase in Col. 2:13, “in . . . the uncircumcision of your flesh.” In verses 11 and 13, we have only briefly discussed how the external rite of circumcision was a pointer to the greater redemptive reality of Christ and his followers being “circumcised” or “cut off” from the old sinful world and set apart to a new one. In verse 11 we have seen that believers’ redemption consists of being “circumcised with a circumcision made without hands,” which has occurred by means of the “circumcision of Christ” (i.e., his death). The clause “uncircumcision of your flesh” (v. 13) appears to represent sinful unbelief, from which one needs to be “circumcised.” This phrase also helps us understand how physical circumcision in the OT foreshadows the circumcision of Christ and believers (for further discussion, see excursus 4).
[2:12]

After explaining that the “circumcising” of verse 11a was a third result of Christ’s authoritative divine status (v. 9) and having described the manner of the “circumcising” (v. 11b–c), now Paul explicitly explains the primary means or manner by which the Colossians were “circumcised” (v. 11a): they were “buried.” The adverbial participle συνταφέντες (syntaphentes) modifies the verb “circumcised” of verse 11 and, besides being generally contemporaneous with the main verb, appears more specifically to express the manner in which the circumcising took place, though the means of the circumcising could also be in mind. Manner is pointed to by recalling that the focus of the last three adverbial dative clauses indicates the manner of the circumcising.70 Though this may seem a fine point of Greek grammar, it makes a significant exegetical and theological difference in the way this passage understands baptism in relation to circumcision. For example, D. Moo (2008: 201) sees that the participle “burying” in baptism expresses only a separate but temporally contemporaneous act alongside the “circumcising.” On the other hand, if the participle more specifically conveys the means or manner of the circumcising, then “buried in baptism” is part of the action or concept of the circumcising. Context is the ultimate determiner of how adverbial participles function. We have tried to understand this participle’s function in connection with the way the preceding three adverbial clauses function. In addition, the following adverbial participles in verses 13b, 14a, 14b, 15a, and 15b all are generally contemporaneous but also specify some logical modification of the verb (or an action that overlaps with or explains the concept of the verb) that they modify.71

That saints were “buried with Him [Christ]” again shows their union with Christ.72 Their belief in Christ means that they are identified with Christ’s death. Their identification with Christ’s burial has occurred in “a baptizing manner” or was “by means of baptism” (the preposition + dative, ἐν τῷ βαπτισμῷ, en tō baptismō). Possibly both may be in mind.

In the manner of or by the instrumentation of baptism (ἐν ᾧ, en hō), the saints were not only “buried” but also “were raised.”73 Their resurrection also came about by “faith in the working of God,74 who raised Him [Christ] from the dead.” As their baptism identifies them with Christ’s death, so it also identifies them with his resurrection. The mention of the believers’ resurrection followed by Christ’s resurrection probably alludes again to his representative role: because Christ has first been raised, their union “with Him” means that they also are “raised up with Him.” This double mention of resurrection also reflects the intensely eschatological nature of what has happened to believers in Christ. Eschatology in the OT and Judaism was inextricably linked to resurrection. The end-time resurrection has begun in Christ, and with it the new creation (see further Beale 2011: 225–354). This notion of resurrection is highlighted even further in Col. 2:13 through mention of God “[making believers] alive together with [Christ]” (see also John 5:21; 6:63; Rom. 4:17; 8:11; 1 Cor. 15:22, 45; 2 Cor. 3:6; 1 Pet. 3:18, where ζῳοποιέω, zōopoieō, also occurs, though σύν [syn, with] is prefixed to the verb in Col. 2:13).

A number of commentators think that highlighting the idea of an inaugurated resurrection (here; 2:13; and 3:1–4) indicates non-Pauline authorship, but several scholars have rightly pointed out that such an idea is found in Paul’s so-called authentic epistles (e.g., see Rom. 6:4, 11, 13; 2 Cor. 5:14–17), and so is the idea of resurrection as new creation.

Though some see βαπτισμός (baptismos) or βάπτισμα (baptisma)75 to be an explicit reference to the physical sacramental rite of water baptism (e.g., D. Moo 2008: 202), it is probable that since the “circumcision” in Col. 2:11 is clearly spiritual, so is the “baptism” in verse 12a (so also Pao 2012: 167). Nevertheless, the visible sacrament is probably in the background, since it signifies spiritual baptism.76

That the rite of water baptism can be explicitly said to stand in the background of spiritual baptism is apparent throughout the NT (Luke 3:16; John 1:33; Acts 1:5;77 11:16). Also, even closer to our passage is Jesus’s statement that his suffering of death is “the baptism with which I am baptized” (Mark 10:38; so likewise Luke 12:50), which he then applies to his disciples: “You shall be baptized with the baptism with which I am baptized” (Mark 10:39).78 If the “circumcision of Christ” (Col. 2:11) is his own death, which represents the believers’ spiritual circumcision, the same spiritual reality seems to apply to the believer being “buried with [Christ] in baptism.” Paul refers to believers who “by one Spirit . . . were baptized into one body” (1 Cor. 12:13). That Paul likely has a spiritual view of baptism in mind in 1 Cor. 12:13 is pointed to by 1 Cor. 10:2, where he says that Israel was “baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea.” Here identification with Moses, Israel’s leader, is primarily in mind. Particularly relevant is Gal. 3:27: “For all of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ,” though one could debate whether this is spiritual or water baptism, or whether the latter stands in the background of the former, as we have argued for Col. 2:12. That Spirit baptism is the focus is apparent from the observation that Col. 2:12a speaks of actually being “buried” with Christ “in baptism, in which you were also [actually] raised up with Him through faith.” One would have to have a supernatural sacramental view of water baptism to see it as the focus.79 If the sacrament is in the background, however, then, as with John’s baptism in relation to Spirit baptism (Luke 3:16; John 1:33; Acts 1:5; 11:16), so here it is a merely symbolic act signifying—not actually conveying—the reality of dying and rising with Christ in Spirit baptism.
[2:13]

There is now further progression (note the “and,” καί, kai) in explaining the circumcising of believers in verse 11, which we saw was a third effect of Christ possessing “all the fullness of Deity . . . in bodily form” (v. 9). We have observed that the OT background of circumcision included a setting apart from death, sin, and the old world and a setting apart to new life (see excursus 4, “The Old Testament Background of ‘the Uncircumcision of Your Flesh’ in Colossians 2:13”). Verses 11–12 explicitly focus on setting apart from death, though the setting apart to life is likely implied. The conclusion of verse 12 makes explicit that circumcision, as one aspect of its meaning, signifies setting apart to life. Verse 13 continues to focus on the giving of life.

The “you being dead”* introduces a concessive idea whereby the contrary proposition of “being dead in trespasses and in the uncircumcision of your flesh”* underscores the strength of the following statement: “He made you alive with Him.” This results in the following rendering: “And though you were dead, . . . nevertheless he made you alive with him”* (see further in the first additional note on 2:13).

“In trespasses” and “in the uncircumcision of your flesh” are parallel expressions. Thus they explain one another. For example, to be in the condition of the “uncircumcision of flesh” is to be dominated by the realm of sin. This confirms our earlier discussion of the phrase (see excursus 4), which is likely an analogical allusion to Gen. 17:10–27 LXX, where “the flesh [σάρξ, sarx] of your/his uncircumcision [ἀκροβυστία, akrobystia]” appears four times. As we saw, the point in Gen. 17 is that those who are in covenantal relationship with God should express that relationship through being “circumcised in the flesh of his uncircumcision [foreskin]” (e.g., Gen. 17:11). This is a symbol expressing that a true Israelite is one whose heart has been cut apart from a condition of cursing, unbelief, and actual sin, and is set apart to God. “The flesh of your uncircumcision [foreskin]” in Gen. 17 is likely best rendered as “your fleshly foreskin” (an attributed genitive) or “your uncircumcised flesh” (adjectival or attributive genitive). Paul’s phrase, “the uncircumcision [foreskin] of your flesh” could also be respectively understood as “your uncircumcised flesh” (attributed genitive) or “your fleshly uncircumcision [foreskin]” (adjectival or attributive genitive).80 The latter is probably best, since there appears to be a parallelism with “the body of the flesh” in Col. 2:11, which is best seen as an adjectival genitive (“fleshly body”). Thus “fleshly body” in 2:11 and “fleshly foreskin” in 2:13 are synonymous (contra Harris 2010: 95) and refer to the old, corrupt Adamic body. Paul compares physical circumcision to the spiritual reality of the new covenantal relationship with Christ. When believers are identified by faith with Christ’s death, which “cut him off” (= “the circumcision of Christ”) from his portion in the old world and led to his resurrection, they are likewise “cut off” from the old world and subsequently raised (the point of 2:11–13). Paul’s reference to the “removal of the fleshly body”* in 2:11 anticipates the allusion to Gen. 17 in Col. 2:13, where also “fleshly foreskin” is part of the description of the symbolic corruptible condition directly preceding circumcision.

If verse 13 is parallel with the similar concessive clause in 1:21–22, then the reference here to “trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh”* also appears to include “being alienated and hostile in mind” and dominated by “evil deeds” (1:21). “Trespasses” are the likely reason for the “dead” state, since Paul elsewhere explicitly states this (Rom. 5:15, 17). The ἐν (en) before the dative τοῖς παραπτώμασιν (tois paraptōmasin) probably does double duty with the dative τῇ ἀκροβυστίᾳ (tē akrobystia): actual acts of “trespasses” and the condition of the “uncircumcision of the flesh” are both not only the reason for their dead condition but also indicate the sphere in which the “dead” live (Harris [2010: 95] prefers a causal use). This condition of deadness in “uncircumcision” is likely the spiritual condition from which actual “trespasses” arise and which enhance their dead state.

In contrast to the readers’ former dead condition, God “made [them] alive together with Him” (the preposition σύν [syn, with] prefixed to the verb intensifies the following σύν). “Made alive” is synonymous with “resurrected.”81 Because Christ was raised from the dead and believers are in union with him, they also are considered to be resurrected: “made alive together with him” or “along with him” (Harris 2010: 201). The σύν- verb and following σύν preposition highlight union with the resurrected Christ.82 It is not their doing but God’s doing. This is a true resurrection from the dead, which begins spiritually in this age and is consummated both spiritually and physically at the end of the age. This resurrection is also the beginning of the latter-day new creation, which will be completed when believers receive new-creation bodies and live in the new cosmos (see further Beale 2011: 249–316). Christians thus are now in union with Christ’s own resurrection life, which will be consummated in union with him at the end of the age: “Your life is hidden with [σύν] Christ in God. When Christ, who is our life, is revealed, then you also will be revealed with [σύν] Him in glory” (Col. 3:3–4).83 This beginning new creation is none other than regeneration.

The saint’s resurrection occurred as a result of God “having forgiven84 [them] all [their] trespasses” when Jesus died. The historical forgiveness at the cross is a necessary condition for existential regeneration. Or possibly the point may be less precise; perhaps Paul merely wants to show that in some manner forgiveness is closely linked to saints being made alive (D. Moo 2008: 207–8).

In the light of Col. 2:11–13, the question presses upon us about how Christ’s historical death and resurrection for his people relate to the believer’s identification and union with those two events during the lifetime of the believer. Paul is clearly focusing on a person’s faith as that which has brought a believer into union with Christ’s death and resurrection (so 2:12, as well as “receive” in 2:6 and “faith” in 2:7; cf. also “faith” in 1:4, 23; 2:5). But in the background is the notion that Christ’s historical death and resurrection entail that those who will believe are already identified with him in his redemptive work. Their union with him by faith in their own time is an outworking of their identification with his redemptive work in his historical time. This is evident sometimes from the near blurring of the two in such expressions as “You [have] been buried with him” (2:12) and “having forgiven us [at the historical event of the cross]” (2:13). Colossians 2:14 makes this historical forgiveness at the cross even clearer: “having nailed it [our debt] to the cross.”
[2:14]

In addition to being “made alive with” in verse 13 being the result of (or necessary for) forgiveness, it is also the result of God’s historical “cancel[ing] out85 the certificate of debt . . . against us, which was hostile to us.” This phrase about canceling debt at Christ’s cross is natural here, since it may be generally synonymous with the preceding participle, “forgiving” (χαρισάμενος, charisamenos) in verse 13, and elsewhere this word can refer to canceling out debts (Luke 7:42–43; Philo, Spec. 2.39; see Lohse 1968: 108). The document contained “the charge of our legal indebtedness, which stood against us” (so NIV) because of sinful violation of God’s law.86 Some have summarized the “handwriting” (χειρόγραφον, cheirographon) as an IOU, which was typically written by the one who owed the debt.87 This meaning is likely in mind, since it occurs often throughout contemporary papyri, sometimes in contemporary Hellenistic Judaism and in classical and Hellenistic writings.88 The content of the IOU is “in decrees” (τοῖς δόγμασιν, tois dogmasin), which likely is a dative of content, though a dative of cause could be in mind (“because of decrees”).89

If this were a dative of cause, then the “certificate of debt” is “against us because of the decrees” written in it that have been broken. That the “decrees” (pl. of δόγμα, dogma) here are part of the OT law of God is apparent from 2:20, where “submitting to decrees” (δογματίζω, dogmatizō) refers to ordinances of the OT law, especially the dietary laws (see on 2:20–21; so also Pao 2012: 171), which the errorists have distorted.90 Ephesians 2:15 makes this even clearer: “abolishing in His flesh the enmity, which is the Law of commandments contained in ordinances [decrees, pl. of δόγμα, dogma].”91 The NJB well paraphrases Col. 2:14 by “He has wiped out the record of our debt to the Law.” This document of legal debt likely existed in the heavenly realm (see the additional note on 2:14), and its being wiped out probably occurred not only in the earthly but also the heavenly dimension, since the “disarm[ing of] the rulers and authorities” in 2:15 has occurred at the earthly cross but certainly also in the invisible heavenly realm, where those evil powers reside (cf. Eph. 6:11–12; cf. Col. 1:16).92 As with the temple in the OT, Christ’s cross was the place where heaven and earth intersected. God’s law condemned Jews, who had contracted with God to obey his law but broke it (Deut. 27:9–26; 30:15–20).93 Gentiles also stood guilty because they violated the law’s moral commands, which would have been known through their conscience (cf. Rom. 2:14–15).94 In fact, Israel was a corporate Adam figure who represented all humanity in their obligation to obey God.95 Thus all humanity needed forgiveness for breaking the divine law.

That the legal “certificate of debt” was “against us” (καθ᾿ ἡμῶν, kath’ hēmōn) indicates that it contained testimony against all people for violating the laws of God. That the “certificate of debt” is also said to be ὑπεναντίον ἡμῖν96 (hypenantion hēmin) emphasizes this even more by explaining that the condemnatory testimony was “hostile to us” (the Greek word appears often in the LXX and always refers to “enemies” or “adversaries”; cf. O’Brien 1982: 126). This opposition of “the certificate of debt consisting of decrees” broken by people is highlighted by observing that the expressions of opposition surround the phrase like bookends, which some English translations obfuscate (NASB, KJV, NIV), or in some cases one of the phrases is omitted because of purported needless redundancy (NRSV, NLT).

The “wiping out of the certificate of debt in decrees against us”* (2:14) is an explanation of “forgiving them all their trespasses”* (v. 13b). What is “wiped out” precisely are not human sins but the written record of sins that testify to the guilt of humans.97 Forgiveness is the wiping out of the “debt” that must be paid or the legal penalty of sins that people deserve to suffer because they have violated God’s OT “decrees” or legal requirements. Galatians 3:10 expresses the same thought: “For as many as are of the works of the Law are under a curse; for it is written, ‘Cursed is everyone who does not abide by all things written in the . . . Law, to perform them’” (following Gupta 2013a: 96). In 2:16–23 Paul says that believers are not subject to the OT ceremonial “decrees,” and the reason for this is given here in 2:14: Christ has taken the penalty for breaking all of God’s law, including the cultic and ceremonial decrees (see esp. 2:14b, on which see below). We must wait, however, until that section to explain why the ceremonial laws are the focus of that to which Christians are no longer obligated.

This context does not explain what that penalty is, though it is clear that the penal debt is “against” people, since they must suffer it. However, since forgiveness results in Christ’s followers being “made alive” from their “dead” condition, not to be forgiven and to remain in that “deadness” would likely be entailed as part of the judicial penalty. That being “dead” in 2:13 is a legal condemnation is apparent from Rom. 5:15–18, where trespasses lead to both “death” and “condemnation.” The penalty also entails “the wrath of God [coming] upon the sons of disobedience” (Col. 3:6), at which time “he who does wrong will receive the consequences of the wrong which he has done, and that without partiality” (3:25).

The last clause of 2:14 gives a yet further explanation of “forgiveness” (v. 13b) and, in particular, what it means that God “canceled out the certificate of debt against” his people (v. 14a): “He has taken it out of the way, having nailed it to the cross.”98 The cross is a metonymy whereby it stands in association with Jesus. Accordingly, “cross” is substituted here for Jesus’s suffering in order to place more emphasis on the suffering itself.99 That is, the penal debt against sinners was removed because it was placed on Jesus at the cross (so also Moule 1957: 98). He suffered the penal debt of his peoples’ sin. He was “nailed” for the sinners’ sin. Once the penal wrath (cf. 3:6) was removed “by the blood of His cross,” then “peace” and “reconciliation”* between the sinner and God result (see Col. 1:20), and the “enmity” between God and humans is “put to death in him”* (Eph. 2:16). Could there be allusion to attaching to the cross the writing (ἡ ἐπιγραφή, hē epigraphē) describing the crime of the one being crucified, as was the case with Jesus (Mark 15:26)? If so, “God nails the accusation against us to the cross of Jesus, just as his accusation had been nailed there” (O’Brien 1982: 126; cf. N. Wright 1986: 113, with a different twist).
[2:15]

While believers were affected positively by the forgiving work of Jesus at the cross, Paul now progresses on and states that the satanic powers were negatively affected by the cross: “by unclothing the rulers and authorities, he made a public demonstration of them.”* The specific link between verses 14 and 15 may be that “the stripping” of these evil powers, “whose possession of the damning indictment” kept people in their grip, is what released their fiendish grip on those who believed (Bruce 1984: 110, followed by O’Brien 1982: 126–27). This may be suggested by the close juxtaposition earlier of “forgiveness of sins” through Christ (1:14) and “delivering us from the authority of darkness and transferring us to the kingdom of his beloved Son”* (1:13). There is a similar juxtaposition here of “forgiveness of trespasses”* (2:13b) through Christ’s death, “the wiping out of the certificate of debt” at the “cross”* (2:14) and defeating “rulers and authorities” (2:15). What could be the connection? Could it be that in both sets of juxtapositions Christ’s forgiveness of saints’ sins relinquished the dominant hold of satanic powers over them? Therein may lie the essence of the victory: that believers’ sins were forgiven through the weakness of the cross, which vanquished the devilish forces’ powerful grasp over their former unbelieving lives and “stripped” the satanic foes of their power over the redeemed. This is in line with Eph. 2:2, where believers formerly “walked on the basis of . . . the prince of the power of the air, of the spirit that is now working in the sons of disobedience.”* This satanic power over people because of sin’s dominance over them was broken through the forgiveness at the cross.100 Thus Christ’s forgiveness of our sins (mentioned in 2:13b) and “wiping out the certificate of debt”* (2:14) appears here to be the lever releasing us from the “authority” of the powers of darkness, which appears to have included their authority in keeping us in bondage to the condemnatory accusations of that “certificate of debt” (2:14).101

God is most likely the subject of the verb and participle, though it could possibly be Jesus. However, the subject of the verb “circumcised” in 2:11 appears to be God, who is also the subject of the “raising” of Christ and believers in verse 12 and of the “making alive” and “forgiving” of saints in verse 13, as well as being the subject of the verbs “nailing” and “taking out of the way” in verse 14. Thus, in light of the preceding verses, God again appears to be the subject of the verb forms in verse 15.102

Many versions translate ἀπεκδύομαι (apekdyomai) as “disarmed” (ESV, HCSB, NASB, NIV, NLT, NRSV, RSV; NET, “disarming”), though some have “stripped” (NJB) more in line with “unclothed” (KJV has “spoiled”). All of these translations render the middle voice of the verb with an active sense, and with God as the subject. It is possible though improbable to see Christ as the subject and rendering the verb with a middle sense: Christ stripping himself of his fleshly body or in some sense stripping himself of evil powers (see the additional note on 2:15 for these views).

The translation of “stripped” or “unclothed” is preferable to “disarmed,” since it is in line with translating the same verb with “stripping” in 3:9 and the noun form with the same translation in 2:11. This translation also preserves the intended background picture here of a conqueror who has vanquished the enemies, shamed them by stripping them of their weapons and armor, and leads them in a victory parade, which typically concludes in their death. One can, however, see how the translation of “disarmed” conveys the same notion, but it is a secondary and derived idea (as D. Moo [2008: 214] admits, though he still favors the translation of “disarmed”). It is more precise to keep the background picture and to see it as metaphorically conveying the idea of removing power or disarming, so that this idea is derived from the picture. This was a common picture in Roman victories over their enemies, and Paul is likely drawing on that background here. The Roman triumph parade, which we will see is in mind, always assumed that the enemies being led in it had been decisively defeated in a previous battle or war. So, if the full Roman picture is in mind, then portrayed here are the satanic powers that have been “stripped” of their battle attire (i.e., their power) and “publicly exposed” (with connotations of mocking and disgrace) “through God leading them in his triumphal parade by means of the cross.”103 The “stripping” can refer to the prior defeat of the evil powers preceding the triumphal parade, or it can be part of the triumphal process itself. The consummation of this parade will be the consummate defeat (death) of these powers at the very end of time (in line with the background picture that the victory parade typically concluded in the death of the humiliated enemy). The NJB is the only translation that expresses this picture: “he has stripped the sovereignties and the ruling forces, and paraded them in public, behind him in his triumphal procession.” Recall that these “rulers and authorities” are satanic powers (see on 1:16 and 2:10), so that they are the ones being stripped here. Their apparent triumphal stripping of Christ (2:11) was ironically their own stripping. Accordingly, verse 15 explains how Christ expresses his victorious headship over the evil “rulers and authorities” in 2:10b.

In the light of the Roman victory image, NJB’s “paraded them in public” conveys the intention of the more straightforward translation of the Greek, “made a public display of.”104 These spiritual enemies were put on display in their defeated and unclothed form, which carries notions that they were “exposed,” “mocked,” or “disgraced.”

However, the NJB renders the last phrase of the verse, ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō), as “behind him,” which is possible but may be too paraphrastic. This phrase may better be translated “through him [Christ]” or “through it [the cross].”105 It is difficult to determine which is best. If ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō) refers to Christ (as in 2:9–10; cf. 2:13), then it is focusing on God as the one who stripped the evil powers in public display and who won the victory through Christ. If the last phrase refers to God, which is unlikely, then Christ is the one who stripped the enemy in public display and triumphed “through God’s” power. If the concluding clause refers to the “cross,” then either God or Christ can be viewed as the subject of the preceding actions, accomplishing them through the “cross.”106

It seems that ἐν αὐτῷ refers to the “cross,” since the nearest preceding nominal antecedent is “the cross” (also in the dative) at the end of verse 14 (τῷ σταυρῷ, tō staurō; so NET note). Furthermore, a reference to the cross would express the irony of the victory: God or Christ overcoming the opponent through the death and weakness of the cross. This ironic idea may be pointed to further by observing that Paul uses the same verb θριαμβεύω (thriambeuō) in 2 Cor. 2:14: “God, who always leads us in triumph in Christ.” This time the verb refers to Paul’s suffering ministry in Christ against the same Roman background picture of triumph over enemies (see Hafemann 2000: 16–34). Paul is now the one who has been defeated (at his Damascus conversion), and now in his ministry God is leading him in a triumphal parade as a captive slave to death. The point in the context of 2 Corinthians is that God brings a deathlike suffering onto Paul in order ironically to show his power and glorify himself through the weakness of Paul. It is true that the suffering captives being led in the triumphal parade in Col. 2:15 are the evil powers and not Paul or other saints, but a positive ironic element may still be included in the picture: victory through the defeat of the cross. In this respect, “the cross on which Christ died is compared to the chariot in which the victor rode in triumph” (E. Scott 1930: 49; also cited by Dunn 1996: 168); “the convict’s gibbet is the victor’s car” (Lightfoot 1875: 192).

Even if ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō) refers to Christ (“in him” or “by means of him”) and not precisely the cross, the same idea of victory through Christ’s death at the cross is probably still in mind. However, some think that, if Christ is in mind, then it is the victory of his resurrection that is the focus, so that the last phrase of the verse represents a temporal order going from death (v. 14) to resurrection (v. 15). Such a view is certainly possible and would be in line with other NT passages affirming that it was Christ’s resurrection that defeated the “rulers and authorities.” For example, Eph. 1:20–21 says God raised Christ from the dead and seated him in the heavenlies, “far above all rule and authority and power and dominion.”107 Yet the only parallels to Christ’s relation to the “rulers” and “authorities” in Colossians are in 1:16, where he is ruler over them in creation because he is the sovereign divine creator, and 2:10, where he is their “head” because of his incarnate sovereign divinity. There is no clear mention of Christ’s resurrection in 1:16 or 2:10, and it is likely not intended in either of these parallels.108 Such an identification is problematic: nowhere is it explicitly mentioned in 2:15. Only Christ’s death has been in mind since verse 14, and as noted earlier, “the cross” at the end of verse 14 is the closest clear antecedent to ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō) at the end of verse 15. However, that the resurrection is secondarily in mind is likely, since verses 11–15 are ultimately to be viewed as a third effect of Christ’s divine temple presence (v. 9). And 2:9 is based on 1:19, which gives the basis for Christ’s preeminence as the resurrected Lord. This is plausibly part of the reason why the resurrection has been mentioned explicitly in 2:12–13.

Probably the best translation of the verb θριαμβεύω both in 2 Cor. 2:14 and Col. 2:15 is “lead in a triumphal procession.”109 Furthermore, God “lead[ing] us in [his] triumph in [through] Christ” in 2 Cor. 2:14 leaves open that in Col. 2:15 either God or possibly Christ is the subject of the “making a public display by stripping” and “triumphing.” Nevertheless, since it is God’s “triumph” in the 2 Corinthians text, it points to God as the subject of the verbs in our text, which we have also argued for in the light of the preceding verses.

Colossians 2:15 concludes a section (vv. 10–15) that gives the effects of Christ’s sovereign deity (v. 9), and verse 9 is the basis for the audience’s “walking” in line with Christ and not the false teaching. Verses 11–13 give an extended explanation of the third effect of verse 9. Verses 14–15 are linked to a development of the third effect of “circumcision,” introduced in verses 11–13, but especially indicate the result of Christ being “head over all rule and authority” in verse 10. Could it be that Paul concludes with the evil powers being led in a triumphal captive train by God as a counterpoint to the warning to the Colossians in verse 8 not to be “taken captive” by the false teaching, which is empowered by the forces of darkness?110

Additional Notes

2:7. If βεβαιόω (bebaioō) is not part of an explicit temple picture, it may carry connotations of “establish” and include the idea of “confirm,” in the sense of “put something beyond doubt” (BDAG 172–73, s.v. βεβαιόω). For example, the word can be used of Christ’s verification of the patriarchal promises by fulfilling them (Rom. 15:8) or the verification of Paul’s testimony through miracles performed by the hearers (1 Cor. 1:6–8) or of Christ’s teaching by miracles performed through the apostles (Heb. 2:3–4). Perhaps the idea is that the Colossian believers’ “rootedness” and being “built up in” Christ leads to their having great assurance about their faith, a development of 2:2, where “having been knit together in love” led to “full assurance of understanding” the “mystery” of Christ.

2:7. The phrase τῇ πίστει (tē pistei) may be a dative of means (“by means of faith”) or sphere (“in faith”) or a dative of reference (“with reference to faith”; see Harris 2010: 82). However, since the context does not favor clearly the first and second uses, the more ambiguous dative of reference is preferable.

2:10. Christ’s resurrection has exalted him to be “head of the corner” (ASV) or “chief corner stone” of the temple, a repeated quotation of Ps. 118:22 (117:22 LXX, κεφαλὴν γωνίας, kephalēn gōnias) in the NT. Macaskill (2013: 149–54, 163–71) makes the novel contention that the use of this passage in the NT may be the origin of Paul’s view of Christ as the “head” (κεφαλή, kephalē) of the church, which is his “body.” Christ is an actual organic part, the chief part, of the temple, which includes his corporate people. But what Macaskill does not note is that in every quotation of Ps. 118:22, Christ as “the head of the corner” of the temple, which occurred by his death and especially resurrection, has its direct effect in defeating his enemies within the covenant community (so Matt. 21:41–46; Mark 12:9–12; Luke 20:16–19; Acts 4:8–28; 1 Pet. 2:4–8)! This idea of defeat of the enemy is explicit in these quotations, and the idea of believers as part of the temple is, at best, only implicit in these quotations, though 1 Pet. 2:4–8 also applies Ps. 118:22 explicitly both to the demise of unbelievers and to believers as part of the temple with Christ. If Macaskill is correct in linking Paul’s use of “head” to Ps. 118111 while viewing Christ and believers as part of the temple, then the use of “head” may also entail the idea that Christ as “head” of the temple also represents his defeat over enemies, over “all [evil] rule and authority.” This would further explain why Paul could see Christ’s headship over evil powers to be an effect of his becoming the incarnate divine temple at his resurrection in Col. 2:9. As noted in the commentary section, this also is in line with the allusion to Ps. 67:17–18 LXX (68:16–17 Eng.) from Col. 1:19 that is continued in 2:9, since the climax of God’s tabernacling in the temple in the psalm happens immediately after he defeats the enemy (see Ps. 67:19 LXX [68:18 Eng.]). Christ’s redemptive work in establishing his people as a temple and as his body is also what decisively defeated the evil powers, and it is through the body of the church that this victory will be consummated (on the latter idea, see O’Brien 1982: 114).

2:11–12. The relationship between circumcision and baptism. The inextricable link or overlapping reality between spiritual “circumcision” and spiritual “baptism” is evident in verses 11–12a:

And you were circumcised

also

in him

in the manner of a circumcision made without hands

in the manner of the removal of the body of flesh

in the manner of the circumcision of Christ

in the manner of having been buried with him in baptism.*

The main verb in this long sentence is “you were circumcised” (περιετμήθητε, perietmēthēte), and this verb is modified by six following Greek adverbial clauses. The first modifier (“also”) merely explains that verses 11–12 are progressing on from verse 10 to give another result of Christ’s divine authority (v. 9). The second modifier (“in him”) explains that believers are in union with Christ, thus being corporately “circumcised in him.”112 The following four clauses of verses 11–12a explain something about the concept that believers “were circumcised” in Christ. All four adverbial clauses focus on describing in what manner the action of the verb “circumcised” occurred.113 That is, they “were circumcised” (1) in the manner of a spiritual circumcision (“without hands,” i.e., by God or Christ), (2) in the manner of “removing the body of flesh [from Christ at the cross]” (which representatively takes away people from their old identification with the sinful world), (3) in the manner of being made to identify with Christ’s own death (which is referred to as “the circumcision of Christ”), and (4) in the manner of being “buried with him in baptism” (which also refers to identification with Christ’s death).

Or plausibly, we saw that these last four adverbial clauses in the layout above could indicate, instead of manner, the means by which saints “were circumcised.” It is hard to know whether manner or means is best, though they are very close in meaning. Perhaps both are included, though we have concluded that the greater focus is on these being adverbial of manner. Whichever is the case, the phrases would be qualifying the action of the verb “circumcise,” so that they would not be seen as a separate concept from the circumcising action but overlapping with it.

At the risk of overanalysis, we suggest the following two points.

    What is the ruling idea, if any, between the verb “circumcised” at the beginning of 2:11 and the following last series of four modifying clauses? It is likely that “You were circumcised” is the dominant or main point. The four last clauses logically support the verbal notion of “circumcised” by indicating the manner, or possibly means, by which this verb’s action was carried out. These supporting phrases are piled one upon another, the first three pertaining to spiritual circumcision and the last to spiritual baptism. Therefore, since the three adverbial phrases following the “in whom also” clause (of v. 11a) are explained to be the manner or means by which they “were circumcised,”114 it is probable that the directly following adverbial participial phrase, “buried with him in baptism,” is also the manner or means of “were circumcised.”115 Furthermore, since these same three adverbial clauses of 2:11 appear to be semantic equivalents conceptually (“circumcision made without hands. . . in the removal . . . in the circumcision”) to the initial “were circumcised,” then, in line with these three preceding clauses, “buried in baptism” (v. 12a) likely designates the same thing as the spiritual notion of “were circumcised” in verse 11a. Syntactically, if “having been buried” is a qualification of the verb “were circumcised,” then it is an aspect of that verbal action and essentially equivalent to it. Or alternatively, “buried in baptism” could perhaps be seen to be a subordinate logical notion included within the larger concept of spiritual circumcision, since it is the manner of such circumcision or the means to achieving it.116 Thus spiritual baptism is likely equivalent to spiritual circumcision in this passage, or at least considered to be an integral though subordinate part of such circumcision.117 In the latter case, one could conclude that spiritual baptism and spiritual circumcision are not precisely equivalent. Even in this case, there is significant conceptual overlap between the two (both representing death and resurrection).118
    In the commentary section and in excursus 4 (“The Old Testament Background of ‘Uncircumcision of Your Flesh’ in Colossians 2:13”) we concluded that OT physical circumcision as a type has been fulfilled in eschatological spiritual circumcision and is no longer relevant for entrance into the new-covenant community. Instead, spiritual “circumcision made without hands” and spiritual “baptism” are ongoing realities designating entrance into the covenant community.119 Since spiritual circumcision in Christ is the fulfillment of the type of physical circumcision, and since spiritual circumcision is virtually equated here with spiritual baptism, then it appears plausible that such spiritual baptism is also seen as the fulfillment of the physical type of circumcision. This is still a step removed from saying that the physical rite of baptism for entrance into the covenant community is the equivalent of the physical rite of circumcision, since spiritual baptism is the focus in this passage. But if behind spiritual baptism lies the physical rite of baptism (as some commentators hold),120 then physical circumcision can be seen to have its typological fulfillment also in the physical rite of baptism. And, further, if it is true that physical baptism stands in the background behind spiritual baptism here, then even the liturgy of baptism is secondarily seen to function equivalently to the OT physical rite of circumcision, since physical circumcision probably stands behind spiritual circumcision in this passage.

Circumcision (physical and spiritual) and its equivalent in baptism (physical and spiritual) represent another example of the transformation of one of Israel’s most explicit distinguishing marks.121

2:12. It is difficult to know whether βαπτισμῷ (baptismō) or βαπτίσματι (baptismati) is the original reading, since both are supported by MS evidence of roughly equivalent quality. Metzger (1971: 623), followed by O’Brien (1982: 118) and other commentators, speculates that βαπτισμῷ is the harder reading and therefore preferred, since it is less common than βαπτίσματι, the former occurring only three times outside Colossians and the latter nineteen times, and two of its three uses refer to Jewish washing rituals (Mark 7:4; Heb. 9:10) and not Christian baptism. It is hard to be confident that βαπτισμῷ is the harder and original reading, since one of its uses likely refers to Christian water baptism (Heb. 6:2), and Josephus (Ant. 18.117) uses βαπτισμός (baptismos), of which βαπτισμῷ is the dative form, to refer to John the Baptist’s “baptism,” which may represent a wider usage in reference to John’s baptizing. Both words can refer to the ceremonial rite of water baptism, therefore, though βάπτισμα can indicate a metaphorical baptism (Mark 10:38–39; Luke 12:50; cf. Matt. 20:22; see BDAG 165, s.v. βάπτισμα). There seems to be no reason why βαπτισμός could not have the same metaphorical connotation, if context warrants, especially since one of its three NT uses likely refers to Christian water baptism (Heb. 6:2). Lightfoot (1875: 184) sees the two words as being almost synonymous, so that it does not matter which word was original in terms of the meaning.

2:12. It is not clear what is the antecedent of the ἐν ᾧ (en hō) phrase. It could refer to the preceding ἐν ᾧ (in him, i.e., Christ) in verse 11a, especially since the very same phrase clearly refers to Christ in verse 11, and ἐν αὐτῷ (en autō, in him) in verses 9–10 also refers to Christ, and Christ is the central strand throughout verses 9–15 (as argued well by O’Brien 1982: 118–19; Sumney 2008: 139; Campbell 2013: 37–38). However, the phrase may just as well refer to “in baptism,” since βαπτισμῷ is the nearest antecedent. Furthermore, it would be easy to see that συνηγέρθητε (synēgerthēte) would be understood to have a following implied “him” (αὐτῷ, autō) carried over from its explicit mention at the beginning of the verse. Otherwise, to attach “in whom” to “you were raised with” is awkward, redundant, and overweights the verb, especially the rendering of “with” as a part of the verb becomes redundant with “in him.” Thus “in which [baptism] also you were raised up with him” is preferable to “in whom [Christ] also you were raised up with.” But whichever is the antecedent, the overall meaning of the verse remains little altered (e.g., see Sumney 2008: 139).

2:13. The phrase “and you being dead” (ὑμᾶς νεκροὺς ὄντας, hymas nekrous ontas) is best rendered concessively (“though you were dead”), and the participle may be an adverbial accusative-absolute construction, modifying the following verb “he made alive” (“nevertheless, he made alive”). However, Campbell (2013: 38) views the participle ὄντας (ontas) as adjectival, modifying the accusative “you” (ὑμᾶς, hymas), which seems preferable. This is parallel to the same kind of construction in Eph. 2:1, ὑμᾶς ὄντας νεκρούς (hymas ontas nekrous), and 2:5 (see Wallace 1996: 191, who sees the participle there to be concessively adverbial, modifying the following verb “made alive”). The initial ὑμᾶς in Col. 2:13, as in Eph. 2:1, would then serve as the object of the following verb, “he made alive” (“You . . . he made alive”), with the “you” then being repeated from the beginning of the verse for emphasis; O’Brien (1982: 123) also sees the “you” as being repeated by Paul for the sake of emphasis. The parallel with Eph. 2:1 is strengthened by observing that in both Eph. 2:1 and Col. 2:13 “you being dead” is followed by “in transgressions” and “made alive together” (cf. Eph. 2:5). Colossians 1:21 is a parallel to 2:13 and Eph. 2:1, since the same phrase καὶ ὑμᾶς ὄντας (kai hymas ontas) also occurs (though “alienated” of Col. 1:21 replaces “dead” in Eph. 2:1 and Col. 2:13), and the participle in Col. 1:21 likely forms a periphrastic participial clause “and you being alienated” (ὄντας ἀπηλλοτριωμένους, ontas apēllotriōmenous). Thus Col. 1:21 is best rendered concessively because of context (“though you were formerly alienated”), apparently as an adjectival participle and functioning as the object of the verb “reconciled” in verse 22 (“nevertheless you being dead, . . . you he now has reconciled”).

Since the distinction between “you” and “we” in Eph. 2:1 and 2:5 likely indicates a distinction between Gentiles and Jews, the same may be the case with “you” and “us” in Eph. 2:11–13 (note the repetition of the second-person form also in Col. 2:10–12; so also MacDonald 2000: 101). Bruce (1984: 108), Sumney (2008: 143), and Pao (2012: 169) express more certainty about this. The following reference to “the uncircumcision of your flesh” (2:13) may further point to Gentiles being addressed at this point, even though the phrase has a primarily spiritual meaning (among others, see Calvin 1972: 333–34; Lightfoot 1875: 180; Caird 1976: 194; likewise Sumney 2008: 142; Pao 2012: 169). MacDonald (2000: 101) observes that “uncircumcision” is one of the ways Paul refers to Gentiles elsewhere (Rom. 3:30; Gal. 2:7). Of course, the spiritual significance of the expression would apply equally to Jewish Christians in their former unbelief.

2:13. The ἐν (en) in the first clause of verse 13 is barely favored by NA28, since equally good MSS support its presence and its omission. If the omission were original, then a scribe may have been influenced to add ἐν, since it has been used repeatedly in verses 9–12. But the problem does not affect the meaning of the verse, since the directly following datives may function with the same sense as ἐν.

2:13. Instead of “you” (ὑμᾶς, hymas) after “he made alive,” which is read by NA28 (and supported by good and early MSS), some good quality and early MSS have “us” (ἡμᾶς, hēmas), which was likely changed by a scribe in order to harmonize with the following “us” (ἡμῖν, hēmin) near the end of the verse; some MSS also omit this ὑμᾶς in order to remove the redundancy created by “you” at the beginning of the verse. Thus “you” is the more difficult reading, which best explains how the other readings could have come into being. O’Brien (1982: 123) and Metzger (1994: 556) agree with this explanation.

2:13. The reading “us” (ἡμῖν, hēmin) has strong manuscript support. Metzger (1994: 556) thinks that the variant ὑμῖν (hymin, you) came about through a copyist’s error in hearing, since the letters η and υ were pronounced alike in later Greek.

2:14. Some see the “handwriting” or “certificate of debt” (χειρόγραφον, cheirographon) explicitly as an IOU. So TLNT 3:508–10, e.g., notes that in the papyri it typically refers to an “‘acknowledgment of debt,’ i.e., the receipt signed by a debtor, who acknowledges that he owes a certain sum . . .”; e.g., one papyrus document says, “merchandise for which you have signed a receipt [χειρόγραφον],” so that the “receipt” is a document testifying to money owed for the merchandise, which has not yet been paid (TLNT 3:509, citing P.Oxy. 745.2 and adducing like papyrus examples). So also O’Brien (1982: 125), Harris (2010: 96, 103), and D. Moo (2008: 208–12) opt for the IOU meaning. For this sense of “certificate of debt,” see also Plutarch, Borrow 4 (Mor. 829A); Life of Aesop G, sect. 122, line 5122 (1st cent. AD). A number of commentators cite uses in Tob. 5:3; 9:5; and sometimes T. Job 11.11 (e.g., Dunn 1996: 164; cf. Davids 2008a: 272) that line up with this meaning, though the notion that this is a heavenly book, as Dunn (1996: 164) argues, is possible but not clear. Some commentators cite Philem. 18–19 as expressing the concept of this IOU, yet this term is not used there (e.g., MacDonald 2000: 102). See also Artemidorus, Dreams 3.40 (R. White 1975), where the word means “a note of hand,” recording a loan that is owed. Polybius, Hist. 30.8 (3rd–2nd cent. BC) uses the word to refer to letters of alliance against Rome that condemned people who had written them in the presence of Roman authorities and their allies; likewise Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Rom. Ant. 5.8.2 (1st cent. BC), applies the word similarly to “letters” of conspiracy against a Roman consul that became a basis of accusation against the letter writers when the letters were discovered.123

After a thorough search of the word in the papyri, K. Kim (2017: 233–39) contends that χειρόγραφον merely refers to a document that contains a declaration or oath that the Colossians had sworn to observe religious obligations (i.e., those listed in Col. 2:16, 18). He argues that Christ has set them free from their previous declaration. He sees no reference in the papyri or in Colossians to a certificate of debt (though he acknowledges that sometimes the word does indeed refer in the papyri to a debt certificate, as we have argued). This is a possible view, which would fit with our following analysis of Col. 2:16–17 (i.e., that Christ has nullified the validity of the OT obligation to observe rituals; on which see). However, in light of the following considerations we still think the document referred to in Col. 2:14 is a document of debt: (1) the word can sometimes have this contextual meaning in the papyri; (2) the directly preceding phrase, “having forgiven us all our trespasses” appears to be further explained by “having canceled out the handwriting consisting of decrees against us, which were hostile to us.” (3) The double reference to the “decrees” being “against” the Colossians fits much better with cancelling out a debt of sin that was “against” the Colossians than with Christ freeing them from the observance of religious rituals. K. Kim never addresses how the double reference fits into his view or how it relates to “forgiveness” at the end of verse 13.

Some appear to be correct to see here in Col. 2:14 some association with the Jewish and early Christian background of a heavenly book in which sins have been recorded (cf. Sappington 1991: 100–107, 208). See Apoc. Paul 17 and Apoc. Zeph. 3.6–9, which refer respectively to a heavenly “document” (Eng. rendering of the Latin graffum, a transliteration of Greek γραφή, graphē) and to a “manuscript” (Eng. rendering of the Coptic) in which sins have been written, which are the basis of a person’s condemnation by God (cited also by Dunn 1996: 164; and Sumney 2008: 144; both of them say that the Latin and Coptic equivalents of χειρόγραφον are used respectively in these two documents). Sappington (1991: 215–17) also sees striking points of contact between Apoc. Zeph. 7.1–8 and Col. 2:14–15: (1) the transliterated word χειρόγραφον appears seven times in the Coptic text (also in chap. 3) and refers to sins written down as a record in heaven for judgment; (2) the “wiping out” of this record of shortcomings (see Col. 2:14) (3) leads to “triumph” and “prevailing over the accuser” (though the one triumphing is the sinner and not Christ, as in Col. 2:15); (4) as a consequence, the seer “crosses over” into the heavenly region of the “righteous ones.”

Some cite the relevance of a saying from the Jewish prayer Avinu Malkeinu: “Our Father, our King, erase through your great mercy all our letters of transgression / entries of transgression” (cited by Str-B 3:628; followed by Barth and Blanke 1994: 369).

2:15. In addition to the position taken in the commentary section, two other translations and consequent interpretations of the phrase ἀπεκδυσάμενος τὰς ἀρχὰς καὶ τὰς ἐξουσίας (apekdysamenos tas archas kai tas exousias) were held by many church fathers and others up until modern times but now are not held by the majority of commentators: (1) “he [Christ] divested himself of the powers and authorities,”124 and (2) “he divested himself of his flesh and made a show of the rulers and authorities” (so that “rulers and authorities” become the object of the verb “make a show of” and not of the participle “strip off”).125 Instead of taking the middle verb in an active sense, as preferred in the commentary section, these two renderings attempt to give full force to the reflexive sense of the middle voice of the verb, which it can sometimes carry.

The problem with the first translation is that God is the probable subject of both the participle “stripped” and the verb “made a show of” (see the footnote accompanying my comments on 2:15). It would be an unexpected change of subject from the previous verses to introduce Christ as the subject of the verb. Furthermore, this view entails the idea that Christ in his preresurrection body was subject to the demonic “rulers and authorities” but at his death cast off their authority over him. This is a problematic view, since Jesus was not always subject to the power of Satan but showed decisive power over him at various significant points throughout his earthly ministry (e.g., at the wilderness temptation and when he cast out demons from people; cf. further Luke 10:17–22). In support of this view, however, one could say that the cross was the consummative point where the devil and his powers were defeated, consummating what was begun in Jesus’s ministry.

The problem with the second rendering, like the first, is that God is the probable subject of both the participle “stripped” and the verb “made a show of.” Another weakness is that the idea of “flesh” has to be added, which is nowhere found in the Greek. The justification for this, however, is appeal to verse 11, where the noun form of the verb “strip off” occurs and which some understand (including this commentator) to refer to the “stripping off of the fleshly body” of Christ, who corporately represents believers in having their “fleshly body” stripped off. This is a possible link but not certain. This link is even more tenuous for commentators who see this phrase in verse 11 referring not to Christ but only to believers. As an additional problem with this position, it may imply that Jesus’s flesh was inherently evil and/or that Jesus’s resurrection body was not a body of flesh.

But most contemporary English translations provide a third and more probable view by which the middle-voice participle has active force (which can often be the case with middle-voice verbs; see, e.g., MHT 3:55; Zerwick 1963: 76). That this is the case is likely because when the related verb “strip off” (εκδύω, ekdyō) occurs with a following personal object, it is that personal object that receives the action of the stripping: one person strips clothes off another person (so D. Moo 2008: 213–14, who cites as LXX examples Gen. 37:23; 1 Sam. 31:9; 1 Chron. 10:9; Ezek. 16:39; 23:26; Hosea 2:5 [2:3 Eng.]; 2 Macc. 8:27; Matt. 27:28, 31; Mark 15:20; Luke 10:30; though in all these examples the verb is in the active and not the middle voice; Moo [2008: 214] claims that there are no examples of this verb in the middle voice followed by a personal object). The middle voice may still be expressed in this active translation with the sense that the action is done in the interest of the subject acting (following MHT 2:310; and Harris 2010: 98). The preposition ἀπό (apo) prefixed to the verb εκδύω likely intensifies the meaning of the verb but does not change its meaning.

For helpful summaries of the three above views of ἀπεκδυσάμενος (apekdysamenos) in Col. 2:15, see Lightfoot 1875: 189–91; Abbott 1897: 258–59; Moule 1957: 101–2; Bruce 1984: 107; O’Brien 1982: 127; Barth and Blanke 1994: 332–34; D. Moo 2008: 213–14.

115. The phrase “having been buried” is a rendering of the adverbial aorist passive participle συνταφέντες (syntaphentes) in 2:12. It is possible that the participle indicates time and action antecedent to the main verb (“were circumcised”), but more likely it points to a generally contemporaneous action (which typically occurs when an aorist adverbial participle modifies an aorist indicative verb; on this see Wallace 1996: 624–25). However, along with this general contemporaneous time element, the participle likely also more specifically indicates the means or manner by which the action of the main verb is carried out (on which, again, see Wallace 1996: 624: “Even if a participle is labeled as temporal, this does not necessarily mean that such is its only force. Often a secondary notion is present, such as means or cause”; yet I maintain that sometimes also the temporal element can be secondary). Context is one of the main clues in determining how an adverbial participle functions, and since the participle here is structurally parallel to the preceding adverbial phrases that indicate the manner or means of the main verb, so likewise probably does the participle συνταφέντες function. If a participle of means is in mind, then the participle would define how the action of the main verb is carried out (Wallace 1996: 629). It is possible that this is a participle of purpose, but this would be exceedingly rare in the aorist (1996: 636). Other logical uses of the participle are also not as likely here as manner or means. Salter (2010: 25–28), like D. Moo (2008: 201; see above) too narrowly categorizes the participle as only temporally contemporaneous.
