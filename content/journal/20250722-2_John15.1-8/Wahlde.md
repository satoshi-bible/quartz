
15:1-17
The Farewell Discourses (Part 2a): The Parable of the Vine and an Exhortation to Mutual Love
THE VINE, THE VINEDRESSER, THE BRANCHES

1 “I am the true vine and my Father is the vine dresser. 2 Every branch in me not bearing fruit, he removes; and every one bearing fruit, he prunes/cleanses so that it can bear more fruit. 3 Already you are clean because of the word that I spoke to you.
REMAINING IN JESUS

4 “Remain in me and I in you. As the branch is not able to bear fruit on its own unless it remains attached to the vine, so you also unless you remain in me. 5 I am the vine; you, the branches. The one remaining in me and I in that person, this is the one who bears much fruit because without me you are not able to do anything. 6 Unless a person remains in me, that person is cast out like the branch and withers; and they gather them together and throw them into the fire and they are burned. 7 If you remain in me and my words remain in you, ask for whatever you wish and it will be granted to you. 8 My Father is glorified in this, that you bear much fruit and become my disciples.
REMAINING IN LOVE

9 “As the Father loved me, I also loved you. Remain in my love. 10 If you keep my commandments, you will remain in my love just as I have kept the commandments of my Father and I remain in his love. 11 I have said these things to you in order that my joy may be in you and your joy may be complete. 12 This is my commandment, that you love one another as I loved you. 13 No one has greater love than this, that a person lay down his life for his friends. 14 You are my friends if you do the things that I command you. 15 I no longer call you slaves, because the slave does not know what the master is doing. I have called you friends because I have made known to you all that I heard from my Father. 16 You did not choose me, but I chose you and have appointed you so that you might go and bear fruit and that your fruit might remain so that whatever you request from the Father in my name, he would give to you. 17 These things I command you: love one another.”
NOTES TO 15:1-17

V. 1 the true vine This is the last of the “I am” sayings with a predicate in the Gospel. It differs from the other such sayings in that the statement identifies Jesus as the vine and then goes on to identify the Father as the vinedresser.

The use of the vine as a symbol is common in ancient religions. In addition to parallels in the Jewish Scriptures, parallels have been found in Gnosticism, the Mandean writings, Plato and elsewhere. (For examples, see Dodd, Interpretation 136-37.) Among the Old Testament passages most commonly thought to bear on the image of the vine in the Gospel are: Isa 27:2-6; Jer 5:10; 6:9; 12:10-11; Ezek 15:1-6; 17:5-10; 19:10-14; Ps 80:9-13; Hos 10:1; 14:8; 2 Esd 5:23. This symbolism also carries over into the rabbinic period. For examples of texts, see Str-B II 563-64.

Carson (and others) has argued that, in the OT, the vine and vineyard are symbols of Israel and so the portrayal of Jesus as the “true” vine suggests Jesus is the fulfillment of or the most adequate embodiment of what the vine represents. As attractive as this sounds, it is not clear how to call Jesus the “true people of Israel” would be relevant to the Johannine context. The issue is not whether Jesus is faithful to God but whether the branches are “faithful” to Jesus.

Another difference between the Johannine image and that used in the Old Testament is that there is no instance in the Old Testament where the vine is a means of life for the branches. Thus, it seems best to say that although the general symbolism of the vine is derived from the OT, it has been given a considerably new and distinctive orientation by the Johannine author. Jesus is being portrayed (1) as the true source (2) to which the branches must be attached.

The image of the vine as such does not appear in the Synoptics. And although the image of the vineyard appears in Mark 12:1-9 (parr. Matt 21:33-41; Luke 20:9-16), the emphasis is on the vineyard as a whole as a symbol of Israel. A vineyard also appears in Matt 20:1-16; 21:28-32 but in both of these cases the symbolism of the vineyard is not central to the parable.

R. Bauckham (“Parable”) points out the similarity between this passage and the explanation of the parable of the sheep and shepherd in chapter 10, except that only the explanation appears here. Bauckham goes on to argue that there may be vestiges of this “lost” parable in Acts of Thomas 146.

V. 2 he removes In Greek, v. 2 involves a play on the similarity of the words involved (paronomasia): “Every branch in me not bearing fruit, he removes (airei); and every one bearing fruit, he prunes (kath-airei).” Verse 3 then takes this up and states that “already you are clean (katharoi).”

he prunes/cleanses The double rendering of the Greek is done in order to reflect the etymology of the Greek verb kathairō and to reflect the similarity to the related term in the next verse. Although the parable may have Semitic roots, the paronomasia is based on Greek wording. Dodd (Interpretation 136) argues that the use of kathairō for “prune” was not common in viticulture but was probably introduced by the author of the Gospel because of the importance he attributed to the term theologically.

V. 3 because of the word The Greek here is dia ton logon. The preposition dia can take either the genitive or accusative case. With the genitive, it expresses instrumentality, as if the word were the means by which the disciples were cleansed. This is not the case here. As Bernard (Gospel 2:480) says: “In so far as this [the message of Jesus] continued to ‘abide’ in them (v. 7), in that degree were they ‘clean.’” This expresses well the meaning of the phrase in the light of the circumstances addressed by the author.

V.4 Remain in me and I in you The Greek word for “remain” here is menō. In vv. 4, 5, 6, 7, the word is used to describe a mutual relationship of great intimacy. That such mutual indwelling is intended is obvious from the fact that v. 4 speaks of the branch remaining in the vine and the vine remaining in the branch. From a literal point of view, the latter part of this statement is nonsense; from a theological perspective, it is totally understandable.

to bear fruit “Bearing fruit” is defined in v. 8 as “becoming my disciples.” The point is that true discipleship is not possible without remaining in the true vine. The statement is not intended to indicate that the followers of Jesus are not yet “disciples.”

V. 6 is cast out … and withers The verbs here are in the aorist and have caused considerable discussion. BDF 171, para. 333 describes this aorist as a gnomic and futuristic aorist that describes “[a]n act which is valid for all time” and gives examples. It is relatively infrequent in the New Testament, but appears also in v. 8.

Ezek 15:1-8, with its reference to casting into the fire the vine that fails to produce fruit, is almost surely the background against which this image is to be understood even though the Johannine text does not speak of casting the vine into the fire but only the branches that do not bear fruit.

V. 7 ask for whatever you wish On petitionary prayer, see at 14:13.

V. 8 is glorified This is an aorist form and probably reflects the anticipation of the glorification. It is another example of the gnomic aorist, which also occurred in v. 6 above. It could well be translated “has been glorified.”

bear much fruit and become my disciples Becoming a disciple is equated with bearing much fruit through the literary technique of hendiadys. There is a difficult textual problem concerning the word translated “become.” P66, B, Dea, a number of patristic references, as well as many Old Latin manuscripts have the aorist subjunctive (genēsthe). Other manuscripts (א, A, and a considerable number of minuscules) have the future indicative (genēsesthe). The UBS committee “found it exceedingly difficult to decide” between the alternatives and opted for the aorist subjunctive on the basis of “the age and diversity of the external support” (Textual Commentary 209). While it is difficult to know how precisely such distinctions were intended, it could be noted that the aorist form would be consistent with the view of the opponents that achieving discipleship was a once-and-for-all event. This is the reading that is adopted here.

V. 11 my joy This is the first of six texts in the Farewell Discourses that speak of joy. Here and in 17:13, Jesus speaks of his own joy and its relation to the joy of the disciples. The joy that is spoken of is an eschatological joy that will result from the successful completion of Jesus’ ministry. It is closely related to the keeping of the commandments and the fact that Jesus remains in the love of the Father. See also at 3:29 and 16:20. For a complete study of the Johannine understanding, see M. Arockiam, Joy.

V. 12 This is my commandment, that you love one another as I loved you. As has been proposed throughout, this is the second of the two specifically “Johannine” commandments.

V. 13 for his friends Some have attempted to argue that laying down one’s life for friends is less exalted than laying down one’s life for one’s enemies. To view this statement in that light is to misread the context of the statement. In v. 10, the love of Jesus for the disciples as well as the love of the Father for Jesus is said to be contingent upon obedience to Jesus and to the Father respectively. It is also commonly pointed out that the community’s love “of one another” would appear less universal than the expression of love in, for example, Matt 5:43-48. However, in all these instances, it is important to recognize that this love is being expressed within an apocalyptic worldview in which one “loves” good and “hates” evil. In the material of the third edition, the focus is apocalyptic throughout and the community is understood to be composed of those who have been chosen out of the evil world. The contingency of Jesus’ and the Father’s love is due to the fact that they cannot be said to react the same to one who does not keep the commandments as to one who does.

V. 15 I have made known to you all that I heard Although this is technically a contradiction to 16:12 (“I still have many things to say to you…”), it is probably intended to mean that he has not held anything back, even though there are some things that they will not understand now.

V. 16 I chose you This is the third of four texts in which Jesus is said to choose the disciples rather than being given them by God. For an explanation of the verb and its relation to the purposes of the third edition, see the Note on 6:70.

have appointed you so that you might go and bear fruit Here the emphasis is on the disciples as the product of the work of Jesus. It is his influence on them that makes it possible to bear fruit even though the disciples must cooperate.

Some (e.g., Bultmann) argue that the words “that you might go” (hypagō) are simply a pleonasm and have no independent meaning. Others (e.g., Barrett, Carson) would argue that the words refer to missionary activity. This is possible but, if so, the topic is not developed here. There will be clearer statements about those who believe because of the disciples in chapter 17.

whatever you request Prayer of petition has appeared earlier in 14:13 and 15:7. See the discussion there and especially the Addendum following 14:14.
THE COMPOSITION OF 15:1-17

These verses contain a parable and an explanation of that parable. The parable, which comes from the second edition of the Gospel, describes the relation between the vine, the vinedresser and the branches (vv. 1-3, 8) and speaks of the vinedresser cutting off branches that bear no fruit and pruning those that do. As was the case with the parable of the shepherd and the sheep, there are two explanations of the parable. The first, which comes from the second edition, is quite short and was probably edited considerably by the author of the third edition. The full extent of its original meaning is difficult to determine. The explanation that comes from the third edition has two basic elements to it. The material of vv. 4-7 focuses on the necessity of the branches “remaining” on the vine (and says nothing of the vinedresser). The material of vv. 9-17 focuses on the necessity of the disciples “remaining” in the love of Jesus. They will do this by keeping the commandment of mutual love that Jesus gives them.

The passage as a whole is only loosely tied to the context of the Farewell Discourses. It does not address any matters directly concerned with the departure of Jesus. Its only association with the genre of farewell is that it represents instructions to followers before the departure of Jesus. But it should also be remembered that almost all instruction to the disciples is reserved for these discourses.

A key to understanding the editorial history of the parable of the vine and its interpretation is, in addition to recognizing the internal literary features that suggest editing, to recognize the parallels between this, the interpretation of the parable of the sheep and shepherd, and the scene of the footwashing.1 These parallels are best described before beginning the analysis of the individual verses.
The Interpretation of the Shepherd Parable and the Interpretation of the Vine Parable

In 10:1-18, there is a single parable from the second edition with an interpretation from that edition as well as two interpretations (Jesus as the “gate” and Jesus as the shepherd “that lays down his life”) from the third edition. So here in 15:1-17 there is an original parable from the second edition (vv. 1-2a) involving a vine, a vinedresser, and branches. This is interpreted in vv. 3, 8. This parable is then developed in another way by the author of the third edition who speaks only of the relation between the vine and the branches. This interpretation focuses on the importance of the branches remaining in the vine, because without Jesus they are not able to bear fruit.

In chapter 10, the understanding of Jesus as the gate (from the third edition) shows Jesus as the sole source of salvation. Here in chapter 15, the interpretation of the vine and the branches (from the third edition) shows that “remaining” in Jesus is the only means of bearing fruit (i.e., Jesus as the sole source of salvation). The disciples are urged to remain in Jesus and are urged to have his words remain in them (vv. 4-7).

Returning to chapter 10, we see that the understanding of Jesus as the good shepherd (also from the third edition) presents him as the model of one who lays down his life for those he loves.

In chapter 15, the second development by the third author occurs in vv. 9-17 and speaks of the need to demonstrate mutual love. And as we have seen, this mutual love is in imitation of the love Jesus will ultimately demonstrate in his death.

Thus, both parables have secondary interpretations that develop the same two themes: Jesus as the sole source of salvation and the importance of self-sacrificing love.2 It should also be noted that in chapter 10, Jesus is “commanded” to lay down his life; in chapter 15, the disciples are “commanded” to imitate Jesus’ love.
The Interpretation of Jesus’ Washing of the Disciples’ Feet and the Interpretation of the Parable of the Vine

There are also significant parallels between the two interpretations of Jesus’ washing of the disciples’ feet in chapter 13 and the interpretations of the parable of the vine here in chapter 15. In both scenes, there is an interpretation by the second author that is modified by the third author. In addition, in both scenes there is a longer addition that also interprets the scene but in a significantly different way.
Interpretation by the Second Author and Modification by the Third

In that part of the account of the footwashing that comes from the second edition (13:4-11), Jesus speaks of a need to bathe, just as in 15:1-2, the material of the second edition speaks of the need to bear fruit. Both are essential. In both instances, the word katharos appears. In chapter 13, those who have bathed are “clean” (katharos) and, in chapter 15, those who bear fruit are “clean” (katharos).

However, there is also an addition to each of these passages from the author of the third edition. In 13:10, the third author has added the words “except for the feet” which specifies the need for a secondary washing of the feet for those who are already clean. In 15:2bcd, the third author speaks of the need for pruning (a secondary “cleansing”) for those who already bear fruit.

Thus in both scenes the issue is whether one is “clean” (katharos) or not. There are statements that indicate that the disciples are indeed “clean,” but in both there is nevertheless a second action in which those who are “clean” are subject to additional “cleansing.” But this is not all.
Re-interpretation of the Scene in Terms of Loving Service

In the footwashing scene, there is also a second interpretation added by the third author after the footwashing is complete. In this interpretation, the development is in terms of humble, loving service within the community. This is an extensive addition comprising roughly eight verses. This second interpretation also has a parallel in 15:9-17, where, as we have seen, the parable of the vine is developed in terms of the commandment of mutual love within the community.3 Thus in both the case of the footwashing and the parable of the vine, an original interpretation by the second author is modified by the third and then a totally new interpretation is added by the third author. As we will see below, the theology being communicated in both scenes is the same throughout.

Verses 1-3 (without v. 2cd) (2E) Vv. 1-2b, 3 represent the original presentation of the image of Jesus as the vine and these verses are independent of the development that begins in v. 4.4 There is considerable evidence that this is the case. In vv. 1-2b, 3, the topic is the relation between three elements: the vine, the vinedresser, and the branches. The vinedresser has a role only in vv. 1-3. In vv. 4-7, the topic is simply the relationship between the vine and the branches. Here in vv. 1-2b, 3, fruit-bearing is the condition for abiding (i.e., remaining on the vine and not being cut off) but, in vv. 4-7, abiding is the condition for bearing fruit.5 Moreover, in vv. 1-2b, 3, the vinedresser is the one responsible for removing branches and the branches that he removes are ones that do not bear fruit. In vv. 4-7, there is no role for the vinedresser and the branches seem to have “free will” whether to be attached or not and are exhorted to remain on the vine.6

The remarkable similarity in structure between 13:8-17 and 15:1-7 sheds further light on vv. 1-2b, 3, 8.7 In both instances, there is talk of a state that is essentially good but in need of improvement. In 13:8-11, Jesus speaks of a washing that is essential for the disciples (or they will have no heritage with Jesus) but at the same time Jesus refers to the need for a secondary washing of the feet. In chapter 15:2cd, the branches are bearing fruit, but the Father “prunes” (kathairei) them (so they can bear more fruit). In both instances, the same Greek word (katharos) appears in statements that are almost identical (13:10d: kai hymeis katharoi este; 15:3a: ēdē hymeis katharoi este …). The considerable similarity between these two statements (as well as the fact that such comments appear only here in the Gospel) suggests strongly that the same author is responsible for both.

This view is strengthened by the fact that each statement has also been reinterpreted in terms of mutual community relationships. Just as 13:8-11 sees the footwashing in terms of being clean and 13:12-17 sees the footwashing as an expression of love, so 15:1-3 sees the vine and branches in terms of being clean and vv. 9-14 develops the theme in terms of love. Thus, by identifying these interpretations as coming from the third author (more will be said of this in the following discussion), we are able to recognize the contrast between them and the “original” parable and so are able to identify that original parable as coming from a prior (i.e., the second) edition.

V. 2cd (3E) However, a problem remains. I have suggested that vv. 1-2b, 3 come from the second author and that, in form and function, they are parallel to the original version of the footwashing. Yet in the discussion of the Composition of 13:10, I have argued that the original version of the footwashing did not speak of a secondary cleansing and that the reference to a secondary cleansing (i.e., the words “except the feet”) was added by the third author. In chapter 13, there was considerable evidence that the words were secondary because of the disruption created by them both in terms of the meaning of the verse and in the manuscript tradition.

I would suggest that something similar has occurred here although the evidence of disruption is more subtle. A close examination of the text indicates that the original parable of the vine spoke only of the Father removing the branches that do not bear fruit. This unqualified statement is identical in intention to the statement of 13:8 where washing is necessary if one is to have a “heritage” with Jesus. However, as was the case in chapter 13, the third author has added the reference to a secondary cleansing (in the form of pruning in v. 2cd).

The first indication this is the case is simply the parallel between these verses and 13:8-11, namely the presence of a secondary cleansing and the use of katharos/katharizō in both instances.

The second indication is the overall use of katharos/katharizō in the Johannine literature. Forms of katharos/katharizō appear seven times in the Johannine literature but are used in a theological sense only in 1 John and within the third edition of the Gospel. In John 2:6 and 3:25 (which come from the first edition), the words refer simply to traditional Jewish ritual purification. All other instances (i.e., in 1 John and in the third edition of the Gospel) refer to forgiveness of sins. Twice, the terms appear in 1 John (1:7, 9) and refer to cleansing from sin (by the blood of Jesus and by the action of God). Then, the words appear four times in the Gospel (13:10, 11 and here in 15:2cd, 3). We have already seen that katharizō in 13:10, 11 refers to forgiveness of sin. In the light of this as well as the usage in 1 John, it is almost impossible to think that the term katharizō in 15:2cd does not also deal with the forgiveness of sin.

A third indication is that in v. 8, which is the only other verse to mention the role of the Father, there is mention only of “bearing much fruit” — there is no reference there to pruning or to bearing more fruit. The only mention of pruning is in v. 2cd. The addition by the third author is almost as brief here as it was in 13:10.

Vv. 4-7 (3E)8 As we have seen, in vv. 4-6 there is a shift in interpretation that speaks, not of pruning, but of the necessity of the disciples remaining in Jesus, of not being able to bear fruit on their own, and of not being able to do anything without Jesus. Here the focus is on the disciples as the active agents rather than on the Father. Without Jesus, the disciples will be cast out, will wither, and will be gathered and burned in the fire.

Most scholars would take v. 6 as referring to the action of the vinedresser in v. 2.9 However, if this were a development consistent with v. 2, the verse would say that every branch not bearing fruit will be cut off, etc. But this is not what is said. Rather, “the one that does not remain” speaks of a branch that has separated itself from the vine. This is not the image of v. 2ab where the branch has no inclination to separate itself but will be forcibly separated by the Father if it does not bear fruit.

In the comparison of this section with the two interpretations of the parable of the shepherd, we saw that in that parable Jesus was interpreted as the “gate” through which one had to go to receive life. We saw that, in chapter 10, this interpretation came from the third edition. The same thing is true here. The interpretation which urges the branches to remain in the vine comes from the third edition. There are three indications that this is the case. First, the images of “casting out,” “withering,” and being “gathered together to be thrown into the fire and burnt” are common apocalyptic images for eschatological judgment and punishment.10 Such apocalyptic imagery is typical of, and exclusive to, the third edition of the Gospel (see 3E-8 to 3E-18). Second, v. 7 speaks of the necessity of the words of Jesus “remaining” in the disciples.11 Thus, the stress on the historical words of Jesus counters the conviction of the opponents of 1 John who sought to replace those words with the unbridled inspiration of the Spirit. This is the theme of the first Johannine commandment and is central to both 1 John and to the third edition of the Gospel (3E-31). The emphasis on Jesus as the sole source of salvation also stresses the permanent role of Jesus in salvation, yet another of the central convictions of 1 John and the third edition (3E-32). Finally, the mutual indwelling/abiding of the believer in Jesus and Jesus in the believer is also distinctive of the third edition (3E-42). Thus, the identity of these verses seems clear.

V. 8 (2E) As I have suggested above, v. 8 seems to come from the second edition and was probably intended to resume the sequence from before v. 7cd (with some slightly different wording in the original). In v. 8, “bearing fruit” is equated with “becoming my disciples.” This notion does not fit with vv. 4-6 but the mention of “becoming my disciples” seems quite close in intention to the statement of 13:8-9 (“you will have no share with me”) which was described as the benefit accruing from (the first interpretation of) the footwashing in 13:8. Consequently, I would suggest that the verse is part of the original interpretation of the parable and is intended to symbolize an essential element of discipleship, as did 13:8.

Vv. 9-17 (3E) These verses contain a number of features that identify them with the third edition: the theme of “love,” of “commandment” and the way this “third” interpretation of the vine parallels the third interpretation of the parable of the shepherd.

In vv. 9-13, a new theme is introduced, that of mutual love (3E-15; 3E-38). Earlier, in 14:15-17, 20-21, 23-24, the keeping of the commandments in general was said to be a requirement of loving Jesus (14:15, 21b, 23a, 24) and a prerequisite for being loved by the Father (14:21c, 23d) and the Son (14:21d). Here, Jesus says that, in fact, he has kept the commandments and is loved by the Father, and he hopes that the disciples will keep the commandments so they will be loved by Jesus (15:9c, 10b, 14) (3E-6). He also hopes that the joy he experiences by being in the love of the Father will be experienced also by the disciples and that their joy will be fulfilled. This type of joy is also characteristic of the third edition (3E-36).

Just as Jesus had gone on to specify the commandment to “keep his word” in chapter 14, so here he goes on to specify the second commandment: that the disciples should love one another. He then describes his own love for them as the model and describes the extent of his love by the fact that it involves laying down his life.

In v. 13, Jesus’ death “for” his friends speaks of his death as something other than simply a departure and recalls the perspective of 10:15c, 17, where the good shepherd lays down his life “for” the sheep. No one has greater love than this: that a person lay down his life for his friends.12 Thus, his love is an atoning love for his own (3E-33). This love (vv. 9, 13) continues to reflect the overarching structure of the second half of the Gospel in the third edition (3E-25).

Verse 14 then defines Jesus’ “friends” as those who do what Jesus has commanded. This is simply a final definition of the fact that one is a friend if one remains in the love of Jesus and one does that by doing what Jesus commands, which in this case is defined as loving one another.13

Verse 16ab speaks of Jesus having “chosen” the disciples rather than their having chosen him. We have seen that this notion of Jesus “choosing” is a feature of the third edition elsewhere (3E-43). It stresses the initiative of Jesus alongside that of the Father who, in the second edition, is said to “give” the disciples to Jesus.

Verse 16d introduces the notion of petitionary prayer into the context. Previously it had appeared in 14:13-14. It will also appear in 16:23b-24 and 16:26-27. In all of these cases, it is associated with material from the third edition. This section then ends with a statement from the third edition that repeats the command to love one another (3E-15; 3E-38).

Throughout vv. 9-17 the topics and themes we have been dealing with are those of the third edition: the ministry of Jesus defined in terms of “commandments” given him by the Father, the parallel relationship between the commandment given to Jesus (laying down his life out of love) and that given to the disciples (loving one another), the death of Jesus as a sacrificial one, and the nature of the love as restricted to the community. All of this indicates that the material is from the third edition.
THE INTERPRETATION OF 15:1-17

After a very brief parable (vv. 1-3), which comes from the second edition, the material of this section is divided into two parts, which address, from the perspective of the third author, the two main topics addressed by 1 John: correct belief (vv. 4-7) and correct behavior (9-17).14 Each part is also structured in the same way. First, each is introduced with an exhortation to “remain,” first to remain in Jesus (cf. v. 4a) and second to remain in his love (cf. v. 9). Secondly, each makes proper performance (of belief and of conduct, respectively) the prerequisite for making petitions and having them granted by Jesus (see v. 7ab with respect to proper attitude toward Jesus and v. 16de with respect to the fruit of proper conduct).

However, as has been noted, the section as a whole appears without introduction and without any apparent relation to the surrounding context. We shall return to this issue following the Interpretation.
In the Second Edition
Vv. 1-3, 8

The remaining elements of the parable as it appeared in the second edition consist only of vv. 1-2ab, 3. The second author interpreted this image to mean that those branches (even though they were on the vine) were cut off if they did not bear fruit. The “fruit” spoken of here is explained in v. 8 as becoming a disciple of Jesus. According to v. 3, the disciples are clean because of the word of Jesus. This is quite direct and is intended to show the judgment (i.e., the removal of the branches) on those who do not believe in Jesus. By becoming disciples of Jesus (v. 8), the disciples also give glory to the Father just as Jesus did throughout his ministry. All of this is consonant with the theology of the second edition and contrasts with the perspective that follows.
In the Third Edition

Like the parable of the shepherd and the sheep, the parable of the vine and the branches has, in the present text, undergone a considerable transformation by the author of the third edition and has been made relevant to the circumstances of the community at the time of its internal crisis. It is an indication of the richness of the image that the community could see it as having an additional relevance at yet a later stage in the community’s history.
V. 2cd

These words represent the third author’s modification of the original parable in order to introduce the notion of a second stage of purification similar to the second stage of purification found in 13:1-11. Just as the followers of Jesus had borne fruit and had become “disciples” (and so, like the disciples of chapter 13, could be said to “have a heritage” with Jesus), so the disciples here in chapter 15 will have need of a secondary purification similar to that expressed by the secondary washing (of the feet) described in chapter 13.

As the third author had rejected (in chapter 13) the overly simplistic notion that a once-and-for-all purification (by the Spirit) was all that was needed, so here he again affirms that the disciple’s “bearing fruit” so as to become a disciple is not a once-and-for-all occurrence.

By emphasizing the need for further pruning/cleansing, the third author once again presents the view that, even if one has established a relationship with Jesus, such a relationship does not obviate the need for further cleansing.
Vv. 4-7

In these verses, the author weaves together three additional themes important to his theological perspective: (1) the importance of remaining in Jesus; (2) the mutual indwelling of Jesus and the believer; and (3) the conviction that the one who remains will receive what is asked for from Jesus. We will see each of these developed in its own way in the verses that follow.

The first and most important theme is the necessity and permanence of the disciples’ relationship with Jesus. The third author begins by exhorting the “branches”: “Remain in me and I in you.” This double imperative, as awkward as it is if taken literally, is an exhortation to the disciples to remain in union with Jesus. They (the branches/disciples) will bear no fruit unless they remain on the vine. In a literal reading, branches would have no ability to remove themselves; but, on the deeper level, this is a powerful image of the branches drawing their life support from Jesus. However, as part of this union of the branch with the vine, the third author emphasizes that Jesus will also remain in them. Although this is awkward within the context of the vine imagery, it is meaningful theologically from the perspective of the third author, who regularly reminds his reader of the reality open to the believer that the believer and Jesus will mutually indwell.

Verse 5 is largely a repetition of v. 4: the one remaining in Jesus (and Jesus in him) bears much fruit because without Jesus a person is not able to do anything.

After speaking this way of the benefits of remaining attached to the vine, the author also speaks (v. 6) about what will happen if the branch does not remain attached. It will be cast out, wither, be gathered and thrown into the fire where it will be burned. Images such as “cast out” and “burned” evoke the traditional apocalyptic scenes of divine punishment. Did this have some special meaning for the Johannine community? It is difficult to tell. Some suggest that it refers to Judas. The observation of Schnackenburg is perhaps the most illuminating. He suggests that it refers to the “sin-unto-death” of 1 John 5:16-17. However, Schnackenburg is reluctant to see this sin as referring to a falling away from faith. Yet, in the context of the crisis of 1 John, it is precisely the secessionists who do not remain “attached” to Jesus.15

While the primary focus of the material is on the importance of the branches remaining in the vine, the image of indwelling is developed briefly in v. 4a.16 In that statement, the author tells his reader that Jesus will also remain in the disciple. This notion of mutual indwelling between the believer and Jesus is found only in the third edition (3E-42). This develops the author’s positive theology and is only indirectly concerned with the views of the opponents.17 It builds on the conviction that Jesus and the Father abided in one another and now affirms this mutual relationship of the believer and Jesus.

Third and finally (v. 7), Jesus promises his disciples that, if they remain in him and his words remain in them, they may ask for whatever they wish and it will be granted.18 Although this overall statement regarding petitionary prayer is much the same as the initial one in v. 4, mention is now also made of the need for Jesus’ words to remain in the disciples. Once the author has set the two conditions (remaining in Jesus and allowing his words to remain in them), he goes on to make clear that Jesus will grant “whatever you wish.”19
Vv. 9-17

The theme now shifts to that of love, a topic not addressed in the previous verses. This time the exhortation begins with a reminder (v. 9) that Jesus has loved the disciples just as the Father has loved him. As he had done in vv. 4-7, Jesus again (v. 9c) exhorts the disciples to “remain.” This time it is an exhortation to “remain in my love,” an exhortation that is as awkward as the previous exhortation to “remain” in the vine. This exhortation is to be understood as expressing the hope that the disciples will do what is necessary to remain in Jesus’ love, for immediately (v. 10ab) we hear that keeping his commandments is necessary for the disciples to remain in this love. Then keeping the commandment of Jesus and remaining in his love is shown (v. 10cd) to parallel the fact that Jesus has kept the commandments of the Father and he remains in the Father’s love. Jesus explains (v. 11) that he wishes they may experience the joy he experiences from being loved by the Father and that their joy may be complete.

Then in v. 12ab, Jesus specifies the commandment he is thinking of as the commandment to mutual love. This love is to be modeled on the love that Jesus has for the believer (v. 12c). Jesus goes on to explain that there can be no greater love than the love he has for the disciples since there is no greater love than that which leads a person to lay down his/her life for “his friends.” And once again Jesus resorts to the language of contingency when he says that a person is a friend of Jesus if the person does what Jesus commands (v. 14). Jesus “no longer” calls the disciples “slaves” (obviously a rhetorical expression, not to be taken literally) because a slave does not know what the master is doing. But the disciples know all that Jesus is doing because Jesus had made known to them all that he heard from the Father (v. 15).

The language of contingency here strikes the modern reader as peculiar. How could one conceive of Jesus not keeping the commandment of the Father and not remaining in his love? However, this must be understood in the context of the purposes of the third edition. The commandment of love was understood by the author of 1 John and the author of the third edition to summarize all ethical obligation on the human level. The opponents believed that they were perfect and not in need of any such direction. Their conduct was spontaneously correct. However, here the author of the third edition stresses that the believer must keep the commandments and only if the believer does so will the believer remain in the love of God. That is to say that not keeping the commandments will merit condemnation on the last day. As he often does, the third author models the obligations of the believer on those of Jesus. The believers are to act as Jesus acted; that is, they are to love as Jesus loved. As a result, the third author portrays Jesus also being subject to the same sort of contingency even though the thought of Jesus not keeping the Father’s commandment is in itself unimaginable.

Jesus reminds the disciples that he has chosen them (v. 16ab) and he has appointed them so that they might bear fruit and it might remain (v. 16cde). By this sentence, Jesus recalls for the only time in these verses the theme of the vine and fruit. To say that their fruit “remains” is to say that they remain faithful to Jesus. Because of this faithfulness, they are able to make requests of the Father in Jesus’ name and he will grant them. The author then concludes by repeating (v. 17) Jesus’ commandment that the disciples love one another.

In retrospect, it is evident that the primary message of these verses is the giving of the love commandment. It is a fuller description of the commandment that was first mentioned, briefly, in 13:34-35 and is, in fact, the most extended discussion of love in the Gospel. It is the culmination of all the other references to mutual love in 1 John and in the third edition. As such, it constitutes the fullest expression of the type of conduct that is to characterize the disciple of Jesus.

But as we have seen again and again, although this exhortation would be important for any circumstances, it was of special importance for the Johannine community since some of the community at the time of the second edition were convinced that the gift of the Spirit was so radically transforming that there was no need for concern about principles of conduct. These additions by the third author affirm the fundamental importance of the love commandment as the root of all proper conduct. And so, together, vv. 4-7 and 9-17 constitute a treatise on issues central to the community crisis of 1 John.
