UNIT ONE, PART TWO (5:33–48)

Further illustrations of Jesus’s intensifying legal hermeneutic are provided in the second half of the segment that began in 5:21–32: the law’s prohibition against breaking oaths is interpreted as a prohibition against oath taking itself, the law’s prohibition against retaliation that exceeds the initial wrong done is interpreted as a prohibition against retribution as such, and the command to love the neighbor is interpreted as a command to love even the enemy. This intensification is carried along rhetorically through a number of graphic images that Jesus creates with his language, for example, of the earth serving as God’s footstool, of a person being struck on the cheek, or of people praying for their persecutors. As with the first triad of antitheses, these vignettes both expound the priorities laid out in 5:17–20 and furnish the readers with models of discipleship. As commentators often point out, Jesus’s instructions for his disciples in this part of the sermon culminate with his pronouncement regarding the love command, which names most definitively the greater principle by which their way of life must be directed. As the depictions in 5:38–42 show, “love” in this context has more to do with actions than with emotions. The disciples are to envision themselves overcoming evil with good, an aspiration that both differentiates them from other groups (tax collectors, gentiles) and reflects the perfection manifested by the goodness of the heavenly Father, which Jesus depicts with the common images of the sun rising and the rain falling.
Text

33“Again you have heard that it was said to the ancients, ‘You shall not swear falsely, but will carry out your oaths to the Lord.’ 34But I say to you, Do not swear at all, either by heaven, for it is the throne of God, 35or by the earth, for it is a footstool for his feet, or by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great king; 36and do not swear by your head, for you are not able to make one hair white or black. 37Let your word be ‘Yes, yes,’ ‘No, no’; anything more than these is from the evil one.

38You have heard that it was said, ‘An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.’ 39But I say to you, Do not resist an evildoer. Rather, whoever strikes you on the right cheek, turn the other to him as well. 40And to the one who wants to sue you and take your tunic, let him have your cloak as well. 41And whoever forces you to go one mile, go with him two. 42Give to the one who asks you, and do not turn away the one who wants to borrow from you.

43You have heard that it was said, ‘You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.’ 44But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, 45so that you may be sons of your Father in heaven, for he makes his sun rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and the unrighteous. 46For if you love those who love you, what reward do you have? Do not even the tax collectors do the same? 47And if you greet only your brothers, what more are you doing (than others)? Do not even the gentiles do the same? 48So you are to be perfect, as your Father in heaven is perfect.”
Commentary

The fourth unit of the antitheses is 5:33–37. In antiquity, swearing an oath (often ὅρκος) entailed solemnly invoking the presence of the deity as a witness or warrant for the veracity of one’s speech, especially in situations where the truth is uncertain or contested.203 By contrast, a vow (often εὐχή) is a solemn declaration addressed to God, promising either to offer God some object or action, or, alternatively, to abstain from doing so.204 That being said, in practice, the distinction between the two speech forms was often blurred, and in our texts, the applicable terms are often used in ways that lack precision. Among biblical authors, the risk of profanation attending the practice of oath taking is taken as self-evident (see below). This concern carried over into the Second Temple period, in which it was customary to use substitute formulae that avoided mentioning God’s name explicitly (e.g., Deut 6:13; 10:20), referring instead to God in an indirect manner, with the rabbis engaging in lengthy debates on which circumscriptions were binding and which were not.205

The use of the full introductory formula (cf. 5:33a) together with the adverb πάλιν (“again”) in 5:33 signals the beginning of a new subunit, the second triad of antitheses (5:33–48). As with the third unit (5:31–32), the thesis in this pericope (5:33b–c) is a summary rather than a quotation of Scripture, in this case a summary of several Scripture texts, the following being most relevant.206

You will not swear [οὐκ ὀμεῖσθε] by my name unjustly, and you will not profane the name of your God. (Lev 19:12)

Whatsoever person who will vow a vow to the Lord, or swear an oath [ὀμόσῃ ὅρκον] … he will not profane his word. (Num 30:3)

If you vow a vow to the Lord your God, you will not delay to carry it out [ἀποδοῦναι αὐτήν]. (Deut 23:22)

The word for “vow” in these texts is εὐχή, a term not found in Matt 5:33–37 (or anywhere in the First Gospel). Certain psalms also speak of the need to “carry out” vows, for example, Ps 49:14 LXX: “Carry out [ἀπόδος] your vows to the Most High.”207 The subject of the thesis in 5:33b–c is stated first negatively (“You will not swear falsely”), then positively (“but carry out your oaths to the Lord”), reflecting the structure of the subsequent exhortation, in which Jesus’s appeal to the audience is stated first negatively (5:34–36), then positively (5:37). The first part of the thesis (5:33b) is formulated using ἐπιορκέω (“to swear falsely”), a verb found elsewhere in biblical Greek only in 1 Esd 1:46 and Wis 14:28.208 The second part of the thesis (5:33c), meanwhile, speaks of carrying out an oath (ἀποδίδωμι + ὅρκος), a formulation that is not found in biblical Greek at all.209 It is also likely that in the background of our texts are the Decalogue’s prohibitions against both using the Lord’s name in vain (Exod 20:7; Deut 5:11) and bearing false witness (Exod 20:16; Deut 5:20).210 With this in mind, it is noteworthy that the form of the opening command (οὐκ ἐπιορκήσεις) in 5:33 has been shaped in such a way that it conforms to the Decalogue citations opening the first (οὐ φονεύσεις) and second (οὐ μοιχεύσεις) antitheses (5:21, 27).

Jesus’s teaching on the subject begins with a general prohibition of swearing (5:34a), which would have had the effect of protecting the sacredness of God’s name from being profaned (cf. 6:9). The ban is supported by an illustrative roster of four proscribed substitute formulae, namely, swearing by heaven (5:34b), by earth (5:35a), by Jerusalem (5:35b), and by one’s head (5:36). Each element in the list exhibits a parallel two-part structure, the first part in each instance beginning with μήτε (“neither … nor”), and the second with ὅτι (“because”). The reader’s responsibility is then stated positively (5:37a), with the final clause providing additional inducement (5:37b).

In terms of its ramifications for the evangelist’s readers, the list provided in 5:34b–36 is best interpreted against the backdrop of contemporary practice. According to certain rabbinic authorities, oaths sworn by heaven, by earth, or by one’s own head were not considered legally binding.211 Assuming such rulings as a background for the Matthean community, “any casuistic attempt to circumvent 34a is excluded; appeal to non-binding oaths—such as the rabbis specified—is rejected.”212 The opposition of heaven and earth in the first two illustrations (5:34b–35a) functions rhetorically to reinforce the sense of totality applied to the prohibition.213 Having moved from heaven to earth, the scene then moves to Jerusalem, the center of the earth (e.g., Ezek 5:5; 38:12).214 The logic in each of the first three explanatory clauses involves associating each referent with the sacral majesty of God.215 The significance of heaven and earth resides not in themselves but in the fact that they are related to God by virtue of being, respectively, God’s throne and footstool, the imagery being derived from Isa 66:1 (cf. Acts 7:49).216 The same reasoning is then applied to Jerusalem, which is described as “the city of the great King” (cf. Ps 48:2).217 Each formula is shown to be unacceptable because each refers (albeit indirectly) to God, and therefore does not avoid the problem of calling upon God as witness.

The fourth illustration (5:36) presents the reader with changes in both form and logic. Regarding the former, the fresh introduction of the verb ὀμνύω (“to swear”) in 5:36a (cf. 5:34a) not only disrupts the verbal pattern established by 5:34b–35 but also shifts the discourse to the second person singular (ἐν τῇ κεφαλῇ σου), with 5:37 then returning to the second person plural (ὁ λόγος ὑμῶν). Regarding the latter, Jesus now drops the theological explanation of 5:34b–35 in favor of an argument more at home in the world of proverbial thought.218 If 5:34b–35 drew attention to the power of God, 5:36 draws attention to the power of human beings or, more precisely, their lack of power. Even to swear by one’s own head oversteps human limits, since even the color of the hair on one’s head has been determined by the creator.219

Jesus does not only offer a critique of existing practices, however, but also proposes for his followers an alternative praxis (5:37), one that transcends the matter of oath taking by addressing the exigencies of human speech at a more fundamental level. In the context of a discussion about taking God’s name in vain, Philo expresses his approval of people who are “in the habit of saying only, ‘Yes, by —’ or ‘No, by —’ and add nothing more, and by thus breaking off suggest the clear sense of an oath without actually making it” (Spec. 2.4).220 Jesus similarly conceptualizes oath taking as something “more” (τὸ περισσόν), not in the sense of something superfluous (as Philo would have it), however, but in the sense of something evil, the final clause of 5:37 alluding to the idea that falsehood has its origin with the devil (cf. John 8:44).221 The unit concludes, then, with a troubling assertion regarding not only the reality of evil but also its potential to corrupt the readers’ lives (cf. 5:39, 45; 6:13, 23, each also with πονηρός).222

To counter this influence, Jesus requires that his followers commit to unqualified truthfulness by restricting themselves to direct affirmations and denials, the doubling of words (“Yes, yes” or “No, no”) serving to express certainty (cf. 2 Cor 1:17–18).223 If consistently observed, this demand for uncompromised verbal integrity renders the distinction between forms of speech (i.e., between forms that require oaths and forms that do not) irrelevant, the assumption being that ordinary speech ought to be sufficient.224

Considered in this light, the invocation of special witnesses by means of the oath is seen to have the paradoxical effect of actually trivializing one’s speech, since the practice represents a tacit admission that one’s speech is unreliable without it. Moreover, if it is true that “the mouth speaks out of that which fills the heart” (12:34), then the practice of swearing runs the risk of trivializing not only one’s speech but also one’s self, that is, one’s moral integrity. That being the case, in the requirement that Jesus imposes, “all places of appeal outside the self are rejected.”225

While concern is routinely expressed by biblical authors regarding the manner or veracity of oaths,226 the closest approximation we have to 5:34a as a principle of law comes from Deut 23:22 (“If you refrain from vowing, it will not be a sin for you”).227 For an actual prohibition of the practice, we must turn to the Essenes, at least as they are represented by Josephus: “Every statement of theirs is surer than an oath [ὅρκου] and with them swearing [ὀμνύειν] is avoided, for they think it worse than a false oath [ἐπιορκίας]. For they say that the one who is not trustworthy except when he appeals to God is already under condemnation” (Bell. 2.135).228 Whether this description accords with Essene practice is debatable, since elsewhere Josephus himself describes the sect’s initiatory oaths (Bell. 2.139–142).229 The categorical rejection of oaths in fact would have been unusual,230 our best examples coming not from Jewish but from Pythagorean traditions (Diogenes Laertius, Vit. phil. 8.22; Iamblichus, Vit. Pythag. 9.47). More common is the position staked out by Philo in Decal. 84, who contends that “to swear not at all is the best course,” though he quickly adds that “to swear truly” represents the next best alternative.231 Elsewhere he condemns much swearing as a source of impiety (Decal. 92) and “proof of faithlessness” (Spec. 2.8).232 Within the gospel itself, there is the negative example of Peter, who denies Jesus by swearing an oath (26:74, also with ὀμνύω), as well as the positive example of Jesus, who refuses to speak under oath (26:63). In the third woe (23:16–22, also with ὀμνύω), meanwhile, the distinction between kinds of oaths made by Jesus’s opponents is used as evidence of their guile and hypocrisy.

The most compelling parallel to our text, however, comes not from elsewhere in the gospel, or from one of the other gospels, but from the Epistle of James: “Do not swear either by heaven or by earth or by any other oath, but let your ‘Yes’ be yes and your ‘No’ be no, lest you fall under judgment” (5:12).233 The extent of structural and verbal agreement indicates that we are dealing with alternative forms of the same tradition, while differences between the two texts make a direct literary connection unlikely.234 In both cases, the initial prohibition, “do not swear” (μὴ ὀμόσαι in Matthew, μὴ ὀμνύετε in James) is followed by a list of examples separated by μήτε (“neither … nor”), the first two of which are heaven and earth. James lacks Matthew’s explanatory clauses (and the biblical allusions that go with them), as well as the references to swearing by Jerusalem and one’s head, in lieu of which, he has a blanket prohibition (“or by any other oath”) that corresponds functionally to the “at all” (ὅλως) of 5:34a. Both versions conclude with an imperative (ἔστω in Matthew, ἤτω in James) incorporating the emphatic repetition ναὶ ναί, οὒ οὔ (“yes, yes,” “no, no”), though the manner in which they present the formula is different.235

The subject of evil, which was broached in the conclusion to 5:33–37, carries over into the next unit (5:38–42), with the repetition of πονηρός in 5:37, 39, 45 serving as a catchword linking the fourth antithesis to the fifth, as well as the fifth to the sixth. Like the fourth antithesis, the fifth presents four illustrations, though unlike the fourth, none of them are furnished with explanations. At this point, the emphasis falls on the element of command,236 with the elements of explanation and motivation being postponed until the sixth antithesis, which draws on the same complex of logia from the synoptic sayings source as the fifth (cf. Luke 6:27–36).237 The fifth and sixth units are further bound by the use of the same introductory (“You have heard that it was said”) and pronouncement (“But I say to you”) formulae (5:38, 43; cf. 5:27).

The double expression cited in 5:38 (“an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth”) is found in Exod 21:24, Lev 24:20, and Deut 19:21. Of these, the third is the most likely source for the argument here, since the immediate context of the verse includes references to one person “resisting” (ἀντέστη) another (Deut 19:18) and to the problem of dealing with “the evil” (τὸ πονηρόν) in the community’s midst (Deut 19:19–20). If this is the case, then the principle Jesus articulates in 5:39 (ἀντιστῆναι τῷ πονηρῷ) can be understood to invoke biblical language as well.238

The law of the talion was a rule of public justice that regulated the extent of retribution in cases of personal harm by applying the principle of punishment in kind, thereby curtailing indiscriminate violence.239 In his response, Jesus does not reflect on the appropriateness of the lex talionis as a judicial rule, his concern instead being with its inappropriateness as a standard of communal ethics. By instructing his followers not to resist evildoers, Jesus repudiates both the concept of (equivalent) retribution upon which the law rests and the use of violence that it sanctions. In its place, he lays out an alternative strategy, one that fulfills the law (5:17) not by curtailing retaliation further but by eliminating it altogether.240 Moreover, this strategy entails not simply refraining from retribution but responding to injustice with actions that constitute a visible protest against the use of force, actions through which one’s “light” shines before others (5:16).241 The sorts of situations presented in the unit would have had special meaning for a group experiencing persecution (see 5:11–12, and note the reference to persecutors in 5:44),242 and, insofar as Jesus’s instructions regarding how to react in such situations embody a countercultural stance, the patterns of behavior they illustrate can be interpreted as a manifestation of the group’s prophetic identity (5:12).243 Accordingly, the contrast established by these patterns between the norms of Jesus’s followers and those of their antagonists acts as a messianic provocation, that is, as a sign of the kingdom’s opposition to the world.

This contrast represents a moral approach that takes as its starting point the virtues of the Beatitudes, especially the virtues of humility (5:3), meekness (5:5), and peacemaking (5:9). Given the citation of Lev 19:18b in 5:43, another likely starting point would have been Lev 19:18a (“You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against any of your people”), which in turn belongs to a wide-ranging critique of retaliation in antiquity, illustrated by texts such as Prov 17:13; 20:22; 24:29 (“Do not say, ‘Thus I will do to him as he has done to me; I will render to the man according to his work’”); 1QS X, 17–18 (“To no man will I return evil for evil; I will pursue a man only for good”); Rom 12:17 (“Do not repay anyone evil for evil, but take thought for what is noble in the sight of all”); 1 Thess 5:15; 1 Pet 3:9.244

The general command of 5:39a (in the second person plural) sets forth a guiding principle for the alternative strategy mentioned above, while the ensuing commands in 5:39b–42 (in the second person singular) present different scenarios that show the principle being put into effect.245 For their part, the four illustrations exhibit an alternating parallel construction, with the first (5:39b) and the third (5:41) beginning with ὅστις (“whoever”) + σε (“you”) + verb, while the second (5:40) and the fourth (5:42) begin with τῷ (“the”) + participle + σοι/σε (“you”). The illustrations are also structurally similar in that each contains two clauses. In the first three cases, the first clause focuses on the provocation of an antagonist who is representative of the evil the reader is commanded not to resist (“whoever strikes you,” “whoever wants to sue you,” “whoever forces you to go a mile”), while the second clause focuses on the reader’s response to the provocation. In contrast to the primary command of 5:39a, which is expressed negatively (“do not resist”), the secondary commands in 5:39b–41 are expressed positively (“turn the other cheek to him,” “let him have your cloak as well,” “go with him two miles”). The fourth illustration (5:42) breaks from this pattern in that it presents two “antagonists,” one in each clause (“the one who asks you,” “the one who wants to borrow from you”), and in that it includes two commands, one in each clause, the first one being positive (“give”) and the second negative (“do not refuse”). With respect to the overall structure, it appears that the scenarios have been arranged in descending order of severity, beginning with physical assault (5:39b) and concluding with financial importunity (5:42).

A version of the first command (5:39b) appears in Q/Luke 6:29a, though the latter uses different verbs and fails to mention the right cheek.246 The likely source for Matthew’s language is Isa 50:6, in which the suffering servant declares, “My back I gave to scourges, and my cheeks to strikes [τὰς δὲ σιαγόνας μου εἰς ῥαπίσματα].”247 A slap to the face was a sign of contempt (e.g., Job 16:10; 2 Cor 11:20). By mentioning the right cheek, Matthew indicates either a backhanded (cf. m. B. Qam. 8:6) or left-handed (cf. 1 Esd 4:30) strike, either of which would have been considered especially offensive.248 In response to personal insult, the reader is enjoined not to turn away, that is, simply to refrain from retaliation, but to turn the other cheek, that is, to confront the aggressor with one’s vulnerability to further abuse. It is left to each reader to decide how the imagery might contribute practically to the realization of a “greater righteousness” in his or her own comportment.249

The same logic applies to the second command (5:40), the scenario in this case being that of a lawsuit. Jewish men typically wore two garments, an inner tunic (χιτών) and an outer cloak (ἱμάτιον).250 According to the law of pledges (Exod 22:26–27; Deut 24:12–13), if a creditor takes the latter as a pledge from one of his debtors, he is required to return the garment to him before the sun sets, so that he has something to sleep in.251 In Matt 5:40, it appears that a creditor is suing the reader for his tunic as collateral for a debt, an action that no doubt would have struck many of Matthew’s readers as extreme (cf. Amos 2:6–8). Nevertheless, the defendant is advised to surrender the tunic being demanded as well as the more valuable cloak, in this manner not only relinquishing his rights but also “exposing” both himself and the shamelessness of the creditor. Again, what is expected extends beyond passive tolerance of mistreatment to a dramatic demonstration of one’s vulnerability (cf. 1 Cor 6:7). Comparison can be made with the illustration provided in 5:25–26, which also appears to envisage a legal matter concerning debt. Once more, the courts are presented as places where the reader can expect mistreatment.252 The version of the logion in Q/Luke 6:29b, which refers to the tunic and cloak in reverse order and (again) uses different verbs, envisions instead the situation of a robbery (cf. Luke 6:30b).253

The third command (5:41) shifts scenarios yet again, this time giving an example of corvée. The act of pressing civilians and their animals into temporary service for the authorities had been customary and legal since Persian times,254 though the practice was subject to abuse.255 The case of “going one mile” probably refers to an individual being enlisted to act as a soldier’s porter, the use of μίλιον (cf. Latin mille) for the unit of measurement giving the description a Roman flavor. Epictetus provides a partial parallel: “If there is a requisition [ἀγγαρεία] and a soldier seizes it [i.e., your donkey], let it go, do not resist or grumble. If you do, you will be beaten and lose your donkey just the same” (Diatr. 4.1.79).256 In contrast to the philosopher’s pragmatic approach, Matthew’s readers are instructed to regain the initiative by going beyond mere acquiescence and confronting the perpetrator with a gesture (“go with him two miles”) analogous to those specified in 5:39b and 5:40 (cf. Did. 1.4). The scenarios addressed by the first three commands are also similar in that examples of each can be found in the passion narrative: Jesus is struck by the Sanhedrinists (26:67, with ῥαπίζω), his garments are taken by the soldiers (27:35, with ἱμάτια), and Simon of Cyrene is compelled to carry his cross (27:32, with ἀγγαρεύω).

The same cannot be said regarding the unit’s fourth and final command (5:42), where significant differences emerge in the nature of the referents. It is unlikely that beggars and borrowers are meant to function as examples of evildoers (5:39),257 and when approached by such individuals, the reader cannot (as in the previous scenarios) be understood as a victim. Instead, 5:42 is linked to 5:40–41 by the theme of receptiveness to the demands of others.258 The practice of almsgiving will be taken up in 6:2–4, while 6:19–24 encourages generosity in the use of one’s material possessions. The directive in 5:42a (“Give to the one who asks you”) has a counterpart in Q/Luke 6:30a, which differs in stipulating that one should give to “everyone” who asks (cf. Did. 1.5).259 The directive not to refuse a borrower (5:42b), meanwhile, has a more oblique parallel in Luke 6:34–35a: “If you lend to those from whom you expect to receive, what credit is that to you? Even sinners lend to sinners, to receive as much again. Instead, love your enemies, and do good, and lend, expecting nothing back” (cf. Gos. Thom. 95).260 While Matthew shares with Luke the verb for lending (δανείζω), he lacks the language about requiring repayment, which is inspired by pentateuchal injunctions against lending with interest (Exod 22:24; Lev 25:35–38; Deut 23:20–21; cf. Deut 15:7–11; Prov 28:8).

What the two parts of the verse in Matt 5:42 have in common is that each concerns the use of money, and in each case, the reader’s obligation is dictated by the request of the other person, with no conditions attached. A combination of directives similar to that found in 5:42a+b occurs in Virt. 83, where Philo explains how Moses urged his compatriots “to give freely with unrestrained hands and hearts to those in need,” and if this was not possible, “at least to be ready and willing to lend, receiving nothing back beyond the principal” (cf. Deut 15:8).261 In the same vein, Sir 29:1–13 combines a series of precepts on lending (29:1–7), which is interpreted as an act of mercy (29:1), with instructions about almsgiving (29:8–13).262

As noted above, the sixth unit of the section (5:43–48) builds upon the fifth, the two passages being linked verbally by the use of the keyword πονηρός (“evil”) in 5:39 and 5:45. The examples of nonretaliation presented in 5:39–42 are now seen as illustrations of an expansive understanding of love, for which we can gain a glimpse also in b. Šabb. 88b: “Those who are insulted but do not insult and hear themselves reviled without answering, act through love and rejoice in suffering.” The sixth unit also builds on the fifth by undergirding its appeal with needed explanation and motivation. The readers’ response to evil in the world is to be modeled on an understanding of the nature of God’s involvement in the world (5:45, 48), a standard of comportment that differentiates them from evildoers (5:46–47).263

The unit’s thesis (5:43), which presents a pair of contrasting commands (cf. 5:33), is juxtaposed by the antithesis (5:44), which presents a pair of complementary commands (cf. 5:32). The next verse (5:45) then offers a theological rationale for the antithesis (5:45a) illustrated by a pair of matching descriptions of divine activity (5:45b). A second rationale is provided by two pairs of rhetorical questions with matching descriptions of human activity (5:46, 47), the second question in each instance providing an answer to the first. While the reasons for compliance with the antithesis provided by 5:45 are stated positively, in 5:46–47 they are stated negatively. The conclusion (5:48) then creates a frame around the questions by restating the theological rationale of 5:45a in the form of an injunction.264

The fifth and sixth units are also connected in that they draw on the same block of sayings in Q/Luke 6:27–36.265

Matthew rearranges the Q material into two units (5:38–42, 43–48) that are distinct both thematically and formally, the former posing a string of commands (5:39–42), while the latter relies on a combination of motive/descriptive clauses (5:45a–b) and rhetorical questions (5:46–47). Matthew also differs from Luke in presenting the material in antithetical form, that is, in relation to the Mosaic law.

The opening formula in 5:43 (which is the same as the one used in 5:38) introduces two commands, the first of which is a partial citation of Lev 19:18 LXX (“you will love your neighbor”), with the phrase “as yourself” omitted, so as to enhance the symmetry with the second command (“and hate your enemy”). Leviticus 19:18 was a formative text for both Jewish (e.g., Sir 13:15; Let. Aris. 228; Jub. 7.20; CD VI, 20–21; m. ʾAbot 1:12) and Christian (e.g., John 13:34; Rom 13:9; Gal 5:14; Jas 2:8) ethics.266 The Matthean Jesus will cite the verse (both times with “as yourself” included) again in 19:19 (together with a set of commandments from the Decalogue) and 22:39 (together with Deut 6:5, in response to a question about the greatest commandment). The second command in 5:43 does not cite Lev 19:18 but rather proposes for it a possible corollary, since the biblical text leaves open the possibility of restricting love to the “neighbor” (i.e., a fellow Israelite). Jesus’s teaching here “fulfills” the law (5:17) by effectively universalizing the statute in Lev 19:18. The obligations of love must extend to all people, even enemies.267 The precise command, “hate your enemy,” occurs nowhere in the Old Testament, though similar statements can be found in Pss 26:5 (“I hate the assembly of evildoers”), 139:21 (“Do I not hate those who hate you, O Lord?”), and elsewhere.268 The command’s actual wording, meanwhile, probably derives from Q (note that Luke 6:27 has both μισέω and ἐχθρός).

The antithesis (5:44) is linked to the thesis (5:43a) by the noun ἐχθρός (“enemy”) and by the verb ἀγαπάω (“love”),269 though a shift occurs from the second person singular to the second person plural, matching the form of the command in Q/Luke 6:27a. In contrast to Luke, who explicates the love command with three further commands (“do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you”), Matthew explicates it with only one further command, “pray for those who persecute you” (5:44b).270 The reader, then, is encouraged to understand “enemies” especially as a reference to the opponents of God’s people (cf. 5:10–12, also with διώκω). To “love” one’s enemies, meanwhile, is seen to entail praying for them,271 that is, interceding to God on their behalf,272 a priority that prepares the way for 5:45, which holds up God’s indiscriminate goodness to humanity as a model for the reader’s conduct.273 The command to love enemies thus encompasses both intention (5:44) and action (5:45), with the figure of God playing a central role in both elements. Comparison in this regard can be made with T. Jos. 18.2, though instead of encouraging divine imitation, it promises divine deliverance: “If anyone wishes to do you harm, you should pray for him, along with doing good, and you will be rescued by the Lord from every evil.”

In 5:46–47, tax collectors and gentiles represent (from a Jewish perspective) stereotypical examples of sinners (cf. 11:19),274 which is the term that Luke uses in his version of the logion: “If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you? For even sinners love those who love them. For if you also do good to those who do good to you, what credit is that to you? For even sinners do the same” (6:32–33). Whereas Luke has two rhetorical questions, each followed by a corresponding statement, Matthew has two pairs of corresponding rhetorical questions.275 The opening questions in the two gospels are especially similar, though in lieu of “credit” (χάρις) in Luke 6:32a (cf. Did. 1.3), Matt 5:46a speaks of “reward” (μισθός), a term that links the passage not only to the Beatitudes (5:12), but also to the unit that follows (6:1–18), where the term figures prominently (6:1, 2, 5, 16).276 Love that is based merely on the principles of reciprocity and social identity represents (from this perspective) conventional behavior and therefore accomplishes nothing deserving of a reward.277 In 6:33a, meanwhile, Luke speaks of “doing good” (cf. 6:35), whereas Matthew implicitly challenges his readers to do “more” (περισσός), the formulation in 5:47a recalling the definition of greater righteousness in 5:20 (with περισσεύω). If the readers are expected to exceed the standard of comportment set by the religious elite, the same certainly applies to the standard set by sinners and outsiders.278 As a further example of such comportment, Matthew (in contrast to Luke) refers to the practice of “greeting” not only one’s brothers (ἀδελφοί), that is, fellow believers,279 but also outsiders, that is, those most likely not to return such a gesture. As 23:6–7 indicates, to extend a greeting (ἀσπασμός) to another person, especially in a public setting, represents a way of honoring that person (cf. Matt 10:12–13 || Luke 10:4b–6).280

For the conclusion to the antithesis (5:43–48) and the antitheses (5:21–48), Matthew returns again to Lev 19, indeed to a verse that functions as a heading for the various statutes assembled in that chapter:281 “You will be [ἔσεσθε] holy because I the Lord your God am holy” (Lev 19:2).282 The theologoumenon of the imitatio Dei, then, which serves as a summing up of the entire section,283 has its origins in biblical law. The version of the command preserved in Q/Luke 6:36 refers to the standard of being “merciful” (οἰκτίρμων),284 while Matthew’s version in 5:48 entails the assimilation of the command’s wording to a text from Deuteronomy: “You will be perfect [τέλειος] before the Lord your God” (Deut 18:13).285 Jesus’s teaching fulfills the law (5:17) by shaping moral agents who are committed to obeying the will of God “fully” or “perfectly” (cf. Col 4:12),286 that is, with a pure heart (5:8).287 A similar use of language with respect to the law is at work in 3 Kgdms 8:61: “Let our hearts be perfect [τέλειαι] toward the Lord our God, and to walk with holiness in his ordinances and to keep his commandments.”288 For τέλειος in relation to the demand Jesus imposes on his followers, comparison can also be made with Matt 19:21 and Did. 6.2 (“If you can bear the whole yoke of the Lord, you will be perfect; but if you cannot, do what you can”). Perfection as an attribute of God, meanwhile, is in this context illuminated especially by 5:45 (which also refers to God as Father), with its depiction of God’s unrestrained and all-embracing goodness.289
Conclusion

If rhetorically the love command is the most striking element of the unit in 5:33–48, from the perspective of Christian morality it is also the most generative.290 Antecedents for Jesus’s love command can be located in various Old Testament passages, especially texts that contain injunctions to aid one’s enemy, such as Exod 23:4–5 and Prov 25:21–22.291 The command also comes to expression in various New Testament texts, especially Rom 12:14 (“bless those who persecute you”), 1 Cor 4:12, and 1 Pet 3:9.292 The idea that the obligation to love encompasses groups besides one’s neighbors is broached by Leviticus itself: “The stranger who resides with you will be to you as the native among you, and you will love him as yourself” (Lev 19:34). Betz also suggests as a parallel Sir 6:1 (“do not become an enemy instead of a friend”),293 though it should be mentioned that at no point in 5:38–48 does Jesus suggest to the audience that obedience to his commands will result in the conversion of persecutors or reconciliation with evildoers. As 5:45 and 5:48 make plain, the readers’ motivation is to arise instead out of a commitment to imitate God by making God’s grace manifest to others. As comparison with the Beatitudes suggests, the imitatio Dei is grounded not only in a recognition of the grace that God manifests to all humanity (5:45) but also in the grace that the readers have experienced specifically as members of God’s people.

Indeed, 5:45a can be understood as an expansion of the seventh beatitude, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God” (5:9). As in 5:9 (cf. 5:12), in 5:45 the aspiration of becoming God’s children is associated with the expectation of receiving an eschatological reward (5:46).294 The text of Luke 6:35b also mentions a reward (μισθός), though instead of promising that “you will be sons [υἱοί] of the Most High,” Matthew’s version speaks of the readers becoming sons of their heavenly Father (cf. 5:48), in keeping with the sermon’s proclivity for familial imagery.295 The two authors also differ in their depictions of God’s activity. Whereas Luke describes how God “is kind to the ungrateful and the evil” (6:35c), Matthew opts for more vivid language: “he makes his sun rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and unrighteous” (5:45b).296 The reader now sees the evildoer of 5:39 (also with πονηρός) as a beneficiary of the impartiality which God provides for creation. That such imagery was proverbial in character is suggested by comparison with texts such as the following.

If you are imitating the gods, you say, then bestow benefits also upon the ungrateful; for the sun rises also upon the wicked, and the sea lies open to pirates. (Seneca, Ben. 4.26.1)

Rain is both for the righteous and the wicked. (b. Taʿan. 7a)

Have you ever seen rain come down on the field of So-and-so who is righteous, but not on the adjacent field of So-and-so who is wicked? Of course the sun shines upon those in Israel who are righteous, but it also shines upon those who are wicked. (Pesiq. Rab. 48.4)

The idea that children should be “like” God their father informs moral appeals elsewhere, for example, Eph 5:1–2 (“Be imitators of God, as beloved children, and walk in love”). We also have the idea that by conferring benefits on others, one imitates God,297 for example, in Let. Aris. 281 (“As God showers blessings upon all, you too in imitation of him are a benefactor to your subjects”) and Exod. Rab. 26.2 (“Resemble me; just as I repay good for evil, so do thou also repay good for evil”).298 Among Greco-Roman authors, such ideas were developed especially in conjunction with discussions about the meaning of φιλανθρωπία (“love of humanity”),299 a virtue that, according to Philo, the Mosaic law extends even to enemies (Virt. 109–119).300 The rhetorical force of this unit, then, can be seen especially in its effect of bringing emerging Christian discourse into contact with diverse streams of moral thought in the ancient Mediterranean world, thereby lending the sermon a certain universalizing quality.
