Form/Structure/Setting
Ps 137 defies straightforward classification in form-critical terms. Schmidt (242) regarded it as unique. Gunkel (580) commented that it begins as if it were a communal lament, continues like a hymn, and ends as a curse. Dahood (269) characterized it as a lament. There is favorable evidence for this label: besides the description of woe in vv 1–3, which refers both to the sufferers and to their foes, vv 5–6 can be understood as an implicit confession of trust, while v 7 is a petition for punishment. These are elements of the lament (cf. Westermann, Praise, 52–64; for, “remember,” in v 7, cf. Schottroff, ‘Gedenken,’ 35). Moreover, the demand of v 3 is related to the mocking questions of laments (Pss 79:10; 115:2; Kraus [1989] 503). So G. S. Ogden (JSOT 24 [1982] 89–90) simply labeled it a lament, consisting of a description of crisis (vv 1–3), a confession of trust (vv 4–6), and a petition (vv 7–9).

Can one say more? Schottroff (‘Gedenken,’ 145), followed by Kellermann (ZAW 90 [1978] 48–51), endeavored to do justice to the psalm as a whole by describing it as a modified song of Zion. There are two types, a formal communal one, such as Pss 46, 48, 76, 87, and a less formal, individual or solo one, found in Pss 84, 122. In fact the form-critical label “song of Zion” is derived from Ps 137:3, though the reference here need not be so specific. Schottroff drew attention to the dominance of Zion or Jerusalem (vv 1, 3, 5–7). Over against the past impossibility of singing any of the songs of Zion in exile is set the present relative joy of self-committing loyalty to Jerusalem, which under the circumstances must replace the normal praise, as worship is offered at the site of the destroyed temple. Kellermann developed this categorization by noting that direct address of Zion (vv 5–6aβ) is an element of the song of Zion (Pss 87:3; 122:2, 6–9; cf. Jer 31:23). The circumstances of a destroyed city required mention of its destruction in v 7 as a reversal of the normal reference to its firm construction (Pss 46:4, 6–8, 12 [3, 5–7, 11]; 48:9, 13–15 [8, 12–14]; 87:5; 122:6–7). The commendation formula in vv 8–9 is found in a song of Zion (Ps 84:5, 6, 13 [4, 5, 12]). Here it is retained but adapted to an imprecation of those involved in Zion’s destruction, functioning as a sort of taunt song (H. Lenowitz, “Mock Śimḥâ,” 153–56). The mingling of elements of the lament and of the song of Zion reflects the nearest the psalmist can get to the latter in straitened circumstances. Schottroff’s form-critical description and Kellermann’s amplification provide an impressive insight into the psalm. Reversal of a further characteristic element of a song of Zion may be seen in vv 1–3. A narrative describing with perfect verbs Yahweh’s victory over foreign enemies at Jerusalem (Pss 48:5–7 [4–6]; 76:4–10 [3–9]; cf. 46:7, 9–10 [6, 8–9]), which typically uses the topographical adverb “there” (, Ps 48:7 [6];, Ps 76:4 [3]), is here transformed into a bitter report involving the loss of Jerusalem. A questionable part of Kellermann’s analysis is his treatment of the self-cursing formulas of vv 5–6 as a motif of innocence, comparing Job 31:22; Ps 7:6 (5) at the end of a confession of innocence (ZAW 90 [1978] 50): the psalmist affirms his innocence in failing to sing a standard song of Zion despite return from exile. The past verbs in the conditional clauses of Job 31:5–40; Ps 7:4–5 (3–4), over against the imperfect (present and future) verbs here, warn against drawing so close a parallel. As Kraus ([1989] 503) has seen, vv 5–6 represent a double vow. In this individual part of the psalm their tone, though not their precise form, invites comparison with the vow of praise characteristic of the individual lament (cf. Westermann, Praise, 75–78). Since their intent is praise of Zion, even more relevant is the expression of individual devotion in the less formal type of the songs of Zion, Pss 84:3 (2); 122:9 (cf. Ps 43:4). The self-cursing form appears to have been used for this purpose.

Determination of the psalm’s setting depends first on the correct understanding of vv 1–3. If they describe a contemporary experience (cf. Note 1.c.), the whole psalm is an exilic composition. Michel (Tempora, 83) argued from vv 5–9 that the exile was not yet over (cf. A. Lauha, Geschichtsmotive, 123). However, Kellermann (ZAW 90 [1978] 49) demonstrated the logical differences between vv 1–4 and their sequel: while in vv 3–4 the psalmist refuses to sing a song of Zion, in v 6 he sets under a curse any failure to sing and play in Jerusalem’s honor. Most scholars understand from the perfect verbs and repeated adverb, “there,” the psalmist’s distance in time and space from exilic conditions. Ogden (JSOT 24 [1982] 91) has proposed that MT, “devastated,” refers to Babylon as already destroyed, having fallen into the hands of Cyrus. However, unless with him one relates vv 8–9 to Edom, one cannot do justice to the desire for revenge. In fact Babylon was occupied by the Persians, rather than destroyed; a conviction that Babylon, like Assyria, lived on in the form of the Persian empire is here presupposed (cf. Ezra 5:13; 6:22; Neh 13:6; see also Ezra 9:7; Neh 9:32, 36–37). But what of the setting that is reported in vv 1–4? Kraus ([1989] 502) found reference to a formal service of lamenting prayer. On the other hand, Kellermann (ZAW 90 [1978] 53–57) presented a good case for understanding the verses in terms of a noncultic mourning lament, akin to the funeral lament. Mention of sitting and weeping (cf. Neh. 1:4; Isa 47:1; Ezek 26:16; Job 2:8; a rendering “dwell” for[Bar-Ephrat, “Love of Zion,” 5] is unlikely) and the neglect of the joyful lyre (cf. Gen 31:27; Job 30:31) are significant pointers in this direction.

Vv 1–4 lie in the past for the psalmist: in vv 5–6aγ he can address Jerusalem directly, as present there. The psalm reveals the sufferings and sentiments of people who perhaps experienced at first hand the grievous days of the conquest and destruction of Jerusalem in the year 587 B.C.E., people who shared the burden of the Babylonian captivity and after their return to their homeland now, at the sight of the city still lying in ruins, give vent with passionate intensity to the feelings lying dormant in their hearts (Weiser, 794).

The early years of return from exile, before the rebuilding of either the temple (and so 537–515 B.C.E.) or the city walls (and so 537–445 B.C.E.), were evidently the period in which the psalm was composed. It is not strictly necessary to presuppose that the psalmist was ever in Babylon (cf. Rogerson and McKay, 150), but the passionate pathos of vv 1–4 suggests that he spoke from experience. It is sometimes suggested that he was a Levitical singer and musician, perhaps one of the Levites who returned to Judah according to Ezra 2:41. Despite Westermann (Praise, 256), the psalm probably had a cultic setting, in view of its content (cf. Kellermann, ZAW 90 [1978] 54–55). In later Jewish tradition the psalm was employed on 9 Ab at the service commemorating the destruction of Jerusalem. It is possible that it was originally used at such a cultic setting (cf. Zech 7:3; 8:19). Ogden (JSOT 31 [1985] 93) suggested that it was used in a postexilic liturgy together with Isa 63:1–6, Ps 60, and Obadiah.

The psalm is written in a remarkably prosaic way. Renfroe (“Persiflage,” 527) characterizes it as consisting “largely of prose fitted into some sort of poetic form,” while Seybold (509) calls it “stylized prose.” More precisely, R. Alter (Art of Biblical Poetry, 19) mentions its virtual avoidance of semantic features in its parallelism. Discussion of the strophical structure of the psalm has by no means been marked by unanimity. Kissane (285), followed by Schildenberger (EstEcl 34 [1960] 675), Mowinckel (Real and Apparent Tricola, 102), and van der Lugt (Strofische structuren, 437–40), advocated a regular division into three strophes of four lines, vv 1–3, 4–6, 7–9. Magne (Bib 39 [1958] 194–95) and Dahood (269) preferred a longer initial strophe, vv 1–4, followed by three shorter ones vv 5–6, 7, 8–9. This grouping corresponds closely to the changes in aspect and number in the development of the psalm. Moreover, from the table of repeated terms plotted by Magne, it is clear that the repetition of the object signin vv 8–9, corresponding to that within vv 1–4 at vv 2, 4, afforded him justification for this division. Magne drew attention to the centrality and so importance of vv 5–6 in the poem. Dahood noted the change of address in the three latter strophes, to Jerusalem, to the Edomites (on his understanding), and to Babylon.

Freedman (“Structure of Psalm 137,” 187–205) might have been inspired by Magne’s explanation to develop his own structural analysis in terms of a concentric pattern, which was followed by Auffret (ZAW 92 [1980] 346–47, 369; id., BZ 41 [1997] 250–52) and also, slightly differently, by J. Magonet (HeyJ 23 [1982] 373–76). On the outside lie parallel units, vv 1–2, 8–9. The repeated preposition, “upon,” followed by noun and bet, “in” (see Note 1.b.), in vv 1–2, are mirrored in the repeated, “how fortunate . . . who,” in vv 8–9. Both vv 1 and 8 are tricola and mention, “Babylon.” Near the edge of the psalm vv 3, 7 match in featuring quotation of enemies with plural imperatives. Deeper into the psalm lie the two lines vv 4, 6aγb, each made up of conjunction and verb, object sign and object, and, “upon, above,” in a genitival phrase. At the heart of the composition cluster vv 5–6aβ, with their precise chiastic order.

In his analysis Freedman was able to tie together certain phenomena of the psalm impressively. Two questions that need to be asked are whether he did justice to sufficient phenomena and whether he correctly interpreted those used in building up his own suggested structure. The framing, “Babylon,” in vv 1, 8 need not have anything to do with divisions within the psalm. There are some seemingly key terms in the psalm that do not feature in the analysis, viz., “remember,” in vv 1, 6aγ, 7 and/, “Zion/Jerusalem,” in vv 1, 3, 5, 6aα, 7. The former term may point to a tripartite division, vv 1–3 (or 4), 4 (or 5)–6, 7–9, and the latter pair can support these demarcations. Freedman was right in highlighting the similarities between vv 4 and 6aγ–b. However, their parallelism may exist for a quite different reason from concentricity. It is noteworthy that v 4 in a “we” sequence appears to cap the narrative of vv 1–3 with a differently structured sentence. Something similar occurs in the course of v 6: after the tightly bound ABB′A′ patterning of vv 5–6aβ, there is an extra line that breaks the pattern and changes to a third-person reference to Jerusalem while maintaining the first-person singular subject and developing further the conditional clause of v 6a. The parallel structuring of vv 4, 6aγ, by which Freedman laid such store, appears to be continued in v 9. Its combination of conjunction and verb, object sign and object, and prepositional phrase invites comparison with the earlier matching lines. It is true that the genitival phrase at the end of vv 4, 6 is not reproduced; nor is that at the center of v 4 in the other two cases, though the suffixed form in v 9 affords partial correspondence.

There are thus good grounds for reckoning with three strophes, vv 1–4, 5–6, 7–9, consisting of five, three, and four lines, respectively. This scheme has often been advocated (see the listing in van der Lugt, Strofische structuren, 439). The third strophe subdivides into two subunits, the two lines of v 7 and the two of vv 8–9. They are united by their imprecatory tones and concerned with Edom and Babylon, respectively, for which the related terms, “sons (of Edom),” and, “daughter,” are used, and both give grounds for lament using article and appositional term (see Note 8.a.). Vv 8–9 are a pair of commendation formulas. The repeated initial and (nearly) final Zion/Jerusalem references of vv 1, 3 and 5, 6 are in this strophe replaced by a spiritually logical wordplay,, “Jerusalem,” and, “will repay,” in vv 7, 8. The second strophe, vv 5–6, is dominated by an isolating chiasm (Trublet and Aletti, Approche poétique, 101). The first strophe shows signs of matching the second in that there are also signs of chiasm in the first strophe:/, “we hung”/“our mockers,” at the center are bordered by, “Zion,” and enveloped by the parallelism of, “beside the rivers of Babylon,” and, “on foreign soil.”

However, the first and third strophes also correspond. The first, like the third, falls into two subunits, the two lines of vv 1–2 and the explanatory three lines of vv 3–4, which are linked closely by the fivefold occurrence of, “song(s), sing.” Bar-Ephrat (“Love of Zion,” 4 n. 7) observed that vv 3 and 4 have in common their vocabulary, grammatical form (first-person plural), and content, while v 4 differs from vv 5–6 considerably, both in form and content. The parallelism of the subunits is expressed by the repetition of, “there,” in vv 1, 3 and by the way that, “foreign,” at the end of v 4 echoes in closure the sound of, “our lyres,” at the end of v 2. The subunits are also linked by the interweaving of the wordplays of vv 1–3a (see Note 3.c.) and the repeated terms for singing in vv 3–4. In the first subunit the first two cola of v 1 are matched by the similarly structured second line. Moreover, prepositional terms with third feminine suffixes,, “within it,” and, “in it,” occur in the second lines of the first and final strophes; the repeated, “to us,” occurs in their penultimate lines. The quotations of the first and third strophes, in vv 3 and 7, echo the intrusion of the first-person–singular speech throughout the central strophe, while the divine name at the end of the first strophe and the beginning of the third (vv 4, 7) frames the chiasm of the second.

One must not overlook the two features of thematic alignment all three strophes have in common. First, remembering dominates all three, whether communal (v 1), individual (vv 5–6), or divine (v 7). Second, the direct note of joy in the first two, refused in the first and embraced in the second, is indirectly taken up in the commendation formulas of the third. These resolve the antithesis of the other two strophes and affirm the second by expressing passionate desire for the punishment of those who made their initial joy impossible. Furthermore, Bar-Ephrat (“Love of Zion,” 10) has observed the bipolar opposition of Babylon and Zion in vv 1–4, which is separated into the pole of Jerusalem in vv 5–6 and the pole of Babylon in vv 7–9.

Comment
Joyless exilic memories of Zion (137:1–4). Ps 137 is a modified version of a song of Zion. The faith community of Judah in the immediate postexilic period mingles its devotion to the holy city with tones of anguish. The typical narrative passage celebrating the impregnability of Zion and its divine protection has to give way to a report of mourning in a foreign land. The normal trilogy of Yahweh, Zion, and defeated foe is replaced by a tragic threesome of the victorious enemy, Zion, and the defeated nation, with Yahweh very conspicuous by absence except as a figure of past tradition (v 4). The misery of vv 1–3 is accentuated in the Hebrew by ninefold repetition of the ending -nû (“we,” “our”), which carries a ring of pathos, as in Isa 53:4–6,

The grief of the exiles was much more than homesickness. As they sat in a passive attitude of mourning beside a tree-lined canal, they were haunted by memories of Zion—bittersweet memories of festivals and fellowship with God and with believer, and tortured memories of the ruins to which God’s earthly home and Judah’s capital had been reduced. So their lyres, used for entertainment, rested up in the branches, idle, silent. Their outburst of grief had been provoked by their enemies tauntingly, in order to humiliate them, bidding them sing one of the old songs of Zion, which were “songs of Yahweh” (, v 4), temple songs in which Yahweh was praised (cf. 2 Chr 7:6; 29:27). That was neither the time nor the place, however. Age-old traditions of worship had been traumatically overthrown and their content seemingly nullified. By the location of the psalm immediately after two psalms that celebrate Yahweh’s gift of the land to the covenant nation, the reversal here expressed is conveyed in even more poignant tones than when the psalm is read in isolation.

Apart from the rivers Euphrates and Tigris and their tributaries, there was also an intricate canal system intersecting the southern Babylonian plain. It was constructed to provide artificial irrigation for grain fields and date orchards, and also transportation of these crops and other goods in the case of larger watercourses. One of the canals was the Kebar canal (Ezek 1:1, 3; 3:15) in the vicinity of Nippur. Various groups of exiled nationals, including Judeans, were settled in a neo-Babylonian program to rehabilitate the area after its devastation from the wars with Assyria in the seventh century B.C.E. Seybold (510) envisioned a settlement of Levitical singers, for which one may compare the settlement for Levites and temple servants at Casiphia according to Ezra 8:15–20. In v 2 the populus euphratica or Euphrates poplar is in view. It looks more like a willow than a true poplar according to J. V. K. Wilson, JSS 7 [1962] 175–76, who equated it with Akkadian ṣarbatu, as well as Arabic ʿarabun. More specifically M. Zohary (Plants of the Bible [Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1982] 130) has observed that the leaves of younger shoots and branches are similar to those of the willow. The, “lyre,” was a type of stringed instrument with a rounded base and straight or slightly curved yoke-arms supporting the strings, as distinct from another type of lyre () that had a jar-shaped sounding box and a curved yoke (see the discussion and illustrations in Keel, Symbolism, 346–49). V 4 implies that cultic sanctity and worship were confined to the land of Israel (cf. Y. Kaufmann, Religion of Israel, 126–31; M. Haran, Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel [Oxford: Clarendon, 1978] 41 n. 47). The verse expresses well the sense of the exiles’ being “alienated from the place which gave identity and security, . . . from all the shapes and forms which gave power to faith and life” (W. Brueggemann, Land, 8).

Joyful commitment to Jerusalem (137:5–6). Thankfully that scene of absolute misery belongs to the past. However, the wound, though closed over by return, still aches with pain. The ruined city bears sad witness to the mystery of fact in conflict with faith. But faith manages to triumph. The psalmist doffs his role as voice of the community and speaks for himself, in expression of his heightened emotion and as mentor to his fellow worshipers. He pledges his personal commitment to Jerusalem and echoes the note of joy associated with the solo songs of Zion, Pss 84 and 122. His devotion takes the form of a solemn vow invoking upon himself the penalty of physical handicap (cf. Matt 5:28–30). May he never be able to play the lyre again nor sing again, should he forget! Just as in the songs of Zion, praise of Zion represents praise of God, so here his expression of loyalty to Jerusalem is a measure of loyalty to Yahweh since the city, the very “city of God” (, Ps 46:5 [4]), symbolizes divine presence.

Passionate appeals on Jerusalem’s behalf (137:7–9). However, the joy has sought him through pain, and it is pain that drives him to articulate the closing portion of the psalm, in which the participants of vv 1–4 largely reappear, but now with new roles as if rearranged in a shaken kaleidoscope. The implicit contrast between the sure foundation of the city of God celebrated in Zion texts (Ps 87:1; Isa 14:32; 28:16) and its present ruined state prompts a prayer for punishment. Yahweh will surely not stay in the background as hero of a dead tradition but must come to the fore. God’s declared will in establishing Jerusalem as the legitimate channel of divine access encourages the psalmist to intercede on its behalf. The human memories of vv 1, 5 must surely have a divine counterpart—not leaving such an outrage in the limbo of unpunished sin but remembering and acting in retaliation on the Edomite collaborators (cf. Obad 11–14). The movement from vv 5–6 to v 7 has a persuasive, even bargaining intention: if the psalmist is determined to remember, won’t Yahweh (Hartberger, “An den Wassern,” 223)? An even greater measure of guilt attaches itself to the Babylonian invaders. Borrowing the commendation formula from a song of Zion, the psalmist uses it ironically to commend dire reprisal from human agents of God. In the light of v 8 Judah had evidently itself suffered the fate of v 9, and it is for this cultural expression of total warfare that demand is made. For the sake of divine justice their turn must come.

For, “remember to someone’s disadvantage,” in v 7, one may compare the sense “remember to someone’s advantage” in Ps 132:1. H.-J. Boecker (Redeformen, 110) found a basic forensic connotation whereby Yahweh is invoked as judge in an accusation (cf. Childs, Memory and Tradition, 32). However, Schottroff (‘Gedenken,’ 233), comparing Jer 18:19–21, interpreted less specifically of Yahweh’s intervention in punishment. At first sight mention of Edom in v 7 looks intrusive; but, as Freedman (“Structure of Psalm 137,” 201) showed, it is of a piece with other sixth-century B.C.E. literature. For the use of, “day,” with reference to a time of destitution, one may compare Ps 37:13, Job 18:20, and especially Obad 12–14. The personification of cities and countries with, “daughter,” in v 8 is a phenomenon found especially in the prophetic literature (see A. Fitzgerald, CBQ 37 [1975] 167–83). Rather anomalously Ogden (JSOT 24 [1982] 91) took it as referring to Edom, as the ally or confederate of Babylon, claiming that, “rocks,” is an implicit play on the rocky heights of Edom. Earlier Kellermann (ZAW 90 [1978] 48) made a similar point, relating the term to the Edomite city Sela, but adopted P. Joüon’s emendation, “daughter/lady Edom.” For the commendation formula used twice in vv 8–9, see the Comment on Ps 106:3. Here it has a rhetorical force in the context of an imprecation (E. Lipiński, RB 75 [1968] 354). The barbarous practice referred to in v 9 was a feature of ancient Near Eastern warfare. One may compare the statement of Nah 3:10 and the oracles of Isa 13:16 (against Babylon) and Hos 14:1 (13:16). It effected total destruction by making war on the next generation. Although not uncommon, it aroused shock and horror (cf. 2 Kgs 8:12), and so the psalm here reaches an emotional climax. The note of retaliation obviously carries over from v 8: “The imprecation holds up a mirror to the Babylonian atrocities against Jerusalem and flashes the scene back onto the perpetrators as their coming recompense” (Eaton, 299). B. Steidle (Erbe und Auftrag 50 [1974] 24) observed that the negative expression of an eminent positive love for Zion is displayed here: Edom-Babylon is the implacable antithesis to Zion and so to Yahweh.

Explanation
For the difficulties raised for the Christian reader by the attitude of vv 8–9, see the discussion of Ps 109. E. Zenger (A God of Vengeance? 48) has described the psalm as

an attempt to cling to one’s historical identity even when everything is against it. Still more, it is an attempt, in the face of the most profound humiliation and helplessness, to suppress the primitive human lust for violence in one’s own heart by surrendering everything to God—a God whose word of judgment is presumed to be so universally just that even those who pray the psalm submit themselves to it.

The citizen of a European country who has experienced its invasion and destruction or a victim of the Holocaust would be the best expositor of such a psalm. The passion that throbs in every line is the fruit of powerless suffering. Is it ignoble? Not if one takes seriously the spiritual framework of the psalmist. It is difficult to detach from the concepts of a chosen nation, a territory possessed by divine right, and a holy city such corollaries as holy war and a divine crusade against the violators of such concepts. The issues of national liability and forfeiture of the covenant gifts, which are stressed in prophetic and deuteronomistic literature, do present a different perspective on Judah’s experience from the one espoused by the psalmist, which was evidently of cultic provenance. However, Isaiah could hold together in tension the paradox of Assyria as agent of the divine will and yet culpable transgressor (Isa 10:5–15). Moreover, the psalm is in line with exilic and postexilic prophecies that promise the aggressor the heaviest of reprisals (cf. Isa 13:16; 14:22). Jeremiah’s acceptance of Babylonian sovereignty (Jer 29:4–7) could never be God’s final word to the OT believer (cf. Jer 50–51).

The Christian faith teaches a new way, the pursuit of forgiveness and a call to love. Both its intrinsic non-nationalism and its ability to fall back on an eschatological final reckoning, the last judgment, facilitate such a course. Yet Brueggemann (77) pertinently asks: “Can there be a claim that overrides forgiveness?” Is there forgiveness for a Judas (cf. John 17:12) or a Hitler? Ps 137:9 forms part of the OT backdrop to the new Babylon of Rev 17–18, and there too fierce joy is inculcated on saint and angel (Rev 18:20), in a context of persecution and in response to the prayer for vengeance in Rev 6:10, while Rev 18:6 picks up the language of Ps 137:8. So there is a tension within the NT and not simply between the testaments. The psalmist’s passion is not simply an expression of nationalism, even when it is appreciated that in the OT, nationalism is itself a kind of spiritual virtue. It is a measure of his sense of an ultimate sin committed against God. The meaning of Jerusalem to him, its sacramental role in God’s revealed purposes as reflection of the divine, could permit the providentional outworking of no lesser retribution. It is his love for God that makes him curse: his God is not mocked.

Such texts as these in Old and New Testaments are given to readers in the throes of disorientation, not to those basking in the seasons of orientation or reorientation. P. D. James in her mystery Original Sin ([New York: Knopf, 1995] 232) has a character say to her Jewish colleague:

“If I had a God, I’d like him to be intelligent, cheerful and amusing.”

He said, “I doubt whether you’d find him much of a comfort when they herded you into the gas chambers. You might prefer a God of vengeance.”
