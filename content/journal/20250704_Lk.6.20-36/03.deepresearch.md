## 2. Exegetical Interpretation (Enhanced)

•   **Theological Word Study:** Instead of just listing key words, select 2-3 theologically critical terms in the original language (Hebrew/Greek). Explain their semantic range (i.e., their various possible meanings) and argue why the author chose this specific word here. What emotional or theological weight does it carry that a simple translation might miss? (e.g., the difference between *agape* and *philia*; the force of *dikaiosynē* as "righteousness/justice").

•   **Grammar that Preaches:** Analyze key grammatical features—such as verb tenses, moods, participles, or sentence structure (syntax)—and explain their theological and pastoral implications. For example, does a Greek aorist tense point to a once-for-all, completed action of God? Does a Hebrew piel verb stem indicate an intense action? How does the flow of the clauses build an argument or create emotional impact?

•   **Literary & Rhetorical Analysis:** Identify the literary structure of the passage (e.g., chiasm, parallelism, narrative arc). Point out the author's use of rhetorical devices like metaphor, simile, irony, or hyperbole. Explain *how* these literary choices serve the passage's main purpose and would have impacted the original audience. What picture is being painted? What feeling is being evoked?

•   **Socio-Historical Immersion:** Go beyond a simple backdrop. Describe the cultural logic, common assumptions, and social pressures of the original audience. How would this text have *challenged* or *affirmed* their lived experience and worldview (e.g., honor-shame dynamics, patron-client relationships, purity laws, Roman imperial ideology)? Cite respected, non-Japanese academic commentaries (e.g., N.T. Wright, Gordon Fee, D.A. Carson, Craig Keener).

•   **Intertextual Echoes:** Trace significant connections to other parts of Scripture. Are there direct quotations or subtle allusions to the Old Testament? How does this passage engage with or develop major biblical themes (e.g., covenant, kingdom, exile, temple, exodus)? How does it fit into the specific author's (e.g., Paul, Luke, John) overarching theological project in this particular book?
