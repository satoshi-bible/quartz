
C. Believers Should Not Be Required to Submit to Extraordinary Religious Practices (2:16–23)
III. 	Letter Body: Christians Should Strive for Maturity (1:24–4:6)
  	B. 	Believers Should Live Only on the Basis of Christ (2:6–15)
   	➤ C. 	Believers Should Not Be Required to Submit to Extraordinary Religious Practices (2:16–23)
   	D. 	Believers Should Seek Heavenly Things and Not Old Earthly Things (3:1–4)

Here is a fuller statement of the main idea of this unit: Believers should not be required to submit to extraordinary practices of bodily discipline and holiday-keeping in order to experience the tabernacling presence of God, since only faith in Christ is necessary for that.

This is the most debated section of the epistle. At the same time, it is the most important segment, since it gives more description of the false teaching. Is the description of the error quoted from the false teachers virtually verbatim by Paul, does it represent Paul’s own interpretive polemical summary of that teaching, or is it a combination of the two?1 The last option appears likely. But to whatever degree Paul is interpreting the errant teaching, it had to correspond to that teaching, since the readers would have been very aware of the content of this teaching (cf. Sumney 2008: 149). There is also much debate about the contextual background of the errant teaching: is it Jewish, Hellenistic, or a combination of the two?2 My conclusion will be that it is primarily Jewish yet with some Hellenistic pagan elements. Recall that even in the OT, false teaching within the Israelite community entails some imbibing of pagan ideas (especially teaching proposing that pagan idols could be worshiped and integrated into Israel’s religion without involving compromise).

The goal of Col. 1:24–2:5 was that believers be “complete in Christ” (1:28), which is achieved by them not being “deluded” by false teaching, so they can maintain “good discipline and stability of faith” (2:4–5). And this is the basis of “walking in” Christ in 2:6 (and 2:8b–9), which explicitly expands on “walking” worthily in 1:10a. The main point of 2:6–15 is that believers should live (“walk”) only on the basis of faith in Christ and not by any other belief system (2:6–9). The reason for living only on the basis of Christ is that he came in the fullness of deity in the flesh as God’s temple (v. 9). In doing so he thereby redeemed people by his death and resurrection (2:10–14), which accomplished victory over spiritual enemies (v. 15). This emphasis on living only by faith in Christ was set in antithesis to the non-Christ-centered false teaching (2:4, 8).

The “therefore” in 2:16 indicates that it is based on the idea of living only on the basis of Christ, the main point of 2:6–15. Saints cannot be “judged” by keeping extraneous and outdated OT temple laws because we are to live only by faith in Christ. We have already been judged through identification with Christ’s death, who suffered judgment on our behalf (2:14). Furthermore, the cleanliness required for entrance into God’s presence comes through being in Christ and not through keeping OT laws dealing with diet or special holidays. The emphasis on Christ as the only necessary focus for Christian living continues in verse 17. The reason why Christians do not need to keep these OT laws is that they are outmoded in the light of Christ’s coming: these OT laws are a “shadow” of Christ, who has come and has brought these laws to material fulfillment (he is the “substance” to which these shadows pointed). As will be seen, these OT laws concerned ways to be acceptable for worshiping God in the OT temple. Now, however, these laws no longer apply because Christ is the temple and we are acceptable in him for worshiping God. We are clean to enter into temple worship because Christ has circumcised us (see on 2:11).

Verses 18–19a return to another extraneous requirement of bodily discipline for which Christians should not be judged or “disqualified” from entry into God’s tabernacling presence. But, just as verse 17 focused on Christ as the reason for not following certain OT laws in verse 16, so verse 19 returns to that Christ-centered focus as a reason for not submitting to the requirements of verse 18.

On the basis of identification with Christ the head (v. 19), believers have died with him (cf. 2:11–12, 14) to the old world (v. 20a). And because they have died to that old world, they should not submit to outmoded OT commandments that belong to the old age (vv. 20b–21). There are three reasons one should not abide by these laws: (1) all of them are part of the old corrupt world, which will pass away (v. 22a); (2) these laws have their origin in human tradition (v. 22b); (3) and these laws about self-made religion and bodily discipline express only a superficial wisdom but are of no value against sin (v. 23).

The main point of verses 16–23 is that saints should not be required to submit to extraneous laws of bodily discipline in order to experience God’s tabernacling presence. The reason for this is because only faith in Christ brings us into God’s holy presence. In this respect, verses 16–23 are an amplification of the false teaching in 2:8, which is apparent from observing the following parallels (generally following D. Moo 2008: 217): (1) warning not to let the false teachers influence them (cf. 2:8 with 2:16 and 18); (2) the false teaching is based on “human” tradition (cf. 2:8 with 2:22); (3) the false teaching is based on “the elements of the world” (cf. 2:8 with 2:20); the false teaching is “empty deception” (2:8) and “inflated in vain” (2:18); (4) the false tradition is not based on Christ (cf. 2:8 with 2:19).

Exegesis and Exposition

16Therefore [let] no one . . . act as your judge in regard to food or drink or in respect to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath day—17things which are a mere shadow of what is to come; but the substance belongs to Christ. 18Let no one keep defrauding you of your prize by delighting in self-abasement and the worship of the angels, . . . [which] he has ⌜  ⌝ seen [upon entering into the temple], inflated without cause by his fleshly mind, 19and not holding fast to the head, from whom the entire body, being supplied and held together by the joints and ligaments, grows with a growth which is from God. 20If ⌜  ⌝ you have died with Christ to the [elements] of the world, why, as if you were living in the world, do you submit yourself to decrees, such as, 21“Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch!” 22(which all refer to things destined to perish with use)—in accordance with the commandments and teachings of men? 23These are matters which have, to be sure, the appearance of wisdom in self-made religion and self-abasement ⌜and⌝ severe treatment of the body, but are of no value [with respect to the full satisfaction of the flesh].3
[2:16]

The “therefore” (οὖν, oun) at the beginning of the verse refers back to the main point of 2:6–15 as the ground for the rest of the verse: Christian living should be centered only on Christ. Such living focused squarely on Christ is the basis for Paul saying, “Let no one judge you” (μὴ . . . τις ὑμᾶς κρινέτω, mē . . . tis hymas krinetō) by OT laws concerning diet and special holidays.* The believing readers have already been judged for their sin in Christ (2:11–12, 14), so that the judgment about diet and holidays is nothing compared with the decisive judgment in Christ on their behalf. This redemptive judgment has reconciled them to God (1:20) and given them inaugurated entrance to the very presence of God in Christ (on this see 1:19 and 2:9–10, where Christ as God’s tabernacling “fullness” is shared by believers).

They need nothing else besides Christ and his forgiving work to help them be closer to God. They do not need to obey the extraneous laws cited in the remainder of verse 16 to draw them closer to God. Also, no one should “judge” them as being incomplete in their spiritual fullness if they do not obey these laws, since their final eschatological “completion” in Christ (1:28; cf. 3:3–4) has already begun through their “having been filled in him”* (2:10). As we concluded in 2:9–10, this “filling” pertains to them being identified with Christ as the end-time temple and being filled with his glorious presence in that temple. But, as we will see especially in 2:18, the false teachers are affirming that obedience to OT dietary laws and ordinances about holidays are necessary requirements in order to come into the presence of God in his heavenly temple. “Let no one judge you” is a further development of “Be careful not to let anyone captivate you”* in 2:8, which emphasizes the warning not to let the false “philosophy” dominate anyone (D. Moo 2008: 218).

Most commentators have mentioned only that these are dietary laws and decrees about holidays, the ongoing validity of which the false teachers are wrongly affirming. While this is true, the vast majority of Colossians commentators have not noticed that the primary purpose of these laws in the OT is to enable the Israelites to become clean and be able to enter into God’s temple to worship him.

For example, the phrase “in food and in drink” (ἐν βρώσει καὶ ἐν πόσει, en brōsei kai en posei) refers to a part of the OT laws concerning how people can become clean or unclean. The commands “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch!” in verse 21 refer to the same thing. There are various purposes for the clean and unclean OT laws. For example, it is possible that they are for hygiene, to promote ethical behavior (such as forbidding indiscriminate killing of animals for recreation), and to distinguish life and wholeness associated with God from death and disorder. But it is much clearer that one of the purposes is to make a separation from things contaminated by pagan idolatry (including immoral sexual activity associated with pagan worship) and especially to show a separation between unclean Gentiles and clean Israelites (which includes separation from idolatry). The latter is highlighted in Lev. 20:25–26:

You are therefore to make a distinction between the clean animal and the unclean, and between the unclean bird and the clean; and you shall not make yourselves detestable by animal or by bird or by anything that creeps on the ground, which I have separated for you as unclean. Thus you are to be holy to Me, for I the LORD am holy; and I have set you apart from the peoples to be Mine.

Of course, the law in general separates Israel from its Gentile neighbors. The abolition of the food laws in the NT symbolizes that the barrier between Gentiles and Jews has been broken down (above all, see Acts 10–11, esp. 10:15 and 11:9, as well as Mark 7:19 and Rom. 14:14). We have already seen that this function of the law has been abolished, so that believing Jews and Gentiles can become one in Christ, which is part of the “mystery” in Col. 1:26–27 (see my comments on these verses). And we will see this point to be relevant again (see on 2:20 and 3:11). In the OT, observing these laws is evidence of being part of God’s true people in contrast to unbelieving nations. The misguided teachers, however, continue to affirm the validity of these laws, despite the fact that the new age has been inaugurated with the coming of Christ (see N. Wright 1986: 119).

But, above all, the purpose of the clean and unclean laws is to make a person clean for worshiping the holy God in the temple (tabernacle) and to preserve the temple as a clean place. God is holy, and so his people are commanded to be holy. This is especially true with respect to God’s holiness in the temple, and the Israelites are to be holy in drawing near to him in worship (cf. Lev. 20:25–26; Lev. 10, on the death of Nadab and Abihu; Lev. 11–15, on laws of purity). The unclean are to be separated from the tabernacle (Num. 5:3; Lev. 15:31) in order to protect the holiness of the tabernacle (Lev. 12:4; 15:31; Num. 19:13; Neh. 13:7–9, 29–30).4

Thus the main purpose of the unclean and clean laws is to preserve the sanctity of the temple by excluding any uncleanness from entering into it.5 This means likewise that one must abide by these laws in order to be clean to worship God in the sanctity of the temple (in the courtyard for the ordinary Israelite, in the holy place for the priests, and in the holy of holies for the high priest).6 Significantly, the purpose of the food laws showing a separation between unclean Gentiles and clean Israelites is also ultimately related to Israel being holy and clean from the uncleanness of the nations in order to be able to worship God at his sanctuary and to keep that sanctuary clean and holy (so Morales 2015: 162–64). Thus, to be clean is to allow entry into varying degrees of the life-giving presence of God in the sanctuary and the order of that sanctuary, but to be unclean is in varying degrees to be separated from the life of God and to be associated with death and disorder (so Morales 2015: 157–59, 166).

The false teaching identified in Col. 2:16 entails, therefore, not merely a wrong assertion that these dietary laws continue to be valid but also that one must abide by them to be clean to worship God in his end-time heavenly temple (so also Dunn 1996: 191–92, commenting on Col. 2:21; for the notion of a heavenly temple, see below on 2:18). Verse 21 has underscored the importance of these dietary laws by repeating them.

When the actual phrase “food and drink” is studied elsewhere in the NT, it typically refers to part of a preparation for worshiping God in the temple. Hebrews 9:8–14 says, “The [ultimate] way into the holy place has not yet been disclosed” during OT times (9:8). However, “gifts and sacrifices” pertaining to “food and drink [βρώμασιν καὶ πόμασιν, brōmasin kai pomasin]7 and various washings [and] regulations for the body” were required in the OT era (9:9–10) in order for one to be “sanctified” and “cleansed” to approach the temple (9:10, 13). Nevertheless, such things “cannot make the worshiper perfect in conscience” (9:9). Only “the blood of Christ” (9:14), the “high priest” (9:11), who “entered through the greater and more perfect tabernacle” (cf. Heb. 8:1–2), can “sanctify” defiled people “for the cleansing of your conscience”* (9:14). “Food and drink” (βρώμασιν καὶ πόμασιν, 9:10) is part of what formerly cleansed one to qualify for temple worship. These old food and drink laws appear to be the very thing that the false teachers in Colossae are still urging Christians to obey to be prepared for heavenly temple worship.8 But, as Hebrews says, and Paul will soon say in verse 17, such laws are now outmoded for worship of God in his temple, which has come about in Christ’s advent.

In a context of instructions for priests serving in the temple, Philo refers to the appetite as “a profane, and impure, and unholy thing,” but temperance is “a pure and unblemished virtue, neglecting everything which relates to eating and drinking [βρῶσιν καὶ πόσιν, brōsin kai posin],” being “superior to the pleasures of the belly, [it] may be allowed to approach the sacred altars” in the temple (Spec. 1.150, trans. Yonge 1993: 548). Philo uses the very same phrase “food and drink” as in Col. 2:16 to refer to what is to be avoided in disciplining oneself to be able to be clean in order to approach the temple altars. This is Philo’s only use of this phrase, which occurs in the context of the temple. Philo also says, with very similar language, that Moses “purified” himself from “meat and drink” (σιτίων καὶ ποτῶν, sitiōn kai potōn) and “neglecting meat and drink” (σιτίων . . . καὶ ποτῶν) in order to be qualified for his “fulfilling of the office of the priesthood” (Mos. 2.66–68) and for approaching God at the top of the “most sacred mountain” (Mos. 2.70), which was considered a sanctuary (see the additional notes on 2:18) and where he was instructed about “the building of the temple” (Mos. 2.71).

In a context of discussing the Sabbath, fasting, full moons, and feasts (Mos. 2.21–24), Philo also says, “It is not lawful to take any food or any drink [οὐ σιτίον, οὐ ποτόν, ou sition, ou poton]” in order that people may think clearly in “propitiating the Father of the universe with holy prayers, by which they are accustomed to solicit pardon for their former sins” (2.24). This may also pertain to a temple context.

Josephus refers to “how the purity of it [the temple] was never to be profaned” (Ag. Ap. 2.102). For example, “all the Jews went into the second court, as well as their wives, when they were free from all uncleanness; into the third [court] went the Jewish men when they were clean and purified” (Ag. Ap. 2.104). There were also to be no “vessels” that “related to [unclean] food or drink being carried into the temple,”9 so that the purity of the temple would be maintained (Ag. Ap. 2.106–8).10

This combined use of βρῶσις καὶ πόσις with a view to temple purity is found only in the NT, OT, and Judaism. The same combination frequently occurs in classical and Hellenistic sources but only with mundane reference to “food and drink” being the typical means of bodily nourishment. As far as I can tell in the sources I have checked, except for one instance, there is no cultic use of this specific phrase outside of biblical and Jewish sources.11 This further confirms the OT and Jewish nature of this precise expression. The Colossian false teaching might have referred to fasting from all food and drink or from only some. That “food and drink” in 2:16 refers to Jewish dietary laws for cultic cleanness is apparent from the observation that the roughly synonymous wording of “do not handle, do not taste, do not touch” occurs in 2:21 and serves to highlight 2:16 as referring to such dietary laws. Furthermore, that Jewish dietary laws are in mind is confirmed by the immediately following phrase “a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath day” in verse 16b, which clearly refers to Jewish holidays (on which see below). Thus it is highly improbable that “food and drink” in verse 16a refers to common, everyday meals or to Hellenistic cultic purification practices.

The false teaching associated with the topic of the temple continues in the second part of verse 16. The verse states what believers are further not to be judged by: “in respect to a feast12 or a new moon or Sabbath days.”* These three words (ἑορτή, νεομηνία, σάββατον, heortē, neomēnia, sabbaton) occur as a triadic formula in the LXX and early Judaism, almost always inextricably linked to temple sacrifices or temple worship;13 or when the temple is not explicitly mentioned, it is likely assumed to be in mind to some degree, since these feasts were held at the temple.14 While “feasts,” “new moons,” and “sabbaths” were observed by some Gentiles,15 this triadic formula is not found elsewhere in pagan Greek contexts but only within OT and Jewish contexts.16 Therefore, the triadic formula in the LXX is the framework for understanding the threefold mention of the holidays of Col. 2:16 and shows the clear Jewish context and association with the temple there. Not only were the false teachers demanding obedience to dietary laws for cleanness to prepare for heavenly temple worship; they were also requiring that people observe the traditional Jewish holidays (perhaps through fasting,17 “self-abasement” [vv. 18, 23], “severe treatment of the body” [v. 23], and other regulations) in order to be eligible for such temple worship.

It is beyond coincidence that the dietary and holiday requirements of verse 16 are inextricably linked with worship at the temple.18 The temple is not a new theme emerging here for the first time. As we have seen, it has been popping up at various points throughout the epistle. However, thus far the references have been either to Christ as the temple or to believers identified with him as the temple. This is the first time that a false view of the qualifications for temple worship has appeared in Colossians. Here is but the beginning of an explanation of a false view of the temple, an antitemple detour, in contrast to the true temple, which will reach its climax in verse 18.19 This false teaching about dietary laws and holidays is clearly rooted in a distortion of OT and Jewish ideas, especially related to the temple, which commentators have not previously recognized.20
[2:17]

Now Paul says that the Colossian Christians should not be judged by the OT requirements of verse 16 because they are “a shadow [σκιά, skia] of what was about to come [μελλόντων, mellontōn],”21 and “Christ” is the “substance” of those foreshadowings.* Since the shadows have found their substantial fulfillment in Christ, it is not necessary to obey them anymore. Hebrews 10:1 expresses almost the identical idea: “For the Law, since it has only a shadow [σκιά, skia] of the good things to come [μέλλω, mellō] and not the very form of things, can never, by the same sacrifices which they offer continually year by year, make perfect those who draw near.” In the following verses of Hebrews, Christ as the supreme sacrifice is presented as that to which the “shadow” of the sacrifices pointed. Thus the continual OT sacrifices by human priests are to be abolished, since what they pointed to has come, which is “better” (Heb. 7:19) and “greater” (Heb. 9:11).22

It is possible that “body” refers to Christ’s fleshly body, in light of Col. 1:22 and 2:11, where “body” does refer to this (and 2:9 uses the adverb “bodily” in the same way). Alternatively, some think that the phrase “body of Christ” (σῶμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ, sōma tou Christou) refers to the church, since the church is called Christ’s “body” (σῶμα, sōma) in Col. 1:18, 24 (?); 2:19; and 3:15 (the latter by implication).23 Accordingly, the most straightforward translation of these first two options would be something like “but [and] the body is of Christ.” While these are possible, it is more likely that “body” here refers to the person of Christ as the “substance” of fulfillment.24 The rendering could be “the substance [or body] is Christ” (an appositional genitive) or “the substance [body] belongs to Christ” (NASB, NRSV, ESV, cf. ASV; genitive of possession). The NLT puts it nicely: “Christ himself is that reality” (likewise NIV, NJB, NET). “Substance” is a better counterpoint to “shadows” than is Christ’s flesh or the church as Christ’s body.

The idea is that the things mentioned in Col. 2:16 are typological foreshadowings of Christ, and these foreshadowings have found the substance to which they point in Christ. Since typology entails that the antitype is seen more clearly retrospectively,25 it appears that the literal notion of a “substantive body” or “reality” casting a shadow is in mind here (see BDAG 984, s.v. σῶμα; a “shadow” always follows a “body”; Philo, Decal. 82; Virt. 118, 181). Accordingly, the “substantive body” of the person of Christ in the new eschatological age casts a typological shadow back into the OT, creating the various types.26 So the idea is somewhat different from how most commentators view this; it is true that in the OT the shadows point forward to Christ, but they do so only because Christ has now come, and it is clear that he himself is the “body” casting those shadows (likewise Hendriksen 1964: 124). Since Christ’s coming was included in God’s plan all along (Col. 1:26–27), even before his coming in history, there was a sense in which “what was about to come” cast its shadow backward. The two other renderings of σῶμα (sōma) as Christ’s flesh or the church as Christ’s body could still fit within such a typological framework, but not as nicely as “substantive body.”

In this epistle we have already seen OT types fulfilled in Christ and his people: Israel’s exodus from Egypt pointed to a greater exodus of God’s people led by Christ (1:13–14a); the promised land pointed to a greater inheritance for Christ’s followers (1:12); the Torah, as the epitome of wisdom, pointed to and was fulfilled by Christ as the greater wisdom (2:3); circumcision pointed to Christ’s death and the spiritual circumcision of his followers (2:11, 13); the OT temple pointed to Christ and was fulfilled in him (1:19; 2:9) and his people as part of the temple (1:9–10; 2:10). And we shall see more types in Col. 3. Beetham (2008: 251) says that the dietary regulations and the sacred holidays found their typological “reality . . . in Christ of the new age.” But precisely how do the clean and unclean laws and the prescribed holidays find their typological fulfillment in Christ? More specifically, the function of the dietary laws to make people clean for temple worship finds its reality in Christ’s sacrificial cleansing of people through his death in the new temple (see Rom. 3:25; see also Beale 2011: 486–96) to make them pure for worship in that temple. Christ’s spiritual circumcision of his people has also qualified them to be clean to enter the spiritual temple and worship there (see on Col. 2:13).

But how do the sacred holidays point to Christ? We concluded (above) that the tripartite formula of “festival, new moon, and Sabbaths” is inextricably linked to temple sacrifices or temple worship. The regulations (e.g., fasting, various bodily disciplines) connected with keeping these holidays were typically part of the preparation for temple worship. These holiday regulations are no longer needed, since the old temple has superseded, and Christ is the new temple and the ultimate temple sacrifice, and only by faith—not through OT rituals—are believers in union with him as the temple. As a consequence, the observance of these holy days is not obligatory in the new-covenant age. Some conclude from this that the “Sabbaths” mentioned in Col. 2:16 mean that the Christian Sunday is not a continuation of the OT Sabbath-day observance (e.g., see D. Moo 2008: 223). This is a possible view, though we think not the best. Yet this is a thorny biblical-theological issue that cannot be fully addressed here (see the additional notes).

Thus the dietary laws and holidays pointed to Christ as the temple, in whom people are now pure. Consequently, they are able to worship in him as the temple. Paul’s contrast of the former OT epoch of shadows with the new age in Christ, the substance, shows that believers are not obligated to follow the shadowy laws but only Christ. The OT-NT contrast also further reveals the OT root of the laws mentioned in verse 16.
[2:18]

This verse is one of the most debated texts in Colossians and perhaps in the entire NT. The verb καταβραβεύω (katabrabeuō) is likely a synonym of “judge” (κρίνω, krinō) in verse 16 (indeed, NET translates the verb in verse 18 as “let no one . . . pass judgment on you”; likewise NLT). The word has a judicial sense of “condemn” or “rule against” in its extrabiblical Classical Greek and Hellenistic uses (Beetham 2008: 202–3). The command, as with the negative command of verse 16, further enforces the warning of verse 8 “not to be captivated” by the false teachers (D. Moo 2008: 229). Some versions translate the verb as “Let no one disqualify you” (NRSV, ESV, HCSB), while others have something like “Let no one rob you of your prize” (ASV, likewise KJV). These are two sides of one coin: whoever is disqualified in a race does not get the prize at the end. The NIV 1984 puts both notions together: “Do not let anyone . . . disqualify you for the prize” (likewise NJB). The notion of judging one’s actions (as does an umpire)27 is common to both renderings. The false teachers are contending that if one does not follow their “self-made religious” rules, then one is disqualified from and robbed of the prize of fully experiencing Christ’s presence. They would be declared to be second-class Christians at best. In reality, as Paul will get to later, the Colossians need to be assured that only “the peace of Christ should judge”* or “act as arbiter” (or rule, βραβευέτω, brabeuetō) over them (Col. 3:15).

Accordingly, Col. 2:18 is probably parallel to verse 16 in offering more regulations by which Christians are being judged. And verse 18, like verse 16, is based on the main point of 2:6–15. That is, Christian living focused squarely on Christ (vv. 6–15) is the basis for Paul saying, “Let no one disqualify you . . .” by anything other than the standard of Christ himself.

The false teachers are wrongly disqualifying people by means of at least two criteria, which are not based on a Christ-centered faith: “by taking pleasure28 in self-abasement and worship of angels.”* The word ταπεινοφροσύνη (tapeinophrosynē) can be rendered “self-abasement” (NRSV, NASB), “voluntary humility” (ASV, KJV), “false humility” (NIV, NET), or merely “humility” (ISV). The context of false teaching shows that this is not true, godly humility, so the NIV may express the idea well, though the ESV’s “asceticism” is best (likewise HCSB, “ascetic practices”). The mention of dietary laws in verses 16 and 21 points to such a meaning, as does verse 23, which explains the same word ταπεινοφροσύνη by “severe treatment of the body.” Furthermore, the verbal form of ταπεινόω (tapeinoō) inextricably links “humility” with “fasting.”29 The noun form ταπεινός (tapeinos) is used in the same way (1 Clem. 55.6).

Why would the readers be urged toward such asceticism?30 The next clause answers this question: to prepare for “the worship of angels.” The NJB paraphrases it well: one “who chooses to grovel [ταπεινοφροσύνη] to angels and worship them.” It is beyond coincidence that becoming “humble” in terms of “fasting” in OT and early Christian literature was a preparation toward seeing heavenly visions (Dan. 10:1–12; Herm. 18.6–10 [= Vis. 3.10], which includes “injuring your flesh”; 56.5–7 [= Sim. 5.3]; cf. also 6.1; 9.1–2 [= Vis. 2.2; 3.1]). Also, Jos. Asen. 10–13 portrays Aseneth’s “humiliation” (11.1, ταπείνωσις, tapeinōsis)31 as preparing her for a visitation of an angel, who announces that God accepts her. In addition to Joseph and Aseneth, fasting was likewise preparatory for heavenly temple visions in other Jewish texts.32

There is debate about whether θρησκείᾳ τῶν ἀγγέλων (thrēskeia tōn angelōn) refers to actually “worshiping angels” (an objective genitive)33 or to “worship by the angels” (a subjective genitive).34 According to the latter view, the misguided teachers were not merely taking pleasure in beholding angels worshiping but presumably wanted to worship along with them. The preposition ἐν (en) before ταπεινοφροσύνῃ (tapeinophrosynē) and its absence before θρησκείᾳ suggest a close conceptual link between the asceticism and worship (e.g., see Harris 2010: 107). This could mean either that the asceticism is directed toward a view for preparation to worship angels (according to the objective genitive option) or that this refers to the humility of the angels themselves and worship by angels (the subjective genitive alternative).35 Accordingly, under either view, this placement of the preposition would also suggest that the genitive τῶν ἀγγέλων would modify both θρησκείᾳ and ταπεινοφροσύνῃ (so Lenski 1961: 130; Sumney 2008: 154).

The immediate context must ultimately decide the issue about whether this is an objective or subjective genitive. The context could allow either of these options. Nevertheless, the first option appears to be preferable, since elsewhere typically “the being who is worshiped is given the objective genitive” after θρησκεία (BDAG 459, s.v. θρησκεία; see further in the additional note on 2:18). There is some evidence of actually worshiping angels elsewhere in Asia Minor (BDAG 459, s.v. θρησκεία) and in Judaism (LAB 13.6; 1QSb 4.28, “You will glorify His name and his Holy Ones”; t. Hul. 2.18).36

Therefore, actually “worshiping angels” is likely correct, which expresses an idea of idolatry. The immediate context of 2:18 repeatedly refers to idolatrous ideas, so that it is more probable that “worshiping angels” is in mind in 2:18. For example, “the tradition of men” in 2:8 and “commandments and teachings of men” in 2:22 are allusions to Isa. 29:13 LXX, which is about idolatry (see on 2:22). Furthermore, the “philosophy” of Col. 2:8 is said “not to be based on Christ,”* and similarly the false teaching in verse 19 is said to be “not holding fast to the head [Christ].” This means that the errant philosophy is focused, even if subtly so, on an object of worship other than Christ, which is idolatry. In addition, “self-made religion” in 2:23 appears to be idolatrous, since it does not come from God (Walsh and Keesmaat 2004: 142–44, with further discussion of the idolatry context of Col. 2). In addition, the unique threefold combination of θέλω (thelō), ταπεινοφροσύνη, and θρησκεία in 2:18 shows that the parallel combination of ἐθελοθρησκία (ethelothrēskia) and ταπεινοφροσύνη (tapeinophrosynē) in 2:23 is developing in 2:18. This points further to an objective genitive in the latter text. That is, in 2:23 the “self-made worship” ἐθελοθρησκία is clearly conducted by humans and not angels, so it is probable that the worship (θρησκεία) in 2:18 is conducted by humans with angels as the object of the veneration (so Arnold 1995: e.g., 93–95).37

Even trusted Christian leaders elsewhere were guilty of such idolatry. For example, Rev. 19:10 says that John “fell at his feet to worship” an angel; so also Rev. 22:8. Similarly, Mart. Ascen. Isa. 7.21, in a context where Isaiah ascends into the temple,38 has Isaiah saying, “I fell on my face to worship him, and the angel who led me would not let me” (see likewise Mart. Ascen. Isa. 9.36). In an apparently unguarded moment, Justin Martyr (1 Apol. 6) says that Christians “worship and adore” the Father “and the Son . . . and the host of angels who follow and are made like to Him.”39 Thus it is easy to see how false teachers could worship angels and propagate such a teaching. A major concern of Colossians has been to highlight the superiority of Christ over all spiritual beings (1:16, 20; 2:10, 15). For Paul to minimize the importance of angels at this point would be suitable, if, in fact, the false teachers are worshiping them (D. Moo 2008: 227). Perhaps the errant teachers are so obsessed with speculations about angels (e.g., how they might provide protection) that they are either neglecting Christ or putting Christ and angels on the same level, thus having an angel-centered faith instead of a Christ-centered one (Arnold 1995: 102; cf. also N. Wright 1986: 122). This is idolatry.

But even if a subjective genitive is preferable, which is plausible, the main focus is on practicing asceticism in order to experience a revelatory vision of angels, as the immediately following phrase shows: “whom [angels] he has seen.”

Now we come to the most controverted word in the whole epistle. What does the participle ἐμβατεύων (embateuōn) mean, and how does it relate syntactically to ἃ ἑόρακεν (ha heoraken)? There are at least four main ways to understand the meaning of this word.

    “Taking his stand [or setting foot] on visions he has seen” (NASB; similarly ASV, NRSV; so also Caird 1976: 199). The phrase ἃ ἑόρακεν is the object of ἐμβατεύων, which is translated as “taking his stand.”
    A second meaning is “investigating” or “entering into detail” about visions that have been seen (NIV, ESV, NET) or about the errant “philosophy” (cf. 2:8). This is the least likely option, since its meaning is possibly attested in only one place (2 Macc. 2:30)40 throughout uses in the LXX, Hellenistic Judaism, Classical Greek, and other Hellenistic sources.41
    “Entering into possession of [something].” Some see this as entering into “a visionary realm of heaven” to fully possess salvation (cf. HCSB),42 while others see this as “entering to possess the full inheritance,” the latter of which is supported by uses of ἐμβατεύω in the LXX to refer to the Israelites possessing the inheritance of the land promises (Josh. 19:49, 51).43 Joshua 19:49 also uses the word “inheritance” (κλῆρος, klēros) of Israel’s possession of the land, after their exodus from Egypt. This could be significant, since the Colossians context mentions that believers have an “inheritance” (1:12, κλῆρος; 3:24, κληρονομία, klēronomia), and this is also in connection with allusions to Israel’s exodus (see above on Col. 1:12–14). Beetham (2008: 206–8) contends that this LXX exodus-inheritance background is the best way to understand ἐμβατεύω in Col. 2:18, especially since this notion is enforced by other classical and Hellenistic uses of ἐμβατεύω referring to “entry into possession of an inheritance.” He says this would make sense of 1:12, where Paul stresses that believers have been “qualified to share in the saints’ inheritance in the light” (Pao [2012: 190] follows Beetham). This would counter the false teachers’ insinuation that Christians are unworthy of a heavenly inheritance if they have not experienced and “entered into” the heavenly visions mentioned in 2:18. This is certainly a viable option.
    A fourth meaning is that of “entering into the innermost sanctuary.”44 This is the view Dibelius put forth in 1917 (see 1975: esp. 85–90), though he was anticipated a few years earlier by Ramsay (1914: 287–90). Dibelius’s view about ἐμβατεύω dominated the interpretation of Col. 2:18 during the last half of the twentieth century (Francis 1975b: 166) and has become “the dominant interpretation” (O’Brien 1982: 144).45

All four of these meanings are possible,46 especially the first, third, and fourth. The third is attractive because of its affinity with the inheritance theme in the broader context of Colossians. Some commentators merely acknowledge some of these options without choosing one and arguing for it.47 However, the fourth view is preferable, as we will argue (though we will see that it has some overlap with the third option). More recently Arnold (1995) has argued for this fourth option most persuasively, and some of the following is a summary of his view, with qualifications here and there.

According to the fourth view, there are two main ways to understand how ἐμβατεύω functions syntactically. First, the participle is adverbial, modifying ἑόρακεν (the later view of Dibelius 1953: 34–36; also Francis 1975b: 167; Sappington 1991: 156–58):48 “Let no one disqualify you by taking pleasure in asceticism and worship of angels, whom49 he has seen when he enters [into the heavenly sanctuary].”50 The formal parallelism of verse 18 with verses 17 and 22 supports this view. In verses 17 and 22, the neuter plural accusative relative pronoun ἅ (ha) sums up a list of preceding members, which in verse 16 are feminine and neuter51 (so also in 3:6, likewise referring to preceding feminine and neuter nouns, though introduced with διά, dia). However, the parallel might break down a bit, since the ἅ in 2:18 could refer back only to “angels” and not also to “asceticism.”52 Furthermore, there is no parallel employment of ἐστίν (estin) to introduce a judgment about the items that precede, as occurs in 2:17, 22 and 3:5, though a different verb than ἐστίν appears in 3:5 (as argued by Arnold 1995: 122).

On the other hand, Arnold (1995: 122–23, following another view of Dibelius 1975: 88) contends that the causal adverbial participle ἐμβατεύων (embateuōn) modifies the verb for “Let no one disqualify you” and should be rendered “because he ‘entered the things he has seen.’” This adverbial understanding of the participle forms a nice parallelism with the preceding adverbial participle, θέλων (thelōn), which also modifies “Let no one disqualify you.” In addition to the weaknesses just noted above about the first view, Arnold (1995: 122) says that this first view becomes especially improbable because “asceticism” (or “humility”) must be viewed as the object of the vision together with the angels; and it would make no sense for “asceticism” to be understood as something that is “seen” (D. Moo [2008: 228–29] makes the same objection as Arnold). However, this is not a necessary entailment, since, as noted above, the object of the vision could be narrowly focused only on the directly preceding “angels” and not include “asceticism” (“humility”).

But even if the neuter relative ἅ includes reference both to “self-abasement” (or “humility”) together with “angels,” it is possible that it refers to the “humility” of the angels, as well as “worship of the angels” (the subjective genitive view), both of which were seen in vision (understanding τῶν ἀγγέλων, tōn angelōn, to modify both “self-abasement” and “worship”).53 As noted earlier, the viability of this is pointed to by the observation that ἐν (en) before ταπεινοφροσύνῃ (tapeinophrosynē, self-abasement) is not repeated before θρησκείᾳ (thrēskeia, worship), so that the two should be taken together with τῶν ἀγγέλων, modifying both. Such a double duty of ἐν is common in the syntax of contemporary Hellenistic Greek (Francis 1975b: 180). Francis also argues in favor of this alternative but takes τῶν ἀγγέλων as a subjective genitive and renders the two clauses as “humility and worship by angels.”54

Arnold (1995: 123) proposes the following translation of his view:

“Let no one condemn you

by insisting55 on

ascetic practices and

invoking angels

because he ‘entered the things he has seen’ (i.e., he based his knowledge/authority on visionary experiences he received during the final stage of his mystery initiation).”

He simplifies the meaning of the verse thus: “Let no one condemn you . . . because he has been initiated [and you have not]” (1995: 123). He proposes that Paul quotes ἃ ἑόρακεν ἐμβατεύων (ha heoraken embateuōn) as “a formula from a local mystery cult that summarizes the visionary experience of the one initiated” (1995: 125; so also Ramsay 1914: 286–87; Dibelius 1975: 88). Arnold’s view includes taking “what things he has seen” as the object of “entering” (as does Dibelius 1975: 87). Accordingly, the inner sanctuary would be the main object of entry, but the neuter relative pronoun would take into view the entire experience of the visionary, which here would include seeing angels (Arnold 1995: 123). This is an alternative and very plausible way to understand the neuter relative pronoun.

In the Asia Minor mystery cults, there were two stages of initiation: (1) an initial entrance into an oracular grotto, followed by (2) a climactic entrance into the inner sanctuary, where the presence of the god resided. Three inscriptions were discovered in the Apollo temple at Claros, the earliest dated at AD 132 and the others to sometime later, likely in the mid-second century (Arnold 1995: 110–11). Strikingly, the second, climactic part of the initiation there is designated by ἐμβατεύω (embateuō), which describes the “entering” of the initiate into the innermost sanctuary of the god. In this respect, there are a total of four relevant inscriptions employing ἐμβατεύω.56

    The earliest inscription describes men who came to the oracle for consultation and concludes by saying, “These men were initiated and then they entered [ἐμβατεύσαν, embateusan].”
    Another inscription at Claros speaks of a man named Androcos, who “received the mysteries [the first stage of the initiation] and then he entered [ἐνεβάτευσεν, enebateusen].”
    The third inscription describes two men, who “after they were initiated and they entered [ἐνβατεύσαντες, enbateusantes], they consulted the oracle.”
    Yet a fourth inscription was found in Laodicea, which also refers to some men who “were initiated and then they entered [ἐμβατεύσαντες]. Following this they consulted the oracle.” Likewise, five other inscriptions associated with the Apollo temple from Claros were found, which also described an implied two-step process of initiation. Each refers to the initiates as theopropoi,57 as do the Claros inscriptions, but instead of using ἐμβατεύω (embateuō), these inscriptions use the phrase ἐπετέλεσε καὶ τὰ μυστήρια (epetelese kai ta mystēria): “He completed also the mysteries.” This phrase summarizes the two-stage initiation into the mysteries as a whole (Arnold 1995: 114). Arnold (1995: 114–18) cites numerous scholars who agree that the ἐμβατεύω (embateuō) inscriptions at Claros and Laodicea indicate a second, climactic stage of initiation, in which the initiate “entered” into the inner sanctuary in the very presence of the god (Ramsay 1914: 288–93; Dibelius 1975: 86–87; H. W. Parke 1985: 146, 219–24).58 Also, Arnold (1995: 118) notes that the standard lexical sources affirm this meaning as one of its accepted usages (see the second additional note on 2:18).

More broadly speaking, it is significant that ἐμβατεύω in Classical and Hellenistic Greek refers to a god who habitually enters or dwells in a sacred space or temple. Josephus uses the verb to describe entry into Mount Sinai, which he views as a divine mountain sanctuary (see the second additional note on 2:18). In addition to the Claros and Laodicea inscriptions, though these uses are not part of a mystery initiation context, they further associate ἐμβατεύω (embateuō) with a sacred space or temple.59

Arnold (1995: 136–41) bolsters his position about ἐμβατεύω by arguing that the Isis mystery initiation recorded in Apuleius’s Metamorphoses (AD 170) is representative of earlier mystery rites, including those of Claros and Laodicea, and has unique parallels with Col. 2 (though it does not contain the word ἐμβατεύω since it is written in Latin): (1) “entering” into a sanctuary (cf. Col. 2:18) (2) in conjunction with a visionary experience (cf. 2:18); (3) the vision of passing through the elements (stoicheia) provides a backdrop against which to understand the “elements” in Colossians (cf. 2:8, 20); (4) fasting and other prohibitions of discipline are to make the initiate pure and prepare for experiencing the god in the inner sanctuary (cf. 2:16, 18, 23); (5) the mystery cult ritual is made known to the initiate by means of a secret tradition (cf. 2:8); (6) one of the motives for going through the mystery initiation is to gain authority and protection against evil powers (cf. Col. 2:10, 15).60 However, one could object to Arnold using a late second-century text to inform a first-century text like Colossians, written over a hundred years earlier.

In contrast to Arnold, Dibelius, and others, Francis (1975a: 202–3) contends that “‘entering’ at Claros had no particular technical meaning to distinguish it from other verbs of ‘entering’ in the contemporary oracle literature.” He sees the initiation into the mysteries to refer only to consulting an oracle and not to a formal, second step of entering into the god’s sanctuary. To support this nontechnical use of ἐμβατεύω, Francis (1975a) appeals throughout his essay to other Classical, Hellenistic, and papyrological uses, many of which revolve around acquiring or taking possession of (or entering into) land rights, though other various uses are also found. He concludes that the upshot of these various uses for Col. 2:18 is that “the Colossians sought to enter heaven in order to possess themselves of salvation, a portion in the Lord” (1975a: 199; this is similar to the conclusion of Beetham noted earlier).

While Francis is likely right that this is not a technical use of the mystery rites language (so also O’Brien 1982: 145), it is more likely that ἐμβατεύω (embateuō), even if not formally considered a technical term here from the mystery cult, still carried general connotations of “entering into a sanctuary,” a conclusion that Francis agrees is a viable alternative (1975b: 176 [with minor qualifications in 1975a: 203–4], on which see below).

Thus, in favor of this conclusion about ἐμβατεύω being focused on “entering” the temple is that it is uniquely used with that sense in the Claros and Laodicea inscriptional evidence (all relatively near Colossae in Asia Minor) that is closer in time and geography with Colossians than any other extant uses of this verb in Classical, Hellenistic, and LXX literature. The even more contemporary text of Josephus gives corroboration to the inscriptional usage of “entering into a sanctuary,” as do other significant uses in association with “entering a temple” or “sacred space” in extrabiblical Classical and Hellenistic literature (see the second additional note on 2:18).

H. Preisker (TDNT 2:535–36) acknowledges that the mystery background at Claros by which the “ἐμβατεύειν takes place in a sanctuary” may be the lens through which to understand better ἐμβατεύω in Col. 2:18. But he sees this as the second-best interpretation, preferring to view the verb to express “entering by painful investigation into what has been seen in ecstasy.” But we have already seen that this is the most weakly attested of the four possible meanings for ἐμβατεύω. Preisker does not prefer the mystery-sanctuary background for two reasons: (1) he sees ἐμβατεύω in the Claros and Laodicea inscriptions as “a technical term for the true mystery” and “the entering of the inner sanctuary,” after receiving the mysteries. Thus the inscriptions use ἐμβατεύω not by itself but together with other directly preceding verbs (i.e., “they were initiated and entered” or “he received and entered”). He does not, however, see the word as a technical term in Col. 2:18, since it does not use the additional verbs or refer to a two-step process.61 However, one does not need a full photographic reproduction of the inscriptional formulas for them to be alluded to in Colossians. Preisker’s point, however, is an excellent basis for concluding that in Col. 2:18 ἐμβατεύω is not part of a technical formula or a technical term from Claros but is an allusion to the concept of entering the sanctuary there. Nevertheless, Ramsay’s point (1914: 299) that ἐμβατεύω in Col. 2:18 is awkward in syntax and in meaning (“which everyone feels”) may point to its technical use, though this could just as well point to an allusion to “entering” from the Claros cult without it being part of a technical formula.62

Then (2) Preisker’s second objection is that the inscriptions have the “entering” occurring in a sanctuary, but he says a sanctuary is not mentioned anywhere in 2:18 (or, apparently, its immediate context). However, this commentary has been at pains to detect probable OT allusions to a temple idea at a number of significant points in chapters 1 and 2, both with respect to Christ as the temple (1:19; 2:9) and believers as the temple (1:9, as temple builders; 2:10a; so also echoes of the temple in 1:22, 23; 2:7, 11). Especially important are the temple allusions in 1:19/2:9 and 1:9/2:10a, applied respectively to Christ and then to believers. In particular, 1:19 and 2:9–10 are usually seen as key verses in the epistle, particularly with respect to presenting Christ as the only source of true “filling” in contrast to the errant visionaries’ view of filling, though most commentators do not relate these verses to a temple notion.63 It should not be forgotten that the clear temple allusion in 1:9 also mentions believers being “filled” by the Spirit in order to complete the building of the temple of believers.64 Particularly relevant is 2:9–10a, where Christ as the temple and believers in him as the temple are set in direct contrast to the false “philosophy and empty deception” of 2:8, which is expanded upon in 2:16–23; recall the temple language of 2:7, believers being “rooted, built up, confirmed in faith,” in direct contrast to the bondage of the false teaching (2:8). In 2:16 Paul shifts the perspective back to the errant teaching and to false temple worship, especially in relation to fasting (2:16a and 23), holidays (v. 16b), self-abasement (v. 18, 23), and bodily discipline (v. 23), which had as their main OT purpose a preparatory function to make one pure for temple worship.

Such a number of allusions, with both positive and negative ideas of the temple in the preceding close context of Col. 2:18, warrant understanding that the “entering” is an “entering” into a heavenly temple through a visionary experience. On the basis of references in apocalyptic Judaism and the Claros and Laodicea inscriptions, Lincoln (1981: 113) and Dunn (1996: 183–84) have independently agreed that Col. 2:18 is about a visionary journey of entering the heavenly temple65 but have not offered any supporting contextual evidence of temple references in the context of Colossians itself (see further in the second additional note on 2:18), which could have strengthened their case.

In this regard, Carr (1973: 499) briefly suggests that the preceding word θρησκεία (thrēskeia) in verse 18 further points to the “worship of angels” in a temple. But Carr provides no supporting evidence for this. In fact, a survey of the usage of θρησκεία (thrēskeia) in Classical and Hellenistic pagan and Jewish sources makes viable Carr’s passing suggestion. Although the word can often be used of worship settings outside of a formal temple context, it is very common for the word to be employed with reference to worship in a temple setting, whether Jewish or pagan (see the second additional note on 2:18). For example, Josephus uses the word θρησκεία at least thirty-five times to refer to “worship” in Israel’s temple.66 The word likewise occurs elsewhere in Judaism (Philo, Emb. 298, 232; Sib. Or. 8.380, the latter referring to a pagan temple) and later Christianity (Clem. Hom. 10.22, referring to worship at an idol’s temple). Likewise the word is used in Classical and extrabiblical Hellenistic writings to allude to worship in or in association with a pagan temple.67

And when biblical literature portrays angels in heaven, they are typically seen around God’s throne in the heavenly temple.68 This is also often the case in Judaism (see the second additional note on 2:18), thus suggesting that the “angels,” who are part of “what is seen” in 2:18, appear in a heavenly temple setting.

What we then have in 2:18 is a warning against a false view of the temple, an antitemple detour, which the false teachers are contending people need to experience in order not to be disqualified from first-class Christian status. Paul has repeatedly been making allusion to the temple of Christ and the temple of believers identified with Christ to insist that Christians already have all the temple experience needed for Christian existence. Therefore no one can “disqualify” them by claiming that they have not had a vision of angels and the heavenly temple. Paul has been building up to 2:18 with positive temple references in order to anticipate and contrast the false temple experience with the true. The false teachers in Colossae themselves may have gone through a mystery initiation before professing Christ; they may have heard about the Asia Minor Claros and Laodicea initiations;69 or they may have been acquainted with the association of ἐμβατεύω (embateuō) with “entering a sacred place or temple” from these mystery rites as well as broadly from elsewhere, as attested in Classical and Hellenistic literature. And they may be trying to integrate this dramatic, charismatic experience into their new Christian faith. Accordingly, the misguided teachers “may have understood humility followed by visions seen upon entering ‘the heavenly temple’ to be analogous to initiation followed by reception of oracles upon entering the Apollos sanctuary” (Francis 1975b: 176).70 They appear to have found further justification for this in the OT dietary and holiday laws, which are preparatory for participation in temple worship.71 They wrongly believe these laws are still valid, and they combine them with the pagan mystery background. What may also have played into this syncretism from the Jewish side is that certain traditions in Judaism focus on various ways in which humans could ascend into the temple throne-room of God (see the second additional note on 2:18). To whatever degree the pagan mystery cults are in view, Paul probably uses God’s “revelation of the mystery” in this epistle as a polemic against the pagan cult’s revelation of its mystery. That is, the true mystery is now fully revealed in Christ in contrast to the pseudomystery.72 Thus the false teaching is based on a combination of distorted Hellenistic and Jewish ideas. Such syncretism is not new, since false teaching in the OT arises from combining pagan idolatry with the worship of Yahweh.

The last clause of Col. 2:18 starts with an adverbial participle of means (φυσιούμενος, physioumenos) modifying καταβραβευέτω (katabrabeuetō; Wheeler 1990): “Let no one disqualify you . . . by means of being puffed up in vain73 by his fleshly mind.”* “Flesh” (σάρξ, sarx), as an adjectival genitive here, has to do with the world’s way of thinking in contrast to God’s revelatory word (e.g., see Rom. 8:4–9, 12–13; 1 Cor. 1:26; 2 Cor. 1:17; 5:16; 7:1; 10:2–3 [cf. 10:4–5]; 11:18). Such a person is “puffed up” or arrogant without reason because of having wrong views of asceticism (claiming to be more godly than others because of self-discipline) and claiming a privileged experience of worshiping (or seeing the worship by angels) and entering into the heavenly temple, where the angels surround God. Because of arrogant overconfidence about their visionary experiences and views, the false teachers condemn others who do not have such experiences or abide by such views.74

In 2 Cor. 12:1–9 Paul shows only too well that such visionary experiences have the potential to cause pride unless God quickly humbles a person to prevent it (among others, see Sumney 2008: 157; Bruce 1984: 122; Yates 1993: 59; cf. O’Brien 1982: 145).
[2:19]

This verse is a continuation of verse 18 in that κρατῶν (kratōn, holding fast) is a fourth adverbial participle modifying καταβραβεύω (katabrabeuō) at the beginning of verse 18. This means that verse 19, like verses 16 and 18, is based on the main point of 2:6–15: Christian living is to be founded only on Christ (vv. 6–15). As we have seen, this is the basis for Paul saying in verse 18, “Let no one disqualify you” by criteria that are not Christ-centered. This fourth adverbial participle offers another reason why no one should “disqualify you” (v. 18a): because such people who are attempting to disqualify Christians are “not holding fast to the head,” which is Christ. Thus this point complements the main point of 2:6–15, that the Christian walk should be grounded only on Christ. The phrase “not holding fast to the head” highlights the false basis of the misguided teaching, since Paul has already said that the false “philosophy” is “not based on Christ”* (2:8; so D. Moo 2008: 229–30).

These may have been unbelieving Jews of whom Paul is speaking, or they may have been professing Christians who did not understand the true essence of the Christian faith. If the latter is the case, which is probable, these people were likely not genuine Christians, since they were “not holding to the head” of the body, which is Christ. Thus they are outside of the “body of Christ” (Campbell 2013: 43–44). Ironically, instead of true Christians being disqualified from being part of God’s authentic people, Paul is now saying that these very accusers are to be judged as not being vitally connected to Christ (N. Wright 1986: 124).

Before the phrase “from whom the whole body,” the implied statement “but you hold to the head” should be inserted. Paul is now shifting positively to why the readers should hold to the head (Christ) and not allow themselves to be “judged” and disqualified by the standards of the false teaching. Verse 19 is verbally parallel with Eph. 4:15–16:
Ephesians 4:15b–16 	Colossians 2:19
Let us grow up in all things into him, who is the head, even Christ, from whom the whole body, being fitted together and held together by every joint of the supply, according to the measure of each part, makes the body grow unto the building up of itself in love.* 	[Such a visionary is] not holding fast to the head, from whoma the entire body, being supplied and held together by the joints and ligaments, grows with a growth which is from God.
Note: Underlining shows common Greek words or word groups.

aA “construction according to sense” is observable here, since Christ as “head” (κεφαλή, kephalē) is feminine and the following relative pronoun “whom” (ὅς, hos), which grammatically should be feminine, is masculine because Christ in natural gender is masculine.

This is one of the clearest verbal parallels between Ephesians and Colossians. In Ephesians this passage further develops the Eph. 2:21 description of the temple:
Ephesians 2:21 	Ephesians 4:16
[In Christ] the whole [πᾶσα, pasa] building [οἰκοδομή, oikodomē], being fitted together [συναρμολογουμένη, synarmologoumenē], grows [αὔξει, auxei] into a holy temple in the Lord.* 	[In Christ] the whole [πᾶν, pan] body, being fitted together [συναρμολογούμενον, synarmologoumenon] and held together by every ligament of support, according to the measure of each part, makes the body grow [αὔξησιν, auxēsin] unto the building up [οἰκοδομήν, oikodomēn] of itself in love.*

It is probable, in light of the common wording, that Eph. 4:16 is developing the organic concept of the temple in terms of the church as the organic body of Christ. However, it is difficult to know whether the parallel wording of Eph. 4:16 is dependent upon and developing Col. 2:19 or vice versa, since there is broad consensus that Colossians and Ephesians were written around the same time. If the latter view is correct, then the Colossians passage could also be developing the ideas of the temple and body present in Eph. 4:16. If the former, one might argue that it would be hard to find a temple idea present in Col. 2:19 but that Paul uses the wording of the Colossians text in Eph. 4:16 to develop the temple notion from Eph. 2:21. However, even if this is the case, Col. 2:19 arises in the midst of Paul’s polemic against a pseudo-temple teaching, which he has earlier contrasted with the true temple experience of being in Christ (e.g., 1:19; 2:9–11). Thus 2:19 may itself be a development of the earlier temple ideas in terms of the body of Christ. This may be why Paul would develop the temple picture of Eph. 2:21 through the body image of 4:16. Consequently, whatever the direction of dependence, it is plausible that Col. 2:19 was associated in Paul’s mind with the organic idea of the temple, however implicit that association was. If so, it makes the contrast with the pseudo-temple experience of 2:18 even stronger (“not holding fast to the head, from whom . . .”).

The organic growth of the temple nourished by Christ75 is virtually identical to believers “being filled in [or by] him” in 2:10, which we have seen, together with 2:9, is one of the key texts in Colossians referring to Christ filling believers as his temple. With respect to 2:19, D. Moo (2008: 232) says, “How individual believers find spiritual ‘fullness’ . . . is one of the precipitating issues in the letter,” affirming that the organic “growth” of the body here is to be understood in terms of the “fullness” (likewise Wolter 1993: 150), though he does not relate this to the temple. I agree with his point, but if this fullness is that of Christ filling the temple of believers, then this further enhances our line of argument that verse 19 continues to be associated with the temple theme.

Christ’s exaltation has placed him in the position of being “head of the corner” (or “head corner stone”) of the temple, which is a repeated quotation of Ps. 118:22 (κεφαλὴν γωνίας, kephalēn gōnias) in the NT (Ps. 117:22 LXX; Matt. 21:42//Mark 12:10//Luke 20:17; Acts 4:11; 1 Pet. 2:7; cf. Eph. 2:20). On 2:10 we noted (see additional notes) Macaskill’s (2013: 149–54, 163–71) novel idea that the use of this passage in the NT with reference to the temple may be the origin of Paul’s view of Christ as the “head” (κεφαλή, kephalē) of the church, which is his “body.” Christ is an actual organic part, the chief part of the temple, which includes his corporate people. If Macaskill is correct, then the use of “head” in Col. 2:19 would have not only an idea of authority but may implicitly convey organic temple associations and further strengthen our above analysis that 2:19 is about the organic temple in Christ.
[2:20]

Verses 16 and 18–19, as we saw, were deductions based on the main point of 2:6–15: Christian living is to be grounded only on Christ (vv. 6–15), and therefore two things can be concluded from this: believers (1) are not to be “judged” (v. 16) (2) nor “disqualified” (v. 18) from being first-class Christian citizens because of the non-Christ-centered standards of the false teaching. Verse 20 now draws a result from these two deductions: that the hearers “died with Christ to the elements of the world”* (see the additional note on the οὖν variant). However, this result is introduced by the conditional particle εἰ (ei). This is a first-class condition, in which the first clause (the “if” clause in the indicative mood) is assumed to be true for the sake of the argument. But this is not some merely hypothetical or theoretical statement. That Paul assumes the reality of the first clause, “You have died with Christ,” is apparent from the context (though Paul likely has in mind not everyone but the majority of the readers; see further on 1:23).76 In Col. 3:3 Paul says, “For you have died and your life is hidden with Christ in God” (likewise Col. 2:12, “having been buried with Him”). And he repeats the same thing in Romans (Rom. 6:2, 8–10).

On the basis that the Colossians have “died with Christ to the elements of the world,” Paul asks, “Why, as if you were living in the world, do you submit yourself to decrees?”77 This conditional sentence can be rephrased as “If you have died with Christ to the old world [which has happened], then you do not live in the old world anymore and must not submit yourself to decrees of the old world.” Those who do not heed Paul’s implied prohibition about not submitting to the old world’s decrees should have no assurance that they, indeed, “have died with Christ,” and should conclude that they are still part of the old world. The “if” indicates that Paul is not merely asserting that Christians have died with Christ; he is also inviting them to reflect on whether they have in fact died with Christ and to contemplate the implications for their lives (so D. Moo 2008: 233). Suppose the translation “Why submit?” is preferred instead of “Why do you submit?” Then the last clause would be part of the rhetorical warning and not an accusation of an already adopted practice (an alternative recognized by Dunn 1996: 188).

Some versions translate the phrase τῶν στοιχείων τοῦ κόσμου (tōn stoicheiōn tou kosmou) “the elementary [basic] principles of the world” (so NIV 1984, likewise NJB), while other translations render it “the elementary spirits of the universe [world]” (NRSV, ESV, NET, similarly HCSB and NLT), and still others have “the elements [rudiments] of the world (Vulgate, Douay-Rheims, KJV, ASV). These three different renderings represent three major different interpretations of the phrase, all of which are possible.78 Under any interpretation, the genitive τοῦ κόσμου (tou kosmou) most likely is a genitive of source (“elements from or arising from the world”).79

The first view is that the plural στοιχεῖα (stoicheia) refers to basic or first principles. Sometimes the word could be used to refer to the ABC’s. This notion is applied to the beginning of knowledge, on which further advanced knowledge is built.80 Accordingly, some see that the word in this context refers to the earlier OT laws as basic principles to abide by, but now they should be jettisoned because of the advanced knowledge with Christ’s coming. The mistake of the false teachers is that they are going backward to the OT shadows instead of forward to the progressive revelation in Christ. Appeal is made to Gal. 4:3 in support of this view: Israel “was held in bondage under the elementary principles of the world.”* Here the phrase “under the elementary principles of the world” is parallel with “under the Law” in Gal. 4:4, which is superseded by the coming of Christ. Also, στοιχεῖα appears again in Gal. 4:9–10 in direct connection to the keeping of OT holidays (“You observe days and months and seasons and years”). Some see the “elements” here applying to the basic principles or teachings of Gentile and Jewish religion (cf. O’Brien 1982: 130). Some apply this to the teachings of food laws and of the calendar in Col. 2:16 and 21 (cf. Caird 1976: 190), which most see as having a Jewish background, though some see a Gentile background, and others a mix of the two.

The second view, held by the majority of commentators, is that the plural στοιχεῖα here refers to evil spiritual powers that exercise control over people.81 Again appeal is made to Gal. 4:3, especially in the light of 4:8–9, where the Galatians are said to have been “slaves to those which by nature are no gods,” and then asks, “How is it that you turn back again to the weak and worthless elemental things [viewed as spirit powers], to which you desire to be enslaved all over again?” Further appeal for this meaning comes from the use of στοιχεῖον (stoicheion) or its plural in T. Sol. 8.2; 18.1, 2 (cf. similar uses in T. Sol. 15.5) and from second-century evidence, which also uses στοιχεῖα to refer to malevolent spirit forces. In addition, the sun, moon, and stars were worshiped and sometimes referred to as spirit powers that controlled human destiny. This view could fit well with Colossians, especially with respect to the notion that placating such spiritual beings could come by asceticism in order to obtain heavenly visions, participate in the liturgy of angels, and thus gain protection from evil powers and spiritual fullness in God’s presence (cf. Col. 2:18; so O’Brien 1982: 132).82

A third view is preferable. It is based on the repeated usage of the full phrase τὰ στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου (ta stoicheia tou kosmou) in contemporary Hellenistic writings. This precise phrase is used often, repeatedly, and exclusively to refer to the elements that ancient peoples believed composed the world: air, earth, fire, and water.83 These uses include those even within Hellenistic Judaism, bringing them even closer to Paul’s framework of thought.84 It is probable that Paul would use this phrase substantially in the same way that it is always used in early Hellenistic Greek literature: a reference to the entire earthly creation.85 However, in the context of the epistle and his other writings, he integrates the expression into his own redemptive-historical worldview. Paul likely applies the expression to the entirety of the old, fallen, and sinful world, which is destined for corruption (Col. 2:22) and destruction (2 Pet. 3:10, “the elements [στοιχεῖα] will be destroyed with intense heat, and the earth and its works will be burned up”). Believers’ death with Christ has “separated” them “from” (ἀπό, apo, Col. 2:20; cf. BDAG 105, s.v. ἀπό 1) their part in the old, fallen world, the στοιχεῖα, which no longer have control over them.86 Paul would consider the constituent components of the old world not merely to be material elements but spiritual elements as well: the devil (in the light of 1:13), demonic spirit beings (in the light of 2:10, 15 and Gal. 4:8–9),87 sinful humans (whom Paul says elsewhere live in the “flesh”), and, as we will see, the application of outmoded aspects of the OT law. The main effect of these spiritually sinful elements is that of division of humans from God and from one another. We might say that a chief spiritual building block of the old world was divisiveness, beginning with Satan’s separation from God and Adam’s separation from God and Eve, at the instigation of the serpent (Adam became known in rabbinic Judaism as “the man of separation”; see, e.g., Exod. Rab. 30.13). The OT’s nationalistic laws were introduced further to enhance the division among humans, distinguishing Israelites from all other people groups. Galatians 4:3–10 supports the notion that both the spiritual powers and the OT law were divisive elements of the old world. And, of course, human sin in breaking the law was a major constituent of the old world’s composition.

Thus, this third view—that “elements of the world” refer to the fourfold material makeup of the world, including the spiritual dimensions of the old world—incorporates as part of it the first two alternative views of “elementary principles” and spiritual powers (following D. Moo 2008: 192).88 Each of the first two views (taken individually) is weak in substantially neglecting the observation that in Hellenism the often-occurring phrase τὰ στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου is used only to mean the four cosmic elements. It is unlikely that Paul would use this phrase without carrying over the substance of this meaning.

Consequently, when Paul says, “You have died with Christ from the elements of the world,”* he means, “Your identification with Christ’s death has caused you to die to the old world and to be separated from that world.”89 Since Christ’s place with respect to his old-world body has been removed from the old world, and believers’ spiritual place has been removed from the old cosmos, a significant piece of that world has been removed, providing partial evidence that this old world has begun to fall apart. Although believers have been removed only spiritually from the old world, the sure consummation of this process will result in their full physical resurrection and complete removal from the old creation at the final coming of Christ. Christ’s defeat of the cosmic evil powers (Col. 2:10, 15) has further guaranteed the demise of the old world, which will consummately occur through destruction even of its material elements. The defeat of such powers means that this demonic part of the old world has lost its spiritual power over those now identified with Christ’s death and resurrection (cf. Dunn 1996: 189).

At the same time, a new cosmos has been launched in Christ’s resurrection as the “new creation,” of which those identified with him have begun to become a part (2 Cor. 5:14–17 NIV). This is why, in the nearby following context of Col. 2:20, Paul tells the Colossians, “You have been raised up with Christ” (3:1), and a little later, “You . . . have clothed yourselves with the new man, which is being renewed” (3:10). They have spiritually died with him to the old world and have been spiritually raised with him into a new world, which will inevitably be completed in final, physical resurrection.

Now the only “element” or fundamental building block of the new creation is Christ. And since there is only one Christ, of which the new creation consists and on which it is built, there can be no divisions but only one new created people subsisting in that renovated creation. The elements of divisiveness (the law, evil powers, etc.) that sustained the sinful structure of the old world have been decisively decimated by Christ, and he has replaced them by himself as the only foundational pillar or element of the new world. As discussed earlier concerning the dietary laws, one of the purposes of OT law was to divide Jew from Gentile in the old creation.90 Such laws, which compose part of “the elements of the world,” are no longer valid, so that Jew and Gentile may now become one in Christ. This is one of the reasons why Paul has said that the dietary laws (Col. 2:16a, 20b–23) and holiday laws (2:16b) have been abolished for Christians (Gal. 4:9–10 also identifies “the elements” with “observ[ing] days and months and seasons and years”). This is also why Col. 3:10–11 says that “clothing yourselves with the new man, who is being renewed,”* is a sphere of renewal “in which there is no distinction between Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave and freeman, but Christ is all, and in all.”

This new creation in Christ—where Jew and Gentile are no longer separated by Israel’s nationalistic laws or by demonic powers or by sin, but are one in Christ—is not new in Colossians. We see in 1:26–27 that “the glory of this mystery among the Gentiles . . . is Christ in you [Gentiles + a remnant of Jewish Christians].” This entails Gentiles and Jews, “every man” becoming “complete in Christ” (1:28). Christ’s coming has abolished Israel’s nationalistic laws that separated Jews from Gentiles (Col. 2:16, 20–23), which pointed all along to Christ. This abolishment in Christ has brought Jews and Gentiles together in him as a new creation.

We can picture Christ as a hermeneutical filter through which the law must pass in order to get to the new creation. The parts of the law that are more nationalistic in nature or are overt nationalistic tags distinguishing Israelites ethnically from other people groups are too large or ill-shapen to be able to pass through the filter. The parts of the law more moral and less ethnic in nature are able to pass through the filter. This appears to be the significance of 1 Cor. 7:19: “Circumcision is nothing, and uncircumcision is nothing, but what matters is the keeping of the [moral] commandments of God.” This is parallel with Gal. 6:15 and 5:6, both of which begin with the same negative statement about circumcision, and then respectively add in the contrastive clause: “[There] is . . . a new creation” and “In Christ Jesus . . . faith working through love [means everything].” The “commandments” to be kept in Christ, in the “new creation,” are summed up in the statement “Love your neighbor” (cf. Gal. 5:6 with 5:14). These moral laws are in contrast to what distinguishes people ethnically, which must therefore exclude those parts of the law distinguishing Israelites as a unique ethnic group.91

Therefore, because of the OT prophecy of new creation that has begun fulfillment in Christ, there can no longer be any nationalistic distinctions between Jew and Gentile: the only distinguishing element is Christ. Those nationalistic distinctions entailed in the law are no longer valid. This is why Paul quotes only from the moral law when he applies OT law to believers, or when he quotes other facets of the OT law (such as the civil rules), he uses it in a typological or nontheocratic manner in employing it within the covenant community of the church (e.g., see Paul’s use of Deut. 17:7 in 1 Cor. 5:13). Thus the purely moral aspects of the law, in contrast to those that nationalistically divide, continue to be valid for Christians, since they do not separate believing Jew from believing Gentile.

Therefore, because of having died with Christ and risen with him into a new world (Col. 3:1), the Colossians are “not to submit to decrees”* (δογματίζω, dogmatizō) from the OT that divide. What further confirms this is that δόγμα (dogma) in Eph. 2:15, the nominal form of the verb δογματίζω, refers to “the law of [OT] commandments in decrees,” which Christ “abolished in order to create in himself one new man, thus making peace.”* We have found that δόγμα in Col. 2:14 has the same meaning of OT commandments (see discussion at 2:14).
[2:21]

The contents of the decrees that are uppermost in mind at the end of Col. 2:20 are now listed in this verse: “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch!”92 This is another way of referring to the OT dietary prohibitions of “food and drink” in 2:16. In addition to one of the law’s purposes of dividing Jew from Gentile, we saw that the main purpose of the dietary laws was to cleanse people so that they could worship in the temple (see on 2:16).93 In the LXX the word ἅπτω (haptō) refers to “handling” or “touching” something that would make one unclean.94 In 2:16 we concluded that the phrase “food and drink and touch” (ἁφή, haphē; Let. Aris. 142, 162) refers to what is unclean for Israelites. Here is the full statement: “To prevent our being polluted by contact with others or by mixing with bad influences, [God] fenced us in on all sides with purity observances connected with food and drink and touch and hearing and sight, after the manner of the Law” (Let. Aris. 142).

The prohibition not to “taste” translates the Greek verb γεύομαι (geuomai), which 2 Macc. 6:18–20 uses to describe a Jewish martyr who would not eat swine’s flesh because “such things are not lawful to be tasted.”95 Similarly, Exod. 19:12 uses θιγγάνω (thinganō) synonymously with ἅπτω (haptō) to prohibit unclean Israel from touching holy Mount Sinai; the word, used rarely, refers to not touching forbidden objects for those “involved in purification rites” (Let. Aris. 106; also cited by Beetham 2008: 211–12).

In the light of this, probably the “handling” and “touching” in Col. 2:21 pertains to “food and drink” but extends to other unclean things as well. But that “food and drink” (from 2:16) is the focus is apparent from verse 22, which says these are “things destined to perish with use.” Such uncleanness makes one impure for temple worship. The touching of unclean things is sometimes explicitly related to the temple. For example, “Anyone who touches a corpse . . . and does not purify himself, defiles the tabernacle of the LORD”; hence such a person is prohibited from entering into the tabernacle (Num. 19:13). Elsewhere in the OT and Judaism it is repeatedly mentioned that uncleanness cannot be tolerated within the temple precincts (see the additional note on 2:21).

In this light, as concluded in 2:16, the false teachers are exhorting believers to be meticulous about not contaminating themselves with unclean food or drink, according to OT law, in order to foster a visionary experience of angels and of the heavenly temple, though this was a false cultic experience.96
[2:22]

These decrees about clean and unclean foods97 were appropriate in the OT epoch, but in the new age they have only one purpose: they “are all destined to corruption with the using.”* Rather than enhancing a visionary experience and purifying one for participation in a heavenly temple, they are useless and only result in “corruption” (φθορά, phthora). Such outmoded laws must eventuate in corruption because they are now part of the old creation, all of which will be finally destroyed. In contrast, the new creation and the temple in Christ are incorruptible and will never break down, decay, or decompose like the old world and its old institutions.98 For example, Israel’s old temple was destroyed, and likewise all the laws pertaining to it, including the dietary laws, are no longer useful for drawing near to God.99 When such laws are made a requirement for Christians, then a corrupt religion is created, which in fact is idolatrous. It is idolatrous because the focus is on “self-made religion” and not on Christ; it is idolatrous because it focuses on outmoded laws that pointed to Christ and not on Christ as the fulfillment of those laws.

The idolatrous nature of these laws is expressed in the next clause of verse 22: the “decrees” are “in conformity with” and “based on”* (κατά, kata)100 “the commandments and teachings of men” (τὰ101 ἐντάλματα καὶ διδασκαλίας τῶν ἀνθρώπων, ta entalmata kai didaskalias tōn anthrōpōn). This is an allusion to Isa. 29:13 LXX: “This people draws near with their lips [and] they honor me, but they remove their heart far from me, and they worship me vainly by teaching the commandments and teachings of men [ἐντάλματα ἀνθρώπων καὶ διδασκαλίας, entalmata anthrōpōn kai didaskalias].”*102 The Colossians context likely links such “commandments and teachings” to “empty deception” based on “the tradition of men” instead of “on Christ” (2:8). Paul’s expansion of the earlier “tradition of men” by Isaiah’s “commandments and teachings of men” associates the false teaching with idolatry, since that is the contextual sense in the Isaiah passage itself, where the wording concerns the sin of idol worship (see the additional notes).103

Paul says that the OT “shadows” (v. 17 NLT), particularly the food laws (about unclean “food and drink” and the commandments “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch!”), are called “the commandments and teachings of men.” In the inaugurated age of fulfillment, it is idolatry to substitute the “shadows” for Christ, who is their eschatological substance (see further on 2:17). Furthermore, among the seven times that the tripartite formula “feasts, new moons, and Sabbath days” occurs in the OT, two refer to them as having become idolatrous.104 Thus there is already a precedent for the transforming of OT festivals into idolatrous activities. In Gal. 4:8–10 virtually the identical phrase (“You observe days and months and seasons and years”), together with the word “elements” (cf. Col. 2:20), is directly linked to objects of idolatry. That Col. 2:16–23 describes idolatrous traditions may also be suggested by 3:3–7, where at least part of the saints’ former unbelieving life is summarized as “idolatry.”105

In Paul’s time this sin manifested itself by Israel worshiping not stone or wooden idols, but rather the idol of human tradition, which was a distortion of the law. The same reference in Mark 7:7//Matt. 15:9 carries with it in context the same idolatrous notion. Mark 7:8 sums up the Isa. 29:13 quotation by referring to it with the phrase “the tradition of men.” Since Paul uses that same phrase in Col. 2:8 to refer to the false philosophy, it appears that he alludes to Isa. 29:13 there and also here in 2:22, under the influence of Jesus’s earlier usage.106 Thus the Isa. 29:13 allusion expands on the earlier reference in 2:8. The context of the Isaiah reference in Mark 7 also reflects that Isa. 29:13 was understood in terms of idolatry107 (and all this in a context dealing with Jewish laws prohibiting eating with impure hands; Mark 7:1–5).108 An idol, in essence, is anything that is worshiped in place of the true God. For example, in Eph. 5 and Col. 3 Paul says that idol worship can be expressed in other ways than merely bowing down to a literal statue; he says in the latter text that “greed . . . [is] idolatry,” though the preceding sins of “immorality, uncleanness, passion, evil desire”* are also likely associated with this idolatry (Col. 3:5).109 Paul is saying in Col. 2:22 that to trust in the shadowy dietary laws of the OT more than in Christ (or on the same level as Christ) is to worship an idol. Colossians 2:17–19 also counters idolatry, since it says that trusting in the “old shadows” also involves substituting “the worship of the angels” for worship of Christ (which is “not holding fast to the head”).110 The reference to Isa. 29:13 in Titus 1:14 is used similarly, since “commandments of men” is parallel to “Jewish myths,” both of which result in “turning away from the truth.” Elsewhere Paul also understands that Jews who trust only in the law and not in Christ are idolaters.111

Paul probably uses Isa. 29:13 analogically: just as Israel was idolatrous in its past, so again professing Jewish believers (i.e., Jewish Christians) are propagating trust in idolatrous objects.112
[2:23]

Paul continues to elaborate on the meaning of the decrees. The sentence is syntactically difficult. Though there are several ways of solving this problem, we prefer the following translation: “Such things are matters having, to be sure, the appearance113 [or reputation]114 of wisdom in self-made worship and self-abasement in neglect of the body, but they are of no value with respect to satisfying the flesh.”*115

Such commandments “have the appearance of wisdom,” but it is a hollow wisdom. This wisdom does not derive from God but arises from idol worship. Rather, “all the [true] treasures of wisdom and knowledge” have their unique origin in Christ (D. Moo 2008: 240).

The very first mention of “wisdom” in Colossians comes in 1:9. There we saw that 1:9 was part of an explicit allusion to Exod. 31:3 (cf. likewise 35:31), where Israelites were “filled . . . with a divine Spirit of wisdom and understanding and knowledge in every good work.”* The point is that believers are “filled” with such wisdom to do their part in contributing to the building of the spiritual temple (see on 1:9). If the “wisdom” of 1:9 is in mind, then this first mention of divine “wisdom” related to the true temple is a contrast here with false wisdom about visions of angels in the pseudo-temple. In addition, recall that commentators generally acknowledge that Christ is presented as “wisdom” in Col. 1:15–18 (see my comments on these verses), and we saw that Christ was portrayed in 1:19 as the end-time temple of God’s presence from Ps. 67:17–18 LXX (68:16–17 Eng.). The link between Col. 1:15–18 and 1:19 may reflect early Judaism, which viewed “wisdom” as having its “headquarters” in the temple (see the additional note on 1:19). To whatever degree this may be in mind, the contrast would include Christ as the temple and the source of true wisdom116 versus false wisdom about the temple.

This apparent wisdom is expressed in “self-made religion and self-abasement and neglect of the body.” The word ἐθελοθρησκία (ethelothrēskia) can be rendered in the following ways: (1) “self-made religion,” (2) “do-it-yourself religion,” (3) “idiosyncratic religion” (BDAG 276, s.v. ἐθελοθρησκία), (4) “self-made worship,” (5) “will-worship” (ASV, KJV), (6) “self-imposed worship” (NIV, NET), (7) “self-chosen worship” (MacDonald 2000: 117), (8) or “freely chosen worship” (D. Moo 2008: 240). Any of these translations are viable. This “wisdom” then is a “self-manufactured religion” (Thompson 2005: 65), which has its source in the human will in contrast to wisdom rooted in God’s or Christ’s will (1:9, “You [should] be filled with the knowledge of His will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding”; cf. likewise 1:27–28; 3:16).

This notion of “self-made religion” is another way of expressing idol worship, that is, worshiping something of one’s own making and not worshiping the unmade Creator. This is an elaboration of 2:18a, where also is found the unique combination of pleasure-taking or self-desiring (θέλων, thelōn) that is expressed in legalistic “self-abasement” (ταπεινοφροσύνη, tapeinophrosynē) and idolatrous “worship” (θρησκεία, thrēskeia; i.e., actually “worshiping angels”). In the light of the parallel with verse 18, the idea of “self-made worship” should be expanded to “self-made worship of angels.”117 The “self-made religion” specifically in mind is that of “self-abasement and neglect of the body,” which apparently was thought to foster a spiritual experience of seeing and then venerating angels. This passage justifies our earlier rendering of ταπεινοφροσύνη in verse 18 as “asceticism,” since here the word is further explained by “neglect of the body” (ἀφειδίᾳ σώματος, apheidia sōmatos), which is characteristic of asceticism (so also D. Moo 2008: 241). Some MSS omit the “and” in this phrase, thus reading “self-abasement in [or by] neglect of the body” (see the first additional note on 2:23). Either way, “neglect of the body” likely further explains “self-abasement” (or “asceticism”). Uppermost in mind here is the avoiding of “food and drink” (2:16; so also 2:21). We have already observed that the verbal form of ταπεινοφροσύνη was closely connected to fasting, wearing sackcloth, and mourning (see further on 2:18).

Such ascetic practices have “no value in checking self-indulgence” (NRSV, followed by BDAG 830, s.v. πλησμονή). The false teaching aimed at neglecting basic bodily needs apparently in order to sharpen one’s spiritual senses to prepare for a visionary experience118 of angels in the heavenly temple (as argued at 2:18a above). But on the contrary, such harsh practices, Paul says, do not make one’s spiritual senses keener. Rather, these external ascetic practices have their origin in the “flesh” (σάρξ, sarx, v. 18) and therefore cannot prevent fulfilling inner sinful desires of the flesh or “checking self-indulgence” (πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός, plēsmonēn tēs sarkos).119

This last phrase of verse 23 could be rendered “but are of no value for the purpose of120 filling of the flesh” (so Geneva, Douay-Rheims; “flesh” refers to the person of the old world in parallel with the “mind of the flesh” in v. 18). Often πλησμονή has the idea of “filling” in the LXX, and that may be the notion here,121 so that a preferable translation might be “for the full satisfaction of the flesh” (Abbott 1897: 278), especially with respect to being filled with spiritual satisfaction.122 If so, the “filling” in verse 23 would be a polemical and sarcastic reference to the opponents’ attempt to be filled with God’s presence through asceticism and fasting and to dispose themselves to ascend into the heavenly temple to worship angels and then to receive God’s fullness.123 This would be more in line with the notion in Col. 2:18 of “self-abasement and neglect of the body” fostering a spiritual experience of seeing and then venerating angels in a heavenly sanctuary. Paul opposes this false experience with the earlier reference to Christ being the one in whom “all the fullness” of God’s temple presence “dwells” (1:19; 2:9), with the result that believers in Christ “have been filled” with that presence (2:10a).124 Accordingly, it is possible to translate ἐθελοθρησκία (ethelothrēskia) as “would-be worship,” Paul’s way of casting doubt on the opponents’ claim about their visionary experience of the temple, which purportedly has “filled” them.125 Since they have cut themselves off from “Christ the head” (2:19), they cannot experience the true temple and the divine presence therein.126 They can never be filled with God’s special presence because they exist in the “flesh” of the old world.

Additional Notes

2:17. Philo uses σκιά (skia) together with σῶμα (sōma) to refer to the words of Scripture as “shadows” and the deeper meaning to be the “substance” or “reality.” In Post. 112 the name of Zillah, one of Lamech’s wives (Gen. 4:19), is “interpreted” to mean “‘shadow,’ a symbol of the equalities of the body and of the external good things, which, in their real essence, are in no way better than a shadow” (trans. Yonge 1993: 143). In Conf. 190, Philo says, “The mere words of the scriptures are, as it were, but shadows of bodies, and . . . the meanings . . . apparent to investigation beneath them, are the real things to be pondered” (trans. Yonge 1993: 251, emphasis added); see likewise Heir 72, where Philo says that it is “impossible” to “use shadows to point to substances [bodies], words to point me to facts” (similarly Migr. 12). In a literal sense, a “shadow” always follows a “body” (Philo, Decal. 82; Virt. 118, 181). Josephus (J.W. 2.28) says Archelaus “desires the shadow of that royal authority, whose substance [body] he had already seized to himself.” Common to all these uses is the notion of something that is a shadow pointing to the substance of something, whether that be words to true, deeper meanings or literal shadows pointing to a physical human body. Paul is the only one who uses σκιά and σῶμα in a redemptive-historical context to refer to OT institutions that point to later realities (substances) in the NT era that are of a prophetic fulfillment nature (though Heb. 10:1 uses σκιά in this manner). The uses in Judaism above show that the rendering of “substance” for σῶμα (sōma) in verse 17 is viable; yet in light of the idea of “a body casting a shadow,” a better rendering might be “substantial body” (in line with the discussion in the commentary above).

2:17. Some affirm that the Christian Sunday is not a continuation of the OT Sabbath day since v. 17 identifies the “Sabbaths” of v. 16 to be part of the “shadows” of the OT dietary laws and other religious holidays that have passed away because the substance, Christ, to which they pointed has come.

I have argued elsewhere that Gen. 2:2–3 expresses a creational mandate to rest, in which there is a creational pattern of one day in seven that is “blessed and set apart” from the other days (Beale 2011: 776–81). The goal of this creational pattern predates Israel’s Sabbath (and all of its added detailed requirements for observance), was partially expressed through the nation’s Sabbath, and continues on after Israel’s institutions find their completion in and are abolished in Christ. In this regard, Calvin affirms,

Therefore when we hear that the Sabbath was abrogated by the coming of Christ, we must distinguish between what belongs to the perpetual government of human life [the creational mandate], and what properly belongs to ancient figures [i.e., what was unique to Israel], the use of which was abolished when the truth was fulfilled [when Christ came].127

That Israel’s Sabbath observance was partly based on a creational mandate and partly on the nation’s unique observance is clear from the two versions of the Sabbath law, respectively in Exod. 20:8–11 and Deut. 5:12–15. The first gives God’s rest after creation as the basis of Israel’s Sabbath, and the second appeals to God’s deliverance of the nation from Egypt as the basis, when God delivered Israel from laborious bondage to the rest of freedom. The Sabbath commemoration of the exodus redemption also points forward to the greater end-time exodus redemption through Christ, which has been fulfilled in his death and resurrection as the Passover Lamb (e.g., see John 19:34–37; 1 Cor. 5:7–8). Thus in various ways the Sabbath pointed to and was fulfilled in Christ, but the aspect of Israel’s Sabbath that is based on and reflects the creational mandate of Gen. 2 continues on into the church age. “Creation and redemption are both motives for its [Israel’s Sabbath] observance, the one for all men [including Israel], the other especially for Israel” (W. Stott, NIDNTT 3:406). Vos sums up well the relationship of the creational mandate to Israel’s Sabbath and to the church’s Sabbath:

The Sabbath . . . has passed through the various phases of development of redemption, remaining the same in essence but modified as to its form, as the new state of affairs at each point [epoch] might require. . . . From all this [the various laws related to Israel’s Sabbath and typological sabbatical celebrations] we have been released by the work of Christ, but not from the Sabbath as instituted at Creation. (Vos 1948: 139–40, 142–43)

Thus the unique nationalistic aspects of Israel’s laws, including the way the Sabbath was practiced and on what day it was observed, do not continue. There is still a one-day special observance on Sunday for the church, the purpose of which is to look forward to the consummation of end-time rest in the new heavens and earth. This Sunday observance is focused around worship of God and Christ in church (and likely not complete rest throughout the day, as was the case with the intense form of the Jewish Sabbath) and is a continuation of the creational Sabbath ordinance of Gen. 2:3, whose purpose was to look forward to consummate eschatological spiritual and physical rest. The pre-Israelite observance of every seventh day was probably not a day of complete physical rest but was to recall God’s seventh day of resting, and the worshipful observance of every seventh day was apparently to remind humanity of a final eternal Sabbath rest without “evening and morning,” which would no longer need to be repeated.

Consequently Col. 2:16 appears to be referring to Israel’s system of sabbatical holidays (not merely the weekly Sabbath) and not to the creation mandate in Gen. 2:2–3 (for an expansion of this discussion on the Sabbath, see the comments on 2:16, esp. the note on “Sabbaths,” and Beale 2011: 775–801).

2:18. A textual variant adds μή (mē) after ἅ (ha), which is preferred, e.g., by the KJV: “intruding into those things which he hath not seen” (likewise Geneva, Douay-Rheims). The idea would be that the false teachers’ claim to have seen visions is a false claim. Though the majority of MSS support including the negative, the earliest and best MSS support the omission of μή (mē), which is likely the original reading.

2:18. Josephus uses ἐμβατεύω (embateuō) in narrating Moses’s ascent to Sinai by observing that no shepherd hitherto was “daring to enter into it” (τολμώντων ἐμβατεύειν εἰς αὐτό, tolmōntōn embateuein eis auto) because of “the opinion people had that God dwelt there” (Ant. 2.265). Thus Moses was “daring to approach a spot unto which no man had arrived before because it was divine [θεῖον, theion]” (2.267). Moses entered into Sinai and to him “were delivered divine oracles from the fire” (2.269). At Sinai, Moses was “granted speech from Him and vision of Him,” and when Moses was later to be “sacrificing” at Sinai, he was to “invoke him [God] by name to be present at the sacred rites” (2.275). Sinai was known as the place of “God’s sojourning,” making it “terrible and unapproachable” (3.76).

In this light, Josephus likely viewed Sinai to be a mountain sanctuary, which is suggested further by his saying that God “desired that a tabernacle should be made for Him . . . so that when we [Israel] move elsewhere we may . . . have no more need to ascend to Sinai, but that He himself, frequenting the tabernacle, may be present at our prayers” (Ant. 3.100–101, likewise 212, 222). Thus God’s sanctuary at Sinai was transferred to the tabernacle.

Philo likewise refers to Sinai as a “most sacred [ἱερώτατον, hierōtaton] mountain” (Mos. 2.70), to which Moses had “been called up” (so almost identically “most sacred [ἱερώτατον, hierōtaton] of mountains” in Spec. 3.125). Part of the meaning that “the glory of God came down upon Mount Sinai” is that it was “the divine place” (Philo, QE 2.45, trans. Marcus 1953); Sinai was “a holy and divine place” (QE 2.39, trans. Marcus 1953) and was “the unchanging habitation of the divine place” (QE 2.40, trans. Marcus 1953). Moses (perhaps including the seventy elders and Aaron) is also said “to enter a dark cloud [on Sinai] and to dwell in the forecourt of the palace/temple128 of the Father” (QE 2.28). Similarly, while Moses “was remaining above [on the mountain], he was initiated [ἐμυσταγωγεῖτο, emystagōgeito]129 by being taught according to all the prominent matters of the priesthood” (Mos. 2.71). Indeed, Moses himself was a “high priest” on Sinai who performed “the rites belonging to his sacred office” (Mos. 2.75; Colson 1935).

Therefore we have in Josephus the notion of Moses “ascending” into the mountain sanctuary of Sinai,130 a place where no one else had formerly “entered” (ἐμβατεύω), to experience the theophanic presence of God,131 which Philo terms his being “initiated” or “instructed in the mysteries” on the “most sacred mountain.” Perhaps not coincidentally, Josephus also notes that the Israelites were “practicing purity” and abstaining from sexual relations with their wives while Moses was on the mountain to promote God’s “converse with Moses” (Ant. 3.78). And Moses himself fasted during the entire forty-day experience (3.99), presumably to prepare himself for continually encountering the very presence of God at Sinai. Philo (Mos. 2.68–70) says that in “having gone up” to Sinai (where he, along with others, was “desiring the vision of God,”132 “it was necessary that he [Moses] purify” himself partly by having no “food and drink” in order that he should not be “defiled.”

In addition to the Claros and Laodicea inscriptions, and not recognized previously, this reference in Josephus is another significant instance of ἐμβατεύω referring to “entering” into the very presence of God in his sanctuary.

Francis (1975a: 198–99) says that ἐμβατεύω in Josh. 19:49, 51 LXX refers to “entering” into the promised land to possess an inheritance, defined as “a portion in the LORD” in light of Josh. 22:24–27. He says it is possible that Col. 2:18 has drawn from a tradition related to Joshua, so that the idea is to “enter heaven to possess themselves of salvation, a portion in the LORD.” But the “portion in the LORD” is more fully unpacked in these very Joshua passages as Israel being able to worship at the tabernacle. First, Eleazar the priest “divided by lot” a portion of land each of the tribes was to possess. This division took place “at the door of the tabernacle of witness” (Josh. 19:51 LXX). In Josh. 22:24–27 the sons of Reuben say that they have built an altar on the border of the land only as a memorial to assure that no one could say they “had no portion in the LORD,” and this is explained as “not being able to worship the LORD”* (22:25). Thus the sons of Reuben did not build the altar “for burnt-offerings, nor for meat-offerings”* (22:26). This is further explained as their desire “to do service to the LORD before him, with our burnt-offerings and our meat-offerings”* (22:27). To be able to worship in this way means that no one can say they “have no portion in the LORD” (22:27). The place where they want to “do service to the LORD” and to give these offerings is then said to be at “the altar of the LORD which is before His tabernacle” (22:29). Thus Reuben’s ability to worship at the tabernacle in the land is virtually equated with having “a portion in the LORD” or is evidence of having such a portion. The two notions are inextricably linked, so that if there were some connection to Col. 2:18, “entering” would still have to do with having “a portion in the LORD” in direct connection with temple worship. Schweizer (1982: 161–62) suggests the possibility that the mystery cults’ idea of entry into the sanctuary, combined with the LXX notion of entering into the promised land, is applied to the Colossians’ entry into heaven.

There are other uses of ἐμβατεύω where it refers to a god habitually walking or entering into a sacred space or temple (in the following references, the translations of ἐμβατεύω are in italics):

1. Sophocles (Oed. Col. 679–80) speaks of “Dionysus,” in a sacred garden space of “the god’s inviolate foliage,” who “always treads [enters] with the nursing nymphs [goddesses] who constantly attend him” (5th cent. BC; partly trans. Jebb 1989).

2. Euripides (Rhesus 225, in LCL) portrays Apollo, the “King Thymbraean, O Delian Lord,” who habitually “treads [enters] the temple of Lycia” (5th cent. BC).

3. Dionysius of Halicarnassus (Rom. Ant. 1.77.1–2) says a young woman was raped by a god in a sacred “grove consecrated to Mars,” in a “sacred precinct . . . consecrated” to the “divinity” who “treads [enters] into the place”* (1st cent. BC).

4. Euripides (Telephus frag. 696, in Page 1941: 17.3) refers to the god “Pan, who haunts [habitually treads or enters] Arcadia’s stormy massif,” apparently the sacred space where Pan dwells (5th cent. BC); see Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Lit. Comp. 26 (this Dionysius text is actually a quotation of the Euripides fragment); cf. similarly Cratinus, Test. Frag. P. 359 (5th cent. BC).

5. Aeschylus (Pers. 447–49, in LCL) says: “There is an island in front of Salamis . . . whose seashore is a haunt of Pan [lit., Pan trod on the seashore],” who helped the Greeks defeat the Persians there (6th–5th cent. BC). This again refers to a sacred space where the god Pan dwells.

The next references pertain to humans stepping into sacred spaces:

6. Sophocles (Oed. King 820–30, in LCL) has Oedipus say, “Am I not altogether unholy, if I must leave my country, and in my exile never see my dear ones, nor enter [set foot upon] my native land?” He asks whether this exile is coming upon him “by the action of a cruel deity” and concludes, “Never, never, O sacred majesty of the gods, may I see that day.” The native land of Oedipus is not just his homeland but is a kind of sacred space for him, entrance to which is determined by the gods and requires him to be “holy.”

7. Almost identical to the reference to sacred space under control of the gods in Sophocles, Oedipus King, is Euripides (El. 585–95, in LCL), who refers to the “exile” of Orestes and has the choir say, “You have arrived. . . . Some god, some god is bringing us victory, dear friend. . . . Utter prayer to the gods that with good luck . . . your brother may enter [tread] the city.”

I have provided more context for the references in points 1, 2, and 5 above as well as to points 6 and 7 in an attempt to confirm the same conclusion drawn by Carr (1973: 498) about ἐμβατεύω referring to either a god or a human entering or treading in either a sacred space or a temple.133 I have adduced additional Classical Greek and Hellenistic examples to further strengthen his conclusion. In the light of these references, he then concludes that ἐμβατεύω in Col. 2:18 refers to “treading upon a sacred place,” i.e., entering the heavenly sanctuary or “haunting the courts of heaven” (Carr 1973: 499). While his conclusion is correct as far as it goes, his citations, plus the above references to the Claros and Laodicea inscriptions, together with uses in Josephus and Joshua, form a conceptual association of entering the temple as a background for understanding the verb in Col. 2:18.

In the LXX the concept of “entering” into the temple is common, expressed often through the verbs εἰσπορεύομαι (eisporeuomai) and εἰσέρχομαι (eiserchomai), as well as εἴσειμι (eiseimi). For example, εἰσέρχομαι is used for “entering into” the “tabernacle” (σκηνή, skēnē, about thirteen times) or the “temple” (ναός, naos, or ἱερόν, hieron, about twelve times) or “holy place” (ἅγιος, hagios, about eight times) or “sanctuary” (ἁγιαστήριον, hagiastērion, twice). In Rev. 15:8, “No one was able to enter [εἰσέρχομαι] the temple” in heaven “until the seven plagues of the angels were finished” (likewise Rev. 21:27). In 1 En. 14.9–14, Enoch “enters into” (εἰσέρχομαι) various outer parts of the heavenly temple until he “enters into (εἰσέρχομαι) the house,” which is the equivalent of the heavenly temple’s “holy place,” and there he peers into the holy of holies “and sees a vision” (cf. further 1 En. 14.15–23); finally God asks him to “come near” (14.24).

The standard lexical sources affirm that one of the options for ἐμβατεύω is a meaning derived from the mysteries and associated with “entering into the inner sanctuary,” as the climax of initiation, which most of these sources prefer for the meaning in Col. 2:18: LSJ 539: “to be initiated into the mysteries,” which LSJ applies to Col. 2:18; another meaning offered by LSJ is “enter into a sacred cave”; BAA, citing the Claros inscriptions, sees the word to be a technical term of the mystery language, which concerns one who steps into the temple (das Heiligtum) that he has seen in the ecstatic experience; likewise BDAG (321, s.v. ἐμβατεύω) offers this meaning as a viable option. About ἐμβατεύω MM 206 (following Ramsay 1914) say, “More significant, however, than any of the above citations [of papyri usage] for the meaning of the verb in its only occurrence in the NT (Col. 2:18) is its use in the mystery religions to denote the climax of initiation, when the mystēs ‘sets foot on’ the entrance to [the sanctuary and] the new life which he is now to share with the god.” L&N 1:533–34 write: “ἐμβατεύω in Col. 2.18 is a technical term derived from the mystery religions”; while acknowledging the other possible renderings, they offer three translations of ἐμβατεύω (embateuō) that they consider most likely: (1) “who enters the sanctuary which he saw in ecstasy”; (2) “taking his stand on what he has seen in the mysteries”; (3) “by what he saw when he was initiated.”134

In addition to the possible relevance of the passage about Sinai from Josephus (above), other aspects of Judaism may also have been combined with this pagan focus on “entering the sanctuary” in the mysteries. Certain Jewish traditions focus on saints practicing purification rites in order to ascend in mystical visions into the heavenly temple and throne room of God (Davila 2000a: 261–62; 2000b: 103, cf. 112–13, 138; these refer only to people who mystically ascend into the heavenly temple without explicit mention of purification rites). Some argue that in their earthly liturgy the Qumran sectarians self-identified with the heavenly temple worship and with the angels who participated in that worship (esp. in the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice; see Davila 2000b: 89–90; and Newsom 1990: 115–18, with some qualifications). One could call this a vicarious ascent to heaven. The Testament of Levi pictures Levi “entering” (though not the word ἐμβατεύω) through various stages of heaven (2.7–8) and being commissioned to “tell forth his mysteries,” which he sees there (2.10). In the “holy of holies” God dwells with “archangels,” who worship and offer sacrifices (3.3–6). An angel opens for Levi “the gates of heaven,” and he sees “the Holy Most High sitting on the throne” (5.1). Levi is then commissioned as a priest while he is in heaven (8.1–18). In T. Isaac 4.45–47 we read of the righteous who ascend to heaven, when “they will be engaged in holy, angelic service by reason of purity. They will be presented before the Lord and his angels because of their pure offerings and their angelic service, . . . and the angels will be their friends” (trans. W. F. Stinespring, OTP 1:908; cited by Francis 1975b: 178).

In addition to Qumran’s Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice and the Testament of Isaac, other Jewish texts cited by Francis (1975b: 176–79), Sappington (1991: 91–94), and Dunn (1996: 180–81) argue in favor of a subjective genitive by appealing to the following texts, where they contend that humans worship with angels: T. Job 48–50; 1 En. 71.5–12; 3 En. 1.9–12; Apoc. Zeph. 8.2–4 (praying with angels); Apoc. Ab. 17.1–7 (temple in 18.3–13); Mart. Ascen. Isa. 8.17; 9.28–33; 1QH 11.20–22 (olim 3.20–22); 1QSb 4.25–26. D. Moo (2008: 227), however, claims that some of these texts do not precisely portray humans worshiping God with angels; he contends that T. Job 48–50 refers to humans employing the speech of angels in praising God, 1 En. 71.11–12 refers to humans only being in the presence of angels before God, Apocalypse of Zephaniah alludes to someone praying with angels, and 1QH 11.20–22 (olim 3.20–22) is only about humans being in the midst of angels. But even these texts are close to the idea of humans worshiping with angels: e.g., praying with angels in Apocalypse of Zephaniah should be considered participating in a heavenly liturgy with angels; 1 Enoch says Enoch was “blessing, glorifying, and extolling” God in the presence of angels; and 1QH pictures the hymnist not only in the company of angels but also “entering into communion” with them and “praising” God’s “name in joyful concord” with the angels (see Dupont-Sommer 1962: 209; 1QH 11.23 [olim 3.23]). All of these above texts may lend some support to the subjective genitive option, but our interest in these texts is also that most of them—likely not coincidentally, as in Qumran’s Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice—portray people entering not merely heaven but into the heavenly temple135 (so 1 En. 71.5–12 [and cf. its development of the temple scene in 1 En. 14.9–20]; 3 En. 1.9–12 [and cf. 1.5–7, 10, 12; 2.1–2]; see Apoc. Ab. 17.1–7 [and cf. 18.3–13]; 1QSb 4.25–26, 28; Mart. Ascen. Isa. 8.17; 9.28–33 [and cf. a temple concept expressed in 7.17, 27; 9.27, 37]; 10.6; 11.32). Note also b. Hag. 14b: R. Jose the priest and Rab Johanan b. Zakkai were seen in a dream “reclining on Mount Sinai, when a voice said, ‘Ascend hither, ascend hither! [Here are] great banqueting chambers . . . for you and your disciples’” (which is in the context of explanations about chariot visions regarding the heavenly temple). Although none of these references contain the word ἐμβατεύω, they still lend further conceptual support to the apocalyptic visions “which you have seen” in Col. 2:18 being a vision of entering into the heavenly temple. Dunn (1996: 183–84) also argues the same thing on the basis of the above apocalyptic evidence. Stuhlmacher (2018: 441–42) sees the Qumran Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice as the background for the “entering” in Col. 2:18, since these songs refer not only to angels worshiping in the heavenly sanctuary but also to people “raising” themselves to join the angelic worship in the heavenly temple.

As noted earlier, it is possible that θρησκείᾳ τῶν ἀγγέλων (thrēskeia tōn angelōn) could be an objective genitive (humans’ “worship of angels”) or a subjective genitive (“worship by angels”). As noted above, to argue for a subjective genitive, appeal is sometimes made to Qumran’s Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice, where repeatedly there is description of angels worshiping in the heavenly temple (and some contend that the Qumranites believed they were identified with the angels in their heavenly temple worship [see Davila 2000b: 89–90]). Francis has argued this, appealing to other Qumran texts and Jewish apocalyptic literature that depicts angelic liturgies (see Francis 1975b: 177–79). In this respect, if it is true that Col. 2:18 is referring to angels surrounding God in his temple, this may support a subjective genitive.

We have argued earlier, however, that the immediate context of Col. 2:18 points to the probability of an objective genitive. An objective genitive is further pointed to by observing that θρησκεία followed by a genitive in the LXX and early Judaism is usually an objective genitive (Wis. 14:27, “worship of idols”; Philo, Spec. 1.315, “worship of gods”); the following all involve θρησκεία followed or preceded by a modifying objective genitive where “God” or the personal pronoun for God is the object of worship: Josephus, Ant. 1.222; 4.306; 7.341; 8.225, 251, 256, 270, 395; 9.133; 10.53; 12.271; cf. similarly 12.253; 13.199; 17.214. See also 1 Clem. 45.7 (“worship of the Most High”). In addition to these Jewish Greek sources, other Hellenistic sources support θρησκεία being modified by an objective genitive.136 Significant in this respect is Origen, Cels. 5.6, which portrays Celsus as accusing Jews of “worshiping [σέβουσι, sebousi] the heaven and the angels who dwell therein” and “treating with great respect” [θρησκύνειν, thrēskynein] “demonic-like beings” who appear in visions or dreams (see ANF 4:545). Significant also is the Pre. Pet. frag. 4a, quoted by Clement of Alexandria (Misc. 5), saying that the Jews “worshiped angels and archangels, the month and the moon,” in conjunction with not holding faithfully to the holidays of the “Sabbath, . . . new moon, . . . nor the feast” (cf. here Col. 2:18 with 2:16).137 Even more relevant is an early Christian section of Sib. Or. 8.380–94 (probably early 2nd cent., no later than 300 AD), where people treat idols as “divine . . . [and] sing their praises over dumb idols . . . with supplications and unholy worship [θρησκεία]”; they offer “the smell of sacrifice filling the feast; . . . they . . . burn offering on altars” and “offer libations”; they sacrifice “unto dead demons, as to heavenly beings [οὐρανίοισιν, ouranioisin], performing godless and destructive worship [θρησκεία].” Here we have idol worship in the context of a temple (or “altar”) together with cultic rituals, and this worship is offered “as to heavenly beings [angels].” In response, God says, “As if they for my honors deemed these things all useful” (Sib. Or. 8.383), but “I have no need of your burnt offerings, nor your libations, or polluted smoke” (8.390–91, trans. Evans 2009).

Less frequent is θρησκεία modified by a subjective genitive, occurring primarily in Josephus (Ant. 8.229, “worship by their forefathers”; 12.253, “worship by the Jews”; 16.115, “the worship by the whole nation”; J.W. 2.198, “worship by men”); yet also 4 Macc. 5:7 (“worship [religion] by the Jews”). Arnold (1995: 91) also cites Chariton, Chaer. 7.6.6 (“worship by the Barbarians”). While acknowledging objective genitive uses, Francis (1975b: 180) appeals to Josephus (Ant. 12.253) and 4 Macc. 5:7 for the viability of a subjective genitive and thus contends that only the context of Col. 2:18 can determine which is the case.

2:20. In verse 20 some scribes (ℵ(*), 2, 0278c, 6, 326, 365, 614, 629, 630, 1505, ar, m, vgmss, syh; Ambr, Spec) inserted a “therefore” (οὖν, oun) before “you died,” expressing explicitly the scribes’ view that verse 20 is drawing a conclusion based on verses 16 and 18.

2:21. Anything or anyone unclean could not enter into the temple complex. It was the priests’ task to keep whatever was unclean out of the temple (2 Chron. 29:5, 15–19). To do this the priests themselves were to “touch [ἅπτω, haptō] nothing unclean” but “purify themselves” (Isa. 52:11; the first part is quoted in 2 Cor. 6:17). Qumran repeatedly affirms that no unclean person or thing is to enter the temple: 11QTa 45.7–18, esp. line 10, “They are not to enter My temple while unclean, for that would defile it”; 46.1–3, 9–12; 47.3–7, “My name and temp[le] there must be holy and pure from anything that is in any way unclean by which one might be defiled. Everything inside it must be pure: wine, oil, edibles, . . . all must be pure”; 47.13–18; 1Q20 12.4, “They are not to enter My temple while unclean, for that would defile it”; CD 5.6–7, “They also defile the sanctuary, for they do not separate clean from unclean according to the Law”;138 Jub. 3.13. The Mishnah records fastidious rules for keeping the temple pure from any uncleanness. For example, one of the main ways to bring about “the purification of the Temple court” is to continually keep the utensils cleansed with water and to have clean duplicates and triplicates to replace the “unclean” ones (Hag. 3.8). Sacrifices offered “on the new months,” on “the festivals,” and on “the Day of Atonement” “effected atonement for a clean person who has eaten something unclean,” and all such sacrifices “are offered up to effect atonement for imparting uncleanness to the sanctuary and its Holy Things” (Shevu. 1.4–5); “awareness of uncleanness” was inextricably linked to awareness of being in the sanctuary or “holy things” that were part of it (Shevu. 2.1, 3). Among the transgressions subject to extirpation in the Torah is “coming to the sanctuary when unclean” (Ker. 1.1); eating “unclean foods” or drinking “unclean liquids” and “entering the sanctuary” requires certain measures to become clean (Ker. 3.3); he who is unclean “is forbidden to enter the sanctuary” (Parah 11.4).139 For other Mishnaic texts pertaining to rules for keeping the temple pure from uncleanness, see Eruv. 10.15; Sotah 3.4A–C; Mak. 3.2A; Ker. 1.1H; 2.4E; Kelim 1.8. For the notion in Philo that the temple is to be kept from uncleanness, see Cher. 1.96; Mos. 2.90, 138, 152, 231; Spec. 1.100, 118–19, 270; 3.130. 2:22. That Isa. 29:13 LXX (“the commandments and teachings of men”) concerns the sin of idol worship is apparent from several observations. Isaiah 29:13 is part of a pericope that begins with an allusion to Isa. 6:9–10 (29:9–10, “Blind yourselves and be blind; . . . the LORD . . . has shut your eyes”), which is a literary taunt against Israel, mocking them because they were becoming like the idols that they worshiped (just as the idols had eyes and ears but could not see or hear, so the Israelites had eyes and ears but could not spiritually see or hear; on which, see Beale 1991; see also Ps. 135:15–18). Isaiah 29:9–13 views Isa. 6 to be commencing fulfillment. Israel’s “removing their heart far from” God and “worship” of God “by teaching the commandments and teachings of men” (29:13 LXX) was, thus, part of their idolatry. When Israel is described as “far” from God elsewhere in the OT, they are “far” in the sense that they are “worshiping” idols and not God (Jer. 2:5; Ezek. 44:10); when they are said to worship in “vain” (Isa. 29:13 LXX), sometimes it is when they are worshiping idols (Isa. 41:29). Isaiah 30:1–5 explains that part of Israel’s sin was to trust in Egypt’s help against another nation that was about to invade Israel. In particular, Isa. 30:1 MT says the Israelites “pour out a drink offering” (NASB marg.), but not to God (rather, implicitly, to an idol to seal a political pact); Isa. 30:3 also says that Israel sought “refuge in the safety of Pharaoh.”* This involved some degree of compromise with idol worship, since Pharaoh claimed to be God, the incarnation of the sun-god; the divine kingship of Pharaoh was a cornerstone of Egyptian religious beliefs. Also, Isa. 30:22 explicitly refers to Israel’s “graven images” and “molten images,” which Israel worshiped apparently in some connection with its alliance with Egypt; so also Isa. 31:7, “Every man will cast away his silver idols and his gold idols, which your sinful hands have made for you as a sin”; cf. with 31:1–6 in relation to Egypt. Thus Isa. 29:13, to which Paul alludes, is about idolatry,141 and he employs these words to characterize the false teaching as idol worship.

Comparable to Col. 2, Paul says in Phil. 3:2: “Beware of the dogs, beware of the evil workers, beware of the false circumcision.” And then in 3:18–19 he calls them “enemies of the cross of Christ, whose end is destruction, whose god is their belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who set their minds on earthly things.” Paul’s statement that the unbelieving Jews have their “belly” as their “god” is most probably a reference to their preoccupation with human traditions concerning all kinds of cleansings associated with eating. Commitment to such traditions, versus trust in Christ, is idolatry.

2:23. The syntactical difficulty of this verse is notorious. There are three major views.

    The ἐστίν (estin) could go with “having” (ἔχοντα, echonta),142 as we have preferred in the commentary discussion: “Such things are having, to be sure, a reputation [or appearance] of wisdom in self-made worship and self-abasement in neglect of the body, but are of no value with respect to satisfying the flesh”* (so likewise, e.g., NASB, TNIV).143 The problem with this view is that μέν (men) is usually placed as the second word at the beginning of the sentence, and it is also unusual to place it between the finite verb and participle.
    Or, the ἐστίν could go with the final prepositional phrase of the verse, as, e.g., the NET renders it: “even though they have the appearance of wisdom with their self-imposed worship and [false] humility achieved by an unsparing treatment of the body—a wisdom with no true value—they in reality result in fleshly indulgence.” The phrase “a wisdom with no true value” modifies the directly preceding subordinate concessive clause (beginning with “even though”), and the main clause (ἅτινά ἐστιν, hatina estin, lit., which things are, rendered by the NET as “they in reality”) resumes with the prepositional clause concluding the sentence, “result in fleshly indulgence” (the NET’s rendering of πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός, pros plēsmonēn tēs sarkos); Hollenbach (1979) argues for this view in the most depth. This rendering presents a problem: it is awkward to have two subordinate clauses intervening between a main verb and its complement. The awkwardness is compounded by having the second subordinate clause subordinate to the first one (for these critiques of the first two translation options, see Pao 2012: 197).
    A variant of this second view connects the main clause (ἅτινά ἐστιν, they are) to the very end of the verse, which begins with “of no value against gratification of the flesh,” preceded by a concessive clause: “While they have a semblance of wisdom in rigor of devotion and self-abasement [and] severity to the body, they are of no value against gratification of the flesh” (NABRE; argued for in some detail by Pao 2012: 197). The difficulty with this option is similar to that of the preceding view: it is difficult to have a long subordinate clause interrupting the flow of the main verb with its complement. Thus the distance between ἐστίν and either οὐκ (ouk) etc. or πρός (pros) etc. renders the second two options weaker (Harris: 2010: 115).

Any of these options are viable, but the first has fewer problems, reads more straightforwardly, and lines up with the majority of commentators and English translations.

D. Believers Should Seek Heavenly Things and Not Old Earthly Things (3:1–4)
III. 	Letter Body: Christians Should Strive for Maturity (1:24–4:6)
   	C. 	Believers Should Not Be Required to Submit to Extraordinary Religious Practices (2:16–23)
   	➤ D. 	Believers Should Seek Heavenly Things and Not Old Earthly Things (3:1–4)
   	E. 	Believers Must Lay Aside the Sinful Lifestyle of the Old World (3:5–11)

Here is a fuller statement of the main idea of this unit: Believers should seek heavenly things and not old earthly things because they have died to the old world and have been raised with Christ as a new creation in a new heavenly world.

Believers have died with Christ (cf. 2:11–12, 14) to the old world (2:20a). Because saints have died to the old world, they should not be subject to outmoded OT commandments (cultic and civil laws)1 of that old world in order to obtain a closer relationship with God. Faith in and identification with Christ is all they need to be close to God (2:19), since Christ represents the full presence of God in the end-time heavenly temple. They also should not be subject to outdated statutes because these decrees are part of the corrupt world that is fading away and have their origin in human tradition, which is rooted in self-made religion (2:21–23). Therefore, as we have seen, the main point of 2:16–23 is that true believers should not be required to submit to the false teachers’ outmoded and distorted laws in order to experience the tabernacling fullness of God’s presence, since only faith in Christ is necessary for that.

The “therefore” in 3:1 should be paraphrased as “on the basis that you have died with Christ to the old world, so that you must not submit to old-world laws, you have also been ‘raised up with Christ.’” Real identification with Christ’s death necessarily leads to identification with his resurrection, just as Christ’s own death necessarily led to his own resurrection. Identification with Christ’s death released believers from the old world and resulted in believers not needing to submit to the old-world’s laws (2:20–23). Likewise, identification with Christ’s resurrection (3:1a) results in God’s people being able to “seek the things above, where Christ is”* (3:1b). Then 3:2 reiterates this: “Think on the things above, not on the things on earth.”* The “things on earth” include, in context, the old “commandments of the world” in 2:16–23. They are not to think on “the things of the [old] earth” because they “have died” (3:3a). The remainder of verses 3b–4 continues to elaborate on the effects of this death as the basis for “not thinking on the things of the [old] earth.” This death has led to a new “life hidden with Christ in God” (3:3b). Consequently, “when Christ will be manifested, who is your life, then, you also with him will be manifested in glory”* (3:4).

Thus the exhortation “to seek the things above” (3:1) and its expansion in verses 2–4 and later in verses 5–10 is a polemic against those who falsely claim to experience heavenly temple visions and exalt themselves in superiority over the rest of the Colossian Christians (see Pao 2012: 204–5, though not specifying a notion of the temple). In this light, it is not surprising to continue to see echoes of a focus on the true heavenly temple in 3:1–11.

The main point highlighted in verses 1–4 is to “think on the things above.” Everything else in verses 1–4 in one way or another supports that point. This first paragraph of Col. 3 continues the Christ-centered theme begun in 1:14–23, reiterated in 1:24–2:5, and intensified in 2:6–15 (see D. Moo 2008: 243, on 3:1–2 picking up especially the imperative to focus on Christ in 2:6 and the implied imperatives in 2:7).

Exegesis and Exposition

1Therefore if you have been raised up with Christ, keep seeking the things above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God. 2Set your mind on the things above, not on the things that are on earth. 3For you have died and your life is hidden with Christ in God. 4When Christ, who is our life, is revealed, then you also will be revealed with Him in glory.
[3:1]

The “therefore” (οὖν, oun) at the beginning of the verse refers back to the main point of 2:16–23: that Christians should not be required to submit to the false teachers’ outmoded and distorted laws from the old world because they have died with Christ to that old world. This main point of the preceding segment is the basis for the rest of 3:1. As the conditional particle εἰ (ei) introduces “You have died with Christ” in 2:20, so likewise the same particle introduces “You have been raised up with Christ” in 3:1. As in 2:20, the particle εἰ introduces a first-class condition, where the first clause is assumed to be true for the sake of the argument. This is not, however, a hypothetical or theoretical assertion. Indeed, Paul assumes the reality of the first clause, “You have been raised up with Christ,” so that “seeking the things above” is a necessary result (however, Paul thinks not of all in the church but of the majority; see further on 1:23). This assumption that the Colossians have really been “raised up with Christ” in the past is apparent from the context, where 3:3 says, “For you have died and your life is hidden with Christ in God” (likewise Col. 2:12–13, “Having been buried with Him, . . . you were also raised up with Him. . . . [God] made you alive together with Him”). And Paul writes the same thing in Romans (6:4–5, 8–10, 13).

Like the “if” in 2:20 (see my comments there), the “if” here shows that Paul is not only stating that the Colossians have “been raised with Christ,” but he is also bidding them to consider whether they have truly been raised with Christ (by faith) and to reflect on the consequences for their existence (see my comments on 2:20).

Some commentators think that the notion of already having been “raised with Christ” is an overrealized eschatology and reveals that Paul is not the author of Colossians, since in the so-called authentic letters Paul purportedly always sees the believers’ resurrection to be future. We have discussed this problem at 2:12 (see my comments there). There we saw that Paul does, indeed, express the concept that believers have already been raised with Christ in this age. Then 3:1–4 shows that their resurrection has begun spiritually in this age and will be consummated physically at Christ’s final coming2 (esp. cf. v. 4; so likewise D. Moo 2008: 245–46). So there is an “already and not yet” in Colossians, as elsewhere in Paul.

The first statement, “You have been raised up with Christ,” should result in believers “seeking the things above.” The second part of the conditional clause (the apodosis) is a present imperative. This is a classic indicative-imperative pattern: because the Colossians have come into union with Christ’s resurrection, they now have resurrection power to obey God’s commands (“keep seeking the things above”). “Resurrection” and “death” (in v. 3) are not figurative metaphors for spiritual realities but refer to a real holistic death and resurrection in Christ.3 In this respect, believers’ spiritual death has occurred and will be followed by physical death: a real redemptive-historical spiritual resurrection has been inaugurated and will be completed at the eschaton in physical resurrection. Thus they are not merely like beings resurrected from the dead; they really are such beings.

Thus Paul commands them to “seek the things above.”4 “Above” (ἄνω, anō) is often used with “heaven” (οὐρανός, ouranos) in the LXX (so ten times, as in Exod. 20:4 and Deut. 4:39, usually rendered “the heaven above”) and occasionally in the NT (Acts 2:19). In this light and in view of the immediate context, the word here designates “heaven.” They are to “seek the things above” in heaven because their “hope [is] laid up . . . in heaven” (1:5), Christ’s reconciliation of them has affected even “heaven” (1:20), and their “Lord,” Christ, has his dwelling “in heaven”* (4:1).5

The heaven above is “where Christ is, seated6 at the right hand of God,” and his followers, who are united with Christ and have been “raised up with Christ,” have also been “seated with him in the heavenlies”* (Eph. 2:6). For this reason, they should “seek the things above,” where their true and ultimate home is, that is, where their “mother” is (Gal. 4:26, “the Jerusalem above [ἄνω, anō] is free,” which “is our mother”) and their true country (Phil. 3:20, “our citizenship is in heaven, from . . . which we eagerly await . . . Christ”).7

The reference to Christ “seated at the right hand of God” is an allusion to Ps. 110:1: “Sit at my right hand.”8 This allusion often occurs elsewhere in the NT, where it refers to the exalted position of Christ in heaven, as a result of the resurrection.9 In fact, Ps. 110:1 is referred to more in the NT than any other OT passage (the passage is referred to twenty-three times; see Hay 1973: 163–64). Psalm 110:1 says, “The LORD says to my Lord: ‘Sit at my right hand, until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet.’” The first phrase, “The LORD says to my Lord,” appears to indicate that the king being addressed is a divine king. At the very least, David refers to a coming king who is greater than him, since he calls this king “my Lord” (Adonai, not Yahweh or Elohim). This points strongly to the original messianic nature of the psalm, and Jesus interprets it this way (Mark 12:35–37, where Jesus also assumes that the psalm is written/spoken by David, not just about David).

Later Judaism applied the text to pious individuals or the Messiah (for use in Judaism, see Hay 1973: 21–33). Psalm 110:1 can be read to mean that the Messiah will be given a place of honor beside God’s throne, as some rabbis understood it; but the uses elsewhere in the NT and here in Colossians are better understood to mean that Jesus “participates in the unique divine sovereignty over all things” (Bauckham 2008: 21–22). Thus it is likely that “my Lord” is on the same divine functional level as “the LORD” (God) at the beginning of Ps. 110:1.

Psalm 110 later refers to “my Lord” in verse 1 as “a priest forever” (110:4) also in reiteration of verse 1, “the Lord is at Your right hand” in 110:5. Hebrews 1:3; 8:1; and 10:11–13, following this link in Ps. 110:1, 4–5, connect Jesus as a “priest” (only implied in Heb. 1:3) with “sitting at the right hand” of God (Heb. 8:1–2 places this in the heavenly “sanctuary and in the true tabernacle”).10 In this connection Hebrews quotes Ps. 110:4 seven times and applies it to Jesus as the everlasting priest (5:6, 10; 6:20; 7:3, 11, 17, 21). Romans 8:34 also portrays Jesus “at the right hand of God” (cf. Ps. 110:1) as an “interceding” priestly figure. In addition, the phrase “in holy array” (בְּהַדְרֵי־קֹדֶשׁ, bəhadrê-qōdeš) in Ps. 110:3 MT, which describes the clothing of the king’s people who serve him, refers elsewhere in the OT to those bringing praise or offerings in the temple.11 In the light of previous references to the temple and heaven in the epistle, could the psalm’s priestly links, especially with “the one seated at God’s right hand,” be in the peripheral vision of Paul at this point in Colossians? A positive answer to this question is pointed to by recalling that God’s heavenly throne (clearly implied in Ps. 110:112 and Col. 3:1) is in the heavenly temple (e.g., among numerous references, see Ps. 11:4; Isa. 6:1; Ezek. 1:26; 10:1; Rev. 7:15; 8:3; 16:17; 1 En. 14.18–19; 3 En. 1.12).

Psalm 110:1 is also used elsewhere in the NT to underscore the exalted position of Christ or believers or to highlight the subjection of all powers (including evil ones) to Christ.13 The idea of the Messiah defeating enemies may also be included in Paul’s reference to the psalm, since such an idea is found in the second part of Ps. 110:1 itself and in verse 2, as well as in verses 5–7. The psalm’s application to Christ’s exalted position over all angels (Eph. 1:20–22; Heb. 1:3–6, 13–14; Pol. Phil. 2.1),14 including evil ones, is particularly pertinent here. Accordingly, the false teachers are guilty of worshiping angels (2:18), and such worship likely includes a deceptive practice of revering evil angelic powers, since such powers are mentioned as having been defeated and subjected to Christ (Col. 2:10, 15; cf. also Eph. 6:10–12). The false teachers are saying that believers need a heavenly experience of seeing or worshiping (with?) angels in the temple, but Paul counters this by saying they already have all the heavenly experience they need through their union with the exalted Christ in heaven. Furthermore, why should they worship heavenly powers that are evil and have been defeated by Christ?

Beetham (2008: 224) summarizes the main thrust of the Psalm as follows: “Psalm 110 portrays a Davidic priest-king through whom YHWH will extend from Zion his dominion over all nations.” The point of Ps. 110:1 is not merely the prophesied achievement of a sovereign position of rule (“Sit at my right hand”); the future focus is on the beginning fulfillment in achieving that rule (“until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet”). Psalm 110:2 expands further on this inaugurated rule: “The LORD will stretch forth Your strong scepter from Zion, saying, ‘Rule in the midst of Your enemies.’” This fits perfectly into the broad NT notion that Christ commenced his messianic rule during his ministry, death, resurrection, and exaltation. Colossians 3:1 depicts Christ as the beginning fulfillment of Ps. 110:1.

The notion of an inaugurated rule is also appropriate within the immediate context of Colossians. In 2:10 Paul says that Christ “is the head over all rule and authority”; similarly in 2:15, “When He [Christ] had disarmed the rulers and authorities, He made a public display of them, having triumphed over them through it [the cross]” (NASB marg.). The Ps. 110:1 allusion underscores Christ’s exalted position that has been given to him as a result of winning the decisive victory over evil angelic powers, which is also how the psalm verse is applied in 1 Cor. 15:25; Eph. 1:20; 1 Pet. 3:22; and possibly Rev. 3:21 (cf. 1 Clem. 36.5; Pol. Phil. 2.1; see O’Brien 1982: 162). Although the crucial battle has been won at Christ’s cross and resurrection, the enemy is not yet completely defeated. In Col. 1:20 Paul says that God has designed “to reconcile all things [whether things on earth or things in heaven] to Himself, as a result of having made peace through the blood of His [Christ’s] cross.” This complete reconciliation has not yet been accomplished by the time Paul writes Colossians, which is clear from observing that he needs to warn the readers against the detrimental influence of false teachers (2:8, 18–23), who are part of the “authority of darkness” (1:13 NASB marg.) and through whom the unseen demonic “rulers and authorities” (2:15) still work, even though they have been decisively defeated.

The Colossians are to focus on Christ’s exalted position (“Keep seeking the things above”) and to want to be identified with their exalted Lord in order continually to be related to their “head,” Christ (2:19). The “seeking” is a desire to have one’s thinking and lifestyle continually oriented around Christ’s kingship over all things (similarly, see the use of ζητέω, zēteō, in Matt. 6:33; John 5:44; 7:18; Rom. 2:7; cf. Phil. 2:21; 1 Thess. 2:4–6; cf. Phil. 3:19–20).15

Commentators sometimes speak conceptually of a vertical (or spatial) and horizontal (or temporal) eschatology in Col. 3:1 (cf. O’Brien 1982: 161, 168; Sumney 2008: 182; Harris 2010: 119). That believers have been “raised with Christ” in the heavenly realm “above,” where Christ is seated and ruling, indicates a horizontal inaugurated eschatology: the end-time kingdom and resurrection have penetrated back into history at the cross and resurrection. But this also entails a vertical eschatology. The end-time kingdom has not come in its new-creational and material fullness but penetrated back into the old creation and exists in the invisible dimension of “heaven” or the sphere “above.”16 This is not old-earth topography but indicates merely an invisible realm, which will become visible at the consummation.
[3:2]

The command to seek “the things above,” the main point of verse 1, is now specifically explained as “think on the things above, [but do not set your mind] on the things on earth.”17 For Paul, “thinking” is inextricably linked to the way one lives life (O’Brien 1982: 163). To seek and set the mind on “the things above” is to seek and focus on not “submitting yourself to decrees” of the false teachers (2:16–18, 21) that are “of the [old] world” (2:19–20); consequently, such decrees are “destined for corruption”* (2:22) and thus cannot satisfy the desire for ultimate fulfillment because they have their origin in human-made religion (2:23).18

As contended earlier, Paul wants to assert that the readers have all they need of the “fullness” of Christ’s presence in the heavenly sanctuary. They have this “fullness” since they already participate in heaven by being “in Christ” (1:2), who resides in heaven (see on 2:18). The false teachers are contending that their approach to get to the heavenly temple is something that can be added to their faith in order to enhance the fullness of God’s presence. But Paul says that if you are in Christ, you have already ascended to heaven’s temple in him, since one cannot be in the true heavenly sanctuary without being in Christ. Lincoln (2000: 638) well observes that the false philosophy starts from an earthly viewpoint, from which by their own efforts (e.g., asceticism) they attempt to transcend their own bodily existence and rise into the heavenly dimension (2:18). Paul starts from the reverse direction: from the resurrected Christ in heaven and his fullness (2:9–10; 3:1–2), which “works itself out in earthly life” (Lincoln 2000: 638; see 3:5–17).
[3:3]

Verses 3–4 state that the reason (γάρ, gar) saints should seek and focus on heavenly and not old-earthly things is because they “have died” to the old world. This repeats 2:20: “You have died with Christ to the elements of the [old] world.” The result of “having died” is that they have also been raised with Christ” (3:1), so that their true life (“your life”) is in union with Christ’s resurrection life. Resurrection life with Christ, as in verse 1, is now offered again here in verse 3b as a reason why they should seek heavenly things and also as a basis for not seeking earthly things (v. 2). Their “life has been hidden with Christ’s” new life and hidden “in God.” Specifically, we saw that applying the Ps. 110:1 prophecy to Christ means that being at God’s right hand (on his throne) shows Christ participating in the unique rule of God over everything. Therefore, those whose “life is hidden with Christ,” who is divine, are also included within the sphere of God’s presence (ἐν τῷ θεῷ, en tō theō, being a dative of sphere, as in 1 Thess. 1:1; 2 Thess. 1:1; 1 John 4:15–16; and Jude 1).

Some think the “hiddenness” refers to protection of believers during the time before their final manifestation in Christ.19 While there is no mention of persecution in the letter, there is certainly danger from false teaching. So perhaps the idea may be spiritual protection from deviant doctrine. But the only words for “hiddenness” elsewhere in the epistle refer to an apocalyptic concealment followed by revelation (1:26; 2:3). Furthermore, this is the typical meaning of κρύπτω (kryptō) and κρυπτός (kryptos) elsewhere in the NT.20 In this light, it is more likely that the same notion with the same term is to be found in Col. 3:3. That this is the idea in 3:3–4 is especially confirmed by noticing that the same combination of a form of κρύπτω (ἀποκρύπτω, apokryptō) and φανερόω (phaneroō, in v. 4) occurs in 1:26, expressing God’s revelation of something previously hidden (see a similar idea in 1 Cor. 2:7–10 and Eph. 3:3–5, 9).

It is possible that the believers’ “life hidden with Christ in God” is an echo of Ps. 26:4–5 LXX (27:4–5 MT, Eng.), where David says that he “will earnestly seek” to “dwell in the house of the LORD” throughout his “life.”21 The goal of wanting to be in the temple is “that I should see the beauty of the LORD.” He will seek this “because . . . God has hidden him in his tabernacle” and because God has “exalted”* him. This is the only place among many uses of κρύπτω (kryptō) and κρυπτός (kryptos) in the LXX where a believer’s “life” is said to be “hidden with God”22 in an invisible sacred space while the believer is still living amid earthly vicissitudes. And, just as in Col. 3:1–3, this hiddenness is given as the reason for why the saint “seeks”23 God in the temple. Also, as in Col. 3:1, the saint has been “exalted . . . over his enemies” in the spiritual tabernacle (Ps. 26:5b–6 LXX [27:5b–6 MT, Eng.]; note here the full context of the Ps. 110:1 allusion in Col. 3:1).24 And finally, just as Col. 3:4 concludes with an eschatological hope of future “life,” so does Ps. 26:13–14 LXX (27:13–14 MT, Eng.): “I believe that I shall behold the good things of the LORD in the land of the living.”*25

The related verb κατακρύπτω (katakryptō) is also used only once out of a number of occurrences with reference to the very same notion found in Ps. 26:5 LXX (27:5 MT, Eng.) of saints “hidden” with God in the invisible “hidden”26 space of the “tabernacle,” while still living in the physical presence of those who cause “trouble” and who speak in “contradictory tongues”:27 Ps. 30:21 LXX (31:21 MT; 31:20 Eng.) says, “You will hide them in the hidden place of your presence from the trouble of men; you will shelter them in the tabernacle from the contradiction of tongues.”28

In addition to Paul alluding to the notion of an apocalyptic hiddenness in the heavenly temple from the LXX of Pss. 26 (27) and 30 (31), the notion of protection is included, since that is also a major idea in both psalms.

Accordingly, the major point in Col. 3:3 is that believers must not live by sight but rather by faith in their resurrected identity with Christ in the heavenly temple. They must continue to trust that their “hope has been laid up . . . in heaven” (1:5), where their “inheritance” is (1:12–13), even though they cannot see it or feel it in the present. Such trust will protect them from false teaching. This is in contrast to the false teachers, who live by sight, the “visions . . . of angels” (2:18) and the concomitant emotions inextricably linked to such visions. The mention of Christ four times (in addition to the pronoun “Him”) in 3:1–4 has been observed by several commentators (e.g., Sumney 2008: 183). But why is this repetition significant? The repetition likely shows that Paul wants to emphasize the centrality of Christ as the heavenly focus in contrast to the false teachers’ focus on angelic powers.
[3:4]

This verse continues to elaborate on the “hidden” resurrection “life” of believers with Christ, which continues to explain why they should seek heaven and not the old earth. Believers are “hidden with Christ” (v. 3) because he himself is hidden, since he is in the unseen heavenly dimension (v. 1). Nevertheless, “he will be manifested”* in the future. Just as the prophesied but “hidden mystery” of Christ was manifested in an inaugurated manner at his first coming (Eph. 3:8–11; Col. 1:26), so who Christ is in his present hiddenness will be revealed at his final coming. At this time, it will be even more clearly revealed that Christ is the repository of “all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (2:3), which had formerly been “hidden” during the interadvent age in him (except for those with believing eyes).

Also, at this final manifestation of Christ, believers “will be manifested with him in glory.”* Is this the glory of God, of Christ, or of believers? (cf. Harris 2010: 123). Since the believers’ life (“your life”) is completely bound up with Christ’s life in the hidden heavenly sphere, “glory” likely refers to Christ’s glory, in the sphere of which believers find themselves at the eschaton. This is a further explanation of Christ being “the hope of glory” (1:27; so likewise Rom. 5:2: “exult in hope of the glory of God”). Since believers have died with Christ, have been raised with Christ, and will be revealed with Christ, their life can be described wholly as “Christ” being “your life.” They have no life apart from their identification with him, a point Paul makes elsewhere: “To live is Christ” (Phil. 1:21; so also Gal. 2:20; following O’Brien 1982: 167).

Christ being the believers’ “life” may be repeated twice in verses 3–4 to anticipate Christ being “all things and in all [every believer]” in verse 11, which underscores that in Christ there no longer are Jew or Gentile distinctions, but Christ is equally present in every believer regardless of ethnicity. This declaration further associates 3:3–4 with the “hidden mystery” of 1:26–27, where the “mystery among the Gentiles” was “Christ in you, the hope of glory.” In 1:26–27 we saw that the “hidden mystery” entails Christ being equally present with Gentile saints as with Jewish Christians. In this way in 3:3–4, 11 the focus is also on Christians becoming part of the “hidden mystery” first expressed in Christ in 1:26–27,29 and it will be even more fully revealed at the eschaton how thoroughly Jews and Gentiles are on the same footing in Christ, the true Israel and last Adam.

Verse 4 is probably a reference to the final glorious resurrection of Christians on analogy with Phil. 3:19–21, where Christ will “transform the body” of those who have their “citizenship in heaven” (in contrast with those “who set their minds on earthly things”) and transform it “into conformity with the body of his glory” on the last day. This is the focus of “the hope laid up . . . in heaven” (Col. 1:5) and of the “inheritance of the saints” (Col. 1:12). That saints “will be manifested in glory with him [Christ]” probably refers to Christ’s glory in the midst of which they will be and which they will reflect (on analogy with the Phil. 3:19–21 parallel and 1 Cor. 15:43 in relation to 15:44–49). In this sense, “in glory” is a possession of Christ’s followers, since they share in it.30 Colossians 3:4 focuses on the “not yet” of eschatology, while verses 1–3 are centered on the “already” or beginning of eschatology (likewise O’Brien 1982: 170).
