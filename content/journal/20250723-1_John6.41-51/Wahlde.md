6:25-50
A Discourse on the Witness of Scripture to Jesus as the True Bread

25 And finding him across the sea, they said to him, “Rabbi, when did you get here?” 26 Jesus responded and said to them, “Amen, amen, I say to you, you seek me not because you saw signs but because you ate of the loaves and were filled. 27 Do not work for the bread that perishes but for the bread that remains unto eternal life, that the Son of Man will give you. For this is the one on whom the Father, God, has put his seal of approval.” 28 They said to him, “What should we do, to do the works of God?” 29 Jesus responded and said to them, “This is the work of God, that you believe in the one whom he sent.”

30 They said to him, “What sign do you perform so that we may see and believe in you? What do you work? 31 Our fathers ate the manna in the desert, as it is written, ‘He gave them bread from heaven to eat.’ ” 32 So Jesus said to them, “Amen, amen, I say to you, it was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread from heaven. 33 For the bread of God is that coming down from heaven and giving life to the world.”

34 Therefore, they said to him, “Sir, give us this bread always.” 35 Jesus said to them, “I am the bread of life. The one who comes to me will not hunger, and the one believing in me will never thirst. 36 But I said to you that you have seen [me] and you do not believe. 37 Everything the Father gives me comes to me, and that coming to me I will not cast out 38 because I have come down from heaven not to do my will but the will of the one who sent me. 39 This is the will of the one who sent me, that I lose nothing of that he has given me but that I raise it up on the last day. 40 For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who sees the Son and believes in him may have eternal life, and I myself will raise the person up on the last day.”

41 So the Jews murmured about him because he said, “I am the bread come down from heaven,” 42 and they said, “Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? How does he say now ‘I have come down from heaven’?” 43 Jesus responded and said to them, “Stop murmuring among yourselves. 44 No one is able to come to me unless the Father who sent me draws the person, and I will raise that person up on the last day. 45 It is written in the prophets ‘And all will be taught by God.’ Everyone listening to the Father and learning comes to me. 46 Not that anyone has seen the Father except the one who is from the Father — this one has seen the Father. 47 Amen, Amen, I say to you, the one believing has eternal life. 48 I am the bread of life. 49 Your fathers ate the manna in the desert and died. 50 This is the bread come down from heaven so that someone might eat of it and not die.”
NOTES TO 6:25-50

V. 27 perishes This can refer to either of two things. It may refer to the “earthly” quality of the bread that they ate at the time of the previous day’s feeding. That food would in fact perish and would not be like the food that is Jesus, the Bread from Heaven. Thus, it would be an encouragement to seek spiritual versus material bread. Jesus as the Bread from Heaven has not been introduced yet, but this would not be an obstacle since such anticipatory references have appeared earlier (e.g., 4:7, 10-15)

It could also be seen as an allusion to the manna that was given to the Israelites each morning in the desert. The manna that had not been gathered by the Israelites melted in the sun (Exod 16:21). Again, the point would be a contrast between the Bread of Heaven and the manna. This too would be an anticipatory reference. It may not be possible to determine which is intended; but, in either case, the contrast involves a temporary bread that was a gift from God and was useful but that will now be superseded by the Bread from Heaven.

the Son of Man will give Here and in vv. 51-58, where Jesus is identified as the Son of Man, it is not the Father who gives the bread (cf. v. 32) but the Son of Man himself. See the analysis of the Composition.

God Here “God” appears in apposition to Father. This is the only instance in the New Testament of this.

has put his seal of approval (Gk: esphragisen) The image is taken from the act of sealing with a signet ring (sphragis).

V. 28 what should we do, to do The two verbs translated “do” here are different verbs in Greek. The first is from the Greek poieō (“to do, make”); the second is from ergazomai (“to work”). The second of these verbs continues a catchword connection with v. 27 where Jesus said: “Do not work for (ergazesthe mē) the bread … ,” which is then taken up by the somewhat redundant: “What should we do (poiōmen), to work the works (ergazōmetha ta erga) of God?” This is not a question about doing the works of the Law but an example of the idiomatic expression found in apocalyptic meaning “to do the will of,” or “to do what is characteristic of.” (The expression appears also in 8:39, 41. See the further discussion in the analysis of the Composition and in 3E-9.)

V. 29 This is the work … that you believe This is often treated as the Johannine answer to the relation between faith and works. It is not. The confusion arises from the presence of the idiomatic expression in v. 28. See below.

V. 30 sign The usage of “sign” here is the same as in 2:18 and is typical of the second edition. It is something demanded by the opponents and it is not granted by Jesus.

V. 31 manna The food miraculously given to the Israelites in the desert (cf. Exod 16:1-36 and Num 11:1-9). As Moloney points out (Signs 46, 62; Gospel 207-26), for the Jews manna was a common symbol for the Law. Thus, it would be common for a first-century Jew to think of the “better” manna as the gift of the Law through Moses.

Perhaps more significant is that there is evidence from the early second century of a Jewish belief that in the last days Yahweh would again feed his people with manna. Examples of this include 2 Bar 29:8 and also Mekilta on Exod 16:25; Eccl. Rabb. 1:9. Although 2 Baruch is from the early part of the second century, the rabbinic documents in their present form come from a period later than that of the Gospel. Nevertheless, in a case such as this, it is reasonable to conclude that such beliefs existed during the time of Jesus. Thus, Jesus would be presented as this eschatological manna that they had been expecting.

as it is written What follows is not an exact quotation of any single text in the Old Testament. It contains elements of Exod 16:4, 15; Ps 78:24; Wis 16:20. This text and its introductory formula are discussed more fully in Volume 3, Appendix 1 (Quotations of the Old Testament).

bread from heaven This is the same phrase used in Exod 16:4 (LXX) for the manna in the desert: artous ek tou ouranou, except that in Exod, “bread” is in the plural (“breads”/“loaves”). When Jesus applies the phrase to himself in v. 32, he will add the word “true” (alēthinon), thus contrasting the prefigurement with the fulfillment.

V. 32 it was not Moses The exegesis that Jesus gives of the Scripture text presupposes that Moses was the subject of the verb “give.” Yet in none of the proposed texts is Moses actually the subject. The text reflects an awareness in Jewish exegesis of the text as applying to Moses. Moses does appear as the one who performs the miracle in some later Jewish traditions (texts and discussion in Menken, Quotations 52-65).

true bread from heaven This is the term that is applied to Jesus as the true, genuine bread from heaven. See v. 31 above.

V. 33 that which is The Greek reads ho katabainōn, which can agree grammatically either with arton (“bread” v. 33) or with egō (“I” v. 35). It seems best to have the pronoun refer to what precedes and then to understand v. 35 as a clarification of the misunderstanding.

V. 34 Sir, give us this bread always This request is verbally quite similar to that of the Samaritan woman in 4:15. In both cases, the statements are from the second edition and cause a misunderstanding.

V. 35 I am the bread of life This is the first of the “I am” statements with a metaphorical predicate within the Gospel. Just as “the Jews” believed that they found life in the Law (cf. 5:39) and that the manna was a symbol of that Law, Jesus now proposes that he is the bread that gives life, thus he surpasses the Law and becomes the true source of life.

hunger … thirst There has been no mention of drinking up to this point in the discourse. But within vv. 51-58, there are repeated references to drinking Jesus’ blood (see vv. 53, 54, 55, 56). The various possible reasons for the statement are explored in the discussion of the Composition and in the Interpretation.

V. 36 I said This does not refer to a specific instance in the Gospel. There are similar references in 8:25 and in 10:25, both of which function in the same way. All are probably intended as rhetorical devices. See the Interpretation below.

V. 37 Everything the Father gives me This is the first of several instances of a peculiar expression involving the neuter singular of pan (everything) with some form of the verb didōmi (giving) in a way that indicates believers are “given” to Jesus by the Father. The expression appears here and in v. 39 and also in 17:2. It is clear from the context that, in spite of the unusual and impersonal neuter, the word refers to those persons who believe in him. This is particularly clear in 17:2, where the neuter is used with the verb didōmi and then the plural personal autois (to them) is used for those to whom Jesus gives eternal life. Functionally identical with this are the instances such as 3:27 (“A person cannot gain anything unless it is given from heaven”) and 6:65 (“No one is able to come to me unless it be given to him from the Father”).

Zerwick (Biblical Greek §31) proposes that this is due to influence of the Hebrew kol de (“all that which”). This would then stress the collective aspect of those who have been given.

Another, similar expression is also used in the Gospel not to refer to those persons who are given but to those prerogatives or powers given to Jesus by the Father (e.g., 3:35; 10:29; 13:3; [cf. 5:20-22; 16:15; 17:2, 7, 10]). The two ideas occur side by side in 17:2, where because he has been given power over all “flesh” he gives eternal life to “all” whom the Father has given him.

Also related to these statements (but from a different author) are those in which Jesus says that he has “chosen” his disciples (6:70; 13:18; 15:16, 19). (For a discussion of these, see at 6:70.)

While, if taken alone, these passages would seem to imply a kind of determinism, they are to be understood as one aspect of a reality the other side of which is the conviction that belief is the free choice of the individual. Thus, the Gospel makes it clear that unbelief is also the result of choice. The Johannine authors express both sides without defining the relationship more specifically. While some persons are “given,” those people also choose belief. The element of predestination seems particularly strong in the present chapter (e.g., cf. 6:37-40, 44, 64b-65). Nevertheless, in the Gospel as a whole, there are statements that indicate the responsibility of the believer in accepting or rejecting Jesus.

A similar portrayal of human choice is found in the SDQ (cf. 1QS 3:21-23 but compare with 1QS 2:26–3:6). See also Brown, “Scrolls,” 151-55. That these two aspects of human choice do not come from separate authors is indicated by the way other documents in the New Testament (e.g., Romans 9) that are from a single author treat the same issue by affirmation of both aspects.

I will not cast out The Greek (ekballō egō) is the same phrase as is used for the casting out of the man born blind in 9:34.

V. 39 This is the will This verse is a doublet of v. 40 with some important theological differences. See the discussion of the Composition and the Interpretation.

that I lose nothing of that The Greek uses the neuter singular for the objects as was the case in v. 37. Moreover, the sentence begins with a nominativus pendens (hanging nominative). This is thought to reflect Semitic influence (see Zerwick, Biblical Greek §25-31). A literal translation is difficult: “… in order that, regarding everything he has given me, I not lose anything of it….” The most likely explanation for the use of the neuter is given in the Note to v. 37.

but that I raise it up This is the first of four affirmations of bodily resurrection in the discourse.

on the last day This expression in the singular appears only in the Gospel of John and appears five times (6:39, 40, 44, 54; 12:48) although the plural is found elsewhere in the New Testament. In 1 John 2:18, the expression “the last hour” appears twice.

V. 40 everyone who sees the Son This is the only instance of “seeing” the Son being said to lead to the possession of eternal life. It means a full interior seeing and is an equivalent of “believing” in the Son.

I myself In contrast to the similar expression in v. 39 (where the pronoun is not expressed apart from the inflection of the verb form), here the pronoun “I” (egō) is expressed and placed in emphatic position. See also the Note on the variation of the expression used in v. 44 below.

V. 41 the Jews This is the first time the group Jesus is speaking with is identified since v. 24, where “the crowd” was said to be embarking in boats to come to Capernaum to find Jesus.

V. 42 the son of Joseph Jesus had also been identified as the son of Joseph in 1:45.

V. 44 and I will raise This is the third grammatical variation of the Greek expression. In v. 39, the subject was communicated only by the inflected verb form; in v. 40, it was expressed by the addition of the pronoun placed in an emphatic position. Here, the pronoun is expressed but in a non-emphatic position.

V. 45 It is written This is a free quotation of Isa 54:13, but it is difficult to tell whether it is from the LXX or MT. The quotation forms the subordinate Scripture quotation common in the format of the typical synagogue homily as described by Borgen. The source of the quotation and its introductory formula are discussed in more detail in Volume 3, Appendix 1 (Quotations of the Old Testament).

V. 47 the one believing has eternal life Again (cf. 3:36) the possession of eternal life is looked upon as a present possession from the point of view of the time of the Gospel. The expression is elliptical: the one believing will receive the Spirit and the Spirit will be the principle of eternal life within the person.

V. 49 Your fathers ate the manna … and died This is the first mention of “eating” since the initial quotation. What follows will develop this part of the citation.

V. 50 may eat of it and not die This does not deny the reality of physical death but is another way of expressing the conviction that the one who has eternal life will not die “forever.”
THE COMPOSITION OF 6:25-50

The composition and arrangement of vv. 25-50 are complex. The passage calls for detailed analysis of the compositional history as is typical of such material in the Gospel. As part of this analysis and because the passage speaks regularly of “bread from heaven,” we need to determine whether all of these texts were intended to refer to the preexistence of Jesus. Furthermore, vv. 30-50 are arranged according to the format of a synagogue homily and so it will be important to say something about that also. Finally, the structure of the discourse also shares many features in common with the discourses of chapter 8 and chapter 10, and it will be important to discuss those features. All of this means that the treatment of vv. 25-50 will be extensive.

Verses 25-29 continue the transition from the scene of the multiplication to Capernaum where Jesus will address them in the synagogue.1

V. 25 (2E) Verse 25 intends to bring the crowd that witnessed the feeding together with Jesus and to establish the context for the dialogue that follows. The verse has no specific identifying characteristics but is most likely from the author of the second edition, the one who was responsible for the first version of the transition to the discourse. In any event, the verse is not crucial for the understanding of the overall thought of the section.

Vv. 26-29 (3E) When the crowd and Jesus meet across the lake, a new exchange begins. The presence of a literary seam after v. 25 is indicated by the considerable shift in, and mixing of, characteristics in the verses that follow. First, there is the use of “signs,” a term otherwise characteristic of the first edition (1E-2). Jesus tells the people they seek him “not because they have seen signs.” Yet this is precisely what they have done— they have seen “signs” at least in the physical sense as used in the first edition. Second, while this use of the term “signs” is elsewhere characteristic of the first author, here not only is it lacking the positive sense that it generally carries,2 but it is “mixed” with other features that are typical of the third edition, suggesting the work of the third author in such literary seams (3E-21).3

Third, we notice three features that appear only here and in vv. 51-58 within the discourse. (1) Jesus identifies himself as “the Son of Man” (v. 27) (3E-14). This term appears also in vv. 51-58 but nowhere else in the discourse. (2) Jesus urges the people to work for another bread that the Son of Man will give them. Only here and in vv. 51-58 is it said that Jesus will give the bread; elsewhere he is the bread. (3) The tense of the verb is in the future here and in vv. 51-58 but is in the present elsewhere in the discourse.4 Thus, we see that the material contrasts with the remainder of the discourse (which we will soon see comes from the second edition). We would then expect features that identify it with the third edition and we are not disappointed.

We have already seen two features that identify the material: (1) the mixing of terms in literary seams (3E-21); (2) the title Son of Man (3E-14). In addition, (3) the fact that the Son of Man is “giving” the bread here as he is said to do in the later (sacramental) portion in vv. 51-58 suggests a Eucharistic (sacramental) theme is intended here in preparation for the fuller development in vv. 51-58 (3E-41).5

There is one final feature that also links the verses with the third edition. In vv. 28-29, the crowd asks “What should we do to ‘do the works’ of God?” Jesus responds that the “work” of God is that they should believe in the one whom he sent. Here, the expression “to do the works of” is not used either by the crowd or by Jesus to refer to miracles (as the word is used in the second edition) or to works of the Law (as was common in Judaism). Rather, the expression is the idiomatic one found in apocalyptic dualism, an expression that has the much simpler meaning of “to do the will of” (3E-3; 3E-9). This is a feature paralleled in the SDQ and the T12P. Thus, we see that there is considerable evidence that the verses come from the third edition.

Vv. 30-50 (without vv. 38-39, 40d, 44c, 46) (2E) With v. 30, we come to the actual discourse that is the centerpiece of the chapter. Aside from the brief additions that will be noted separately, vv. 30-50 come from the second edition of the Gospel. There are numerous indications this is so. The material is identified, first, by the primary linguistic criterion, the presence of “the Jews” in the hostile sense in v. 41 (2E-1). Moreover, they reflect the unanimity of opinion typical of this group (2E-9). However, it would appear that “the Jews” here refer to a group that was earlier identified as the common people (cf. 6:22, 24, 25). This (and the related example in v. 52) are the only instances where “the Jews” would seem to be identified with the common people rather than the authorities. This is also the only instance of “the Jews” being present in Galilee. Is this an instance of the term referring to a larger group than the circle of religious authorities? There are several peculiarities of the text that need to be assessed before an opinion can be ventured.

First, from the discussion of the Composition of the verses, it is evident that the sequence between vv. 22-25 and v. 30 has been broken by an addition by the third author in vv. 26-29. We do not know how much, if any, of the original material has been lost, but, from the other factors, it would seem that at least some material has been omitted. Although we cannot be sure what the original sequence was, at the very least we can be sure that we do not have it. Moreover, in v. 59, we find out that the discourse of vv. 30-50 has taken place in the synagogue at Capernaum. Yet, in the present state of the text, there has been no indication that either Jesus or his listeners have moved from the place where they met immediately after arriving. This strongly indicates that some significant disruption of the text has taken place due to editing by the third author. Given this evidence that the text has undergone such extensive disruption between v. 24 and v. 30, it would be risky at the least to argue that the second author intended to identify “the Jews” in 6:42 with the crowd.

More importantly, as we have had occasion to note before, in what follows throughout chapters 6 to 10, the author of the second edition has joined discourses of his own creation to narratives that come from the first edition. In each of the discourses, the people identified as being in dialogue with Jesus are identified as “the Jews.” Furthermore, we have seen indications that this association of discourse with narrative has as its purpose the presentation and elaboration of each of the essential witnesses before “the Jews,” a group that is to be identified as the representative of official Jewish religious belief. This pattern is clear throughout the chapters, including here where the narrative of 6:1-21 is joined (by means of 6:22-29) to the discourse of 6:30-50, which elaborates the witness of Scripture to Jesus. The fact that Jesus urges belief based on the witness of Scripture is also typical of the second edition, which has a much wider basis for belief than just the “signs” of the first edition (2E-14). Finally, it should be noted that “the Jews” are presented as in dialogue with Jesus, as is typical of the second edition (2E-11).

Consequently, it is evident that the present state of the text does not reflect the original order at the time of the second edition even though the overriding pattern of the combination of a narrative plus discourse remains. We cannot say whether the second author neglected the narrative consistency of having “the Jews” be present only in Judea and of being contrasted with the common people in order to achieve his overriding intention or whether the order of the text was disrupted by the third author.6 Given these various possibilities as well as the consistent portrayal elsewhere, it is unlikely that either the second or third authors intended to indicate by the portrayal here that “the Jews” (in the hostile sense) referred to both common people and authorities.

In addition to the use of “the Jews,” there are several other indications that the primary material of the discourse comes from the second edition. Another linguistic criterion is the use, in v. 30, of “sign” in the pejorative sense as a demand for an apologetic proof (as at 2:18). That this use of “sign” is to be distinguished from the use in the first edition is evident from four features of its use: (1) the sign is demanded by the authorities rather than freely given by Jesus; (2) Jesus does not give a sign in the sense that the term is used elsewhere in the Gospel; (3) it is followed by a misunderstanding (2E-13); (4) the interlocutors are identified as “the Jews” rather than “the Pharisees.” All these features indicate this use is not from the first edition but from the second (2E-3). In addition, the demand for a sign is an example of the lack of concern for, or attention to, the actual miracles such as the multiplication that had just taken place (2E-7). It is also significant that the crowd seems to slip into the more common language of the second edition when it asks, “What do you work?” (Ti ergazē;), using the verbal form of the characteristic term for miracles in the second edition (2E-2; 2E-4).

Also characteristic of the second edition is the literary technique of misunderstanding (2E-13; cf. 2E-31F). Moreover, the statement: “Sir, give us this bread always” closely parallels the statement of the second edition in 4:15. Jesus’ use of “I am” with a symbolic predicate (e.g., “bread of life”) is a literary device found repeatedly in the second (and taken over by the third) edition.7

Fifth, in the introduction of the primary Scripture citation (v. 31), we find the introductory formula typical of the second edition (2E-32F). The same is true of the introduction to the secondary scripture citation (v. 45) (2E-32F).

A sixth indication of its authorship is that, as we shall see in greater detail in the Interpretation, the entirety of vv. 30-50 represents the offering to, and the rejection by, “the Jews” of the witness of Scripture to Jesus. In this respect, the discourse parallels those other central discourses of the second edition that appear in 8:12-59 and 10:22-39 and which clearly come from the second edition.8

A seventh indication the material comes from the second edition is the overall format of the discourse, which reflects the pattern of an exegetical homily on Scripture, a sophisticated feature (2E-33F) that undoubtedly reflects the situation of the later community rather than the circumstances of the historical ministry (2E-31F).

Finally, as we shall see below, vv. 51-58 can be clearly identified as coming from the third edition. Since vv. 51-58 build on the earlier part of the discourse, we have another indication that the material of the main body comes from the second edition.9

Vv. 38-39 (3E) The wording of these verses and that of v. 40 is very similar. The verses are so repetitious that it is unlikely that all three verses come from the same author. Because the identity of these verses is closely connected with the question whether the words “coming down from heaven” (v. 38) imply the preexistence of Jesus, I have decided to treat the verses in an Addendum below, in connection with the discussion whether all references to “bread from heaven” and “coming down from heaven” in the discourse imply preexistence. It will be the conclusion there that both v. 38 and v. 39 come from the third edition.

In any event, it is quite certain that the last clause of v. 39 is from the third edition. This clause, which speaks of Jesus raising the person up on the last day, is significant not only because of the apocalyptic worldview it reflects (3E-16), but also because it indicates a belief in bodily resurrection (3E-37), because it describes Jesus as having the power in himself to raise these up (3E-26:2), and because it represents the first of a series of bundled references to “raising up on the last day” (cf. 6:40c, 44c) (3E-57F). All of these features indicate that this clause at the very least comes from the third edition.

Vv. 40abc (2E), 40d and 44c (3E) As is explained in the Addendum, v. 40abc comes from the second edition. The last clause of v. 40 and of v. 44 (as well as of v. 39) reads “…I will raise the person up on the last day.” Like the similar clause in v. 39, vv. 40d, 44c are also considered by many scholars to be additions to the verses where they occur.10 The literary evidence that they have been added is the awkwardness that their presence creates. Together with the instance in v. 39, they represent another instance of the “bundling” of features found only in the third edition (3E-57F; cf. also 6:54). Of course, the clear references to bodily resurrection (3E-37) and final eschatology (3E-16) reflect important elements of belief in the third edition, and also, like v. 39, they reflect the more exalted Christology of the third edition (3E-26:2).

V. 46 (3E) This sentence comes from the third author. In the third edition, there are repeated references to the fact that no one has seen the Father except Jesus. Such “seeing” of the Father is intimately related to the preexistence of Jesus, to the fact that Jesus had been with the Father and had descended from the Father and so is able to witness to what he saw (3E-28; 3E-55F). Elsewhere in the Gospel, such references to the fact that only Jesus could see the Father appear in material that is from the third edition (1:18; 3:11, 32; 8:38, 57).

When viewed in this light, the purpose of the verse seems much clearer. In the second edition, Jesus had repeatedly said that he spoke what he heard from the Father. Hearing the Father would not require preexistence since the prophets and others heard the voice of God. Indeed, in the preceding verse (6:45), Jesus has said that anyone listening to the voice of God and learning will come to me. Verse 46, then, seeks to make it clear that hearing the voice of God is not the same as seeing God. All have access to the word of God but only the preexistent Jesus could see him. Consequently, although there is no obvious literary aporia suggesting that it is an insertion, on the basis of the theological intent of the verse, it is likely that v. 46 is an insertion by the third author.
THE INTERPRETATION OF 6:25-50
Preliminary Comments

The crowds now meet Jesus across the lake. A brief but important exchange leads into a major discourse by Jesus, which, we are told later (v. 59), has taken place in the synagogue at Capernaum. A key to properly understanding the discourse as it appeared in the second edition, as well as its form in the final edition of the Gospel, is the discovery by P. Borgen (Bread) that the discourse itself is cast in the form of a synagogue homily as found in Philo and the Palestinian Midrashim. In order to appreciate fully the value of recognizing this form, we will pause briefly to say something of the format before continuing.

The form of the homily is illustrated in the chart on page 302, which arranges the text of the discourse according to the various elements of the homiletic format.

The homily begins with a quotation from Scripture. In the Johannine homily, this is the modified quotation of Exod 16:15 (“He gave them bread from heaven”) now put on the lips of “the Jews” (v. 31). The initial quote is then reinterpreted by the homilist in the form of a paraphrase. This takes place in v. 32, where Jesus explains that the one who gives is not Moses but the Father and that the Father gives the “true bread” not just “manna.”

The homily then comments on each part of the quotation. In chapter 6, this is the exegesis of “bread from heaven” (vv. 33-36); exegesis of “the Father gives” (vv. 37-40) and eventually “to eat” (vv. 49-50).

It was common in such homilies for the homilist to introduce hypothetical objections to the thesis being proposed. This takes place in vv. 41-44 where “the Jews” object to the claim that Jesus is “coming down from heaven.”

e. Objection to Thesis Re: Coming Down from Heaven

41 So the Jews murmured about him because he said, “I am the bread come down from heaven,” 42 and they said, “Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose mother and father we know? How does he say now, ‘I have come down from heaven’?” 43 Jesus responded and said to them, “Do not murmur among yourselves. 44 No one is able to come to me unless the Father who sent me draws the person, and I will raise the person up on the last day.

f. Subordinate Quotation of Scripture

45 “It is written in the prophets, ‘And all will be taught by God.’ Everyone listening to the Father and learning comes to me. 46 Not that anyone has seen the Father except the one who is from the Father — this one has seen the Father. 47 Amen, amen, I say to you, the one believing has eternal life. 48 I am the bread of life.

g. Exegesis of: “To Eat” and Concluding Paraphrase of the Original Quotation (Conclusion of the Material of the Second Edition)

49 “Your fathers ate the manna in the desert and died. 50 This is the bread come down from heaven so that someone might eat of it and not die.12

As can be seen, in the Gospel the homily has been “historicized” and so what would ordinarily be only the words of the homilist are cast as an exchange between Jesus and his listeners. In this exchange, it is Jesus’ listeners who provide the quotation from Scripture (v. 31) and it is he who provides the paraphrase. Moreover, what would be a hypothetical objection (vv. 41-44) in the homily is a real objection by Jesus’ listeners.

Typically, in such a homily, a subordinate quotation is introduced at some point to support the original interpretation. In chapter 6, this is a quotation of Isaiah 54:13 (“And all will be taught by God”), which appears in v. 45 and which is developed in vv. 46-48.

Finally, there are an exegesis of the last part of the original Scripture quotation “to eat” (v. 49) and the conclusion (v. 50), which consists of a repetition of the original Scripture showing the meaningfulness of the new paraphrase.

With this homiletic pattern in mind, it should be evident that, by the end of v. 50, all of the elements of the original Scripture passage have been commented on. This means that the homily could end in v. 50 without doing violence to the “form” of the homily. As we shall see, there is considerable terminological, ideological, literary and theological evidence that the third author has extended the discussion of the last element in order to draw out his own implications of the discourse. This takes place in vv. 51-58 and will be discussed in the following Section.
In the Second Edition
Vv. 30-50 (without vv. 38-39, 40d, 44c, 46)

These verses make up the body of the Bread of Life discourse and the body of the homily in the second edition.
a. Quotation of Scripture by “the Jews” (v. 31)13

Without receiving an answer to the question of how Jesus got to Capernaum and without a reference to the fact that they have witnessed the miraculous feeding, the Jews demand a sign from Jesus so that they might see and believe in him (v. 30).14 They say: “Our Fathers ate the manna in the desert.” They then present a quotation from Scripture to support their position (v. 31). At this point, the transition begins to the format of the exegetical homily. Because the presentation in the Johannine Gospel is a “historicized” form of the homily, the presentation of the quotation is made by the the Jews rather than by the homilist.

The quotation the crowd presents is: “He gave them bread from heaven.” As is discussed in the Note on v. 31 and in Volume 3, Appendix 1 (Quotations of the Old Testament), the exact source of the quotation cannot be determined although close approximations have been discovered. It will be the reinterpretation of this citation that will be the object of Jesus’ words in the remainder of the discourse. However, before proceeding to that paraphrase, it is helpful to recall that the gift of manna at the time of the Exodus and through the hands of Moses was looked upon by Jews as a prototype of events that would take place also in the final days. In 2 Baruch 29:8 (written ca. A.D. 100), the author states: “The treasury of manna will come down again from on high, and they will eat of it in those years.”15 This thought is also echoed in the later Midrashim (Eccl. Rabbah 1:9): “As the first redeemer caused manna to descend, as it is stated, ‘Because I shall cause bread to rain from heaven for you (Exod 16:4),’ so will the later redeemer cause manna to descend.” Mekilta on Exod 15:26 states: “You will not find it (manna) in this age, but you shall find it in the age that is coming.”16 Thus, it is very likely that Jesus’ listeners would have held a hope, related to the hope of a Prophet like Moses, that in the final days the nation would receive another gift of manna similar to that first at the Exodus. This forms the context for the Exodus text and for Jesus’ reinterpretation of it.17
b. Interpretive Paraphrase of Scripture (v. 32)

As was typical in such a synagogue homily, the speaker does not allow the text to remain simply a record of the past but rephrases and reinterprets it in order to apply it to his present listeners with an eschatological meaning. And so Jesus says, “It was not Moses who gave you bread from heaven, but my Father who gives you the true bread from heaven.” When this is interpreted by Jesus, it is to be apparent that the gift of manna in the past should now be seen to be superseded by the gift of Jesus who is the true bread. Jesus is the eschatological gift of God and is the source of eternal life.

But if it is a contrast between Jesus and manna that is the focus of the comparison, why did the author choose to emphasize that it was not “Moses” but “the Father” who gave the manna?18 It may be that the author of the second edition makes this change in order to provide two comparisons between what was given through Moses and what was given through Jesus. In 6:30-50, Jesus, the living bread, is compared with the manna (that Moses gave); and, in 7:37-39, the living waters that will come forth from the side of Jesus are compared with the water that Moses brought forth from the rock in the desert. Thus, it would seem that while it is most accurate to say that in the present text, the primary comparison is between Jesus and the manna, a broader comparison with the first of two gifts associated with Moses in the desert is also evident.

It is this theme of Jesus as the true bread from heaven that the homily will develop. The homily will do so in three steps: (1) an interpretation of “bread”; (2) an interpretation of “the Father gives”; and (3) an interpretation of “to eat.” Throughout the homily, Jesus will be described in terms that are reminiscent of Wisdom (cf. Prov 9:5; Sir 24:21).
c. Exegesis of: “Bread from Heaven” (vv. 33-36)

Jesus now proceeds to provide his interpretation of “the bread from heaven.” Like the manna, Jesus is “bread”; like the manna, he is from heaven; unlike the manna that gave physical life, Jesus will give eternal life. As is often the case in the second edition, the crowd misunderstands the words of Jesus, taking them on an earthly, physical level rather than on a spiritual level. As a result, they respond enthusiastically, “Sir, give us this bread always,” just as the Samaritan woman had requested “physical” water in 4:15. While the woman’s comment reflected her lack of understanding, it did not reflect the hostility that is evident in the reaction of “the Jews” in 6:41.

The Jews often understood manna as a symbol of the Torah, and the Gospel has told us that “the Jews” had searched the Scriptures because they hoped to find life in them (cf. 5:39).19 There was also a Jewish hope for an eschatological manna, as we have seen. But now the gift of God has become perfect in the true bread that is Jesus, who, like the manna, has come down from heaven. The manna had given life (i.e., sustained it) in the desert, but the bread that is Jesus is of a different sort. He is the source of the life that comes from the Spirit, eternal life.

Jesus then explains that the one who comes to him “will not hunger, and the one believing in me will never thirst.”20 This statement bears considerable similarity to several Old Testament passages. For example, it could well be seen as a comparison of Jesus with Wisdom. In Sir 15:3, Wisdom is described with the image of eating and drinking. Even more pertinent is Sir 24:21, which says: “The one who eats of me will continue to hunger; the one who drinks of me will be thirsty for more.” Prov 9:5 also speaks of gaining wisdom through the image of eating and drinking. Isa 49:10, speaking of the blessings of the eschatological age, says: “They shall not hunger, nor will they thirst.” Isa 65:11-13 also speaks of those who are not faithful to the Lord as going hungry and thirsting. Amos 8:11 speaks of a time when the people will seek God and not find him: “The time is coming, says the Lord God, when I will send forth a famine upon the land; not a famine of bread nor a thirst for water, but for hearing the words of the Lord.” Midrash Tanḥuma (Beshallaḥ 21:66) speaks of wisdom metaphorically and says, “It has been prepared for the righteous in the age to come. Everyone who believes is worthy and eats of it.” Each of these in its own way would have an appropriate application to the context of the discourse of chapter 6.

Twice in these verses (vv. 33, 35) Jesus refers to himself as the source of life. He is the giver of the Spirit that will be the source of eternal life. Whatever images and metaphors may be used to describe this event, it is the gift of life through the Spirit that is central and essential. However, scholars often misinterpret this. Bultmann sees this “life” as the revelation that Jesus brings.21 Brown speaks of it as “… his power to give life through the bread of his teaching and of his flesh, a power he has because he has come down from heaven.” These statements, in my opinion, are not completely to the point. Jesus is like “manna” (a metaphor) but he is in actuality the source of eternal life.22 This latter statement is not metaphorical! He is the source of eternal life in the sense that he is the one who brings the Spirit. Jesus does bring a revelation but, in the second edition, this revelation is limited to the revelation of his own identity and to his promise of the gift of the Spirit to those who believe in him.

In the Bread of Life discourse, Jesus describes his own identity by the image of bread. He offers people the bread come down from heaven. And Jesus is the bread. He also offers people “living water” (i.e., cf. 4:10-15; 7:37-39) but he is not the water. His identity is symbolized by “bread”; his gift is symbolized by “water.”

Although doing so provides a good parallel with the Jewish understanding of manna as symbol of the Law, to equate the bread with a “teaching” or “message” or to parallel it with “the Law” misses the central point of the second edition, namely, that it is the Spirit that will give knowledge and life. When they receive the Spirit, believers will have no need for anyone to teach them (1 John 2:27).

Nevertheless, to refer to Jesus in the categories of Jewish hopes remains appropriate. The Jews saw the manna as a symbol of the Law, which gave life. For the author of the second edition, manna had been the source of physical life; Jesus was the source of eternal life. As is the case in “misunderstandings” throughout the Gospel, the source of the misunderstanding is to understand Jesus’ words as referring to something physical (e.g., rebuilding the Temple, physical rebirth, living water as water from a spring, worship in a physical Temple) rather than something spiritual (e.g., the Resurrection of Jesus, spiritual birth from the Spirit, living water as a symbol of the Spirit, worship in Spirit). So here the “misunderstanding” is to expect manna again in the physical sense rather than in the spiritual gift that is Jesus. As Jesus will say in his final comparison with manna (vv. 49-50; cf. v. 58): “Your fathers ate manna in the desert and died. This is the bread come down from heaven so that someone might eat of it and not die.” For the author of the second edition, these misunderstandings consistently demonstrate the failure of various listeners to notice that “The Spirit is what gives life; the flesh is useless.”

It is an offer without parallel that Jesus is making to the listeners, but they do not understand. He now responds to his audience in a way that indicates the hostility that he perceives in them: “You have seen me and do not believe” (v. 36).
d. Exegesis of: “the Father gives” (vv. 37-40)

Jesus now addresses the role of the Father and the notion of giving. It will be noticed that Jesus does not discuss how the Father gives bread but how the Father gives people to Jesus. With the unusual use of the neuter, Jesus speaks of “everything” the Father gives him as coming to him. Jesus is speaking to “the Jews” here and thus explaining that, since they do not believe, they are not “coming” to him. At the same time, the words have such a positive aspect to them that they seem to be directed equally to the believer. The promises Jesus makes are absolute and would be seen as an assurance for the believer who is undergoing persecution. All who are given will come and no one who comes will be rejected — because the will of the Father is that everyone who sees the Son and believes in him may have eternal life. In these statements, Jesus once again affirms his relationship of total dependence upon the Father, not in terms of the Father as source of his power (as in 5:19-30) but in terms of doing the Father’s will. Jesus has come only to do the will of the Father (v. 40abc).
e. Objection to the Thesis Regarding Jesus’ “Father” (vv. 41-44abc)

An objection is now raised against the words of Jesus (vv. 41-42). In a typical synagogue homily, these objections would be hypothetical ones raised by the homilist. Here, because the homily is historicized, they are actual objections raised by his listeners, “the Jews.”

“The Jews” begin by challenging the statement that Jesus is “the bread come down from heaven.” The challenge is based, not on whether or not he could “come down from heaven,” but on the fact that they know Jesus’ real father — Jesus is the son of Joseph. Because they know his father, they know that he cannot have come down from heaven. So their refutation of his claim is indirect.

Jesus, however, responds (v. 43) in words that pick up the words of v. 41 and predicate of “the Jews” the same attitude as was predicated of the Israelites wandering in the desert (when they had received the manna): they murmured against God. But Jesus refutes their objection by implying that “the Jews” have not been given to him by the Father and that is why they do not come to him.
f. Subordinate Quotation of Scripture (vv. 45-48)

As was common in synagogue homilies, at one point the homilist introduces a second quotation of Scripture in order to help establish his main point. Here (v. 45ab), Jesus quotes Isa 54:13 (“And all will be taught by God”) as corroboration of his previous statement. The point of the quotation is that, if they (“the Jews”) listen to God’s teaching, they will come to Jesus (v. 45cd). And then the “payoff” is reached in v. 47: the one believing has eternal life.23 And so Jesus makes it clear that everyone has access to the Father and that the Father desires that all come to the Son. He then (v. 48) summarizes by restating his central affirmation: “I am the bread of life.”
g. Exegesis of: “To Eat” and Concluding Paraphrase of the Original Quotation (vv. 49-50)

Jesus begins the conclusion to his homily by recalling the original Scripture and by a final contrast between it and what is being offered now through Jesus: “Your fathers ate the manna in the desert and died. This is the bread come down from heaven so that someone might eat of it and not die.” The reference to “not dying” does not refer to physical life, but to eternal life. The one who believes will have eternal life and so will not die “forever” (cf. 4:14; 8:51-52; 11:26; see also 8:35; 10:28).

It will be noticed that the exegesis of “eating” here in the second edition is understandably brief since the notion of “eating” is to be understood symbolically and refers to the acceptance of Jesus in faith, and this has been treated already.
In the Third Edition
Vv. 26-29

We now return to see the changes that the author of the third edition has made within this material. The first changes (vv. 26-29) occur in the material that serves as a transition from the journey across the lake to the scene of the homily in the synagogue. Verses 26-29 are remarkable both for the content of the changes but also for the consistency of language and theology that confirms these changes.

In a technique that is not unlike that used by the author of the second edition, the third author has Jesus begin to speak but not to address directly the question posed by the crowd in the second edition. The crowd seeks him not because they “see” signs but because of the physical nourishment they have received. But then, in a kind of catchword chain, Jesus urges them to work not for bread that perishes (i.e., the nourishment they now seek) but for “the bread that remains unto eternal life” “that the Son of Man will give you.” As we have seen detailed in the homily as it appeared in the second edition, starting from the interpretive paraphrase, it is the Father who gives and the Son is himself the bread. But here (and in vv. 51-58) the author of the third edition introduces an important (and obvious) change: it is not the Father who gives; rather it is the Son of Man who gives and so the bread is a gift of the Son — and as we will learn in vv. 51-58, this bread is the flesh of the Son of Man and is his blood.

Jesus then reminds his listeners that God (the Father) has “put his seal” upon the Son of Man. The crowd then, taking up Jesus’ mention of God, asks what they should do in order to do “the works of God.” This phrase (often interpreted in the context of the later theological disputes about the relation between “faith and works”) is simply the dualistic expression found often at Qumran (and typical of the third edition) and which means “What are we to do in order to do what is pleasing to God?” Jesus, continuing with the catchword “work,” responds by saying simply: “this is the work (i.e., ‘will’) of God, that you believe in the one he has sent.”

The effect of the third author’s work here is to prepare his reader to view the discourse within the framework of apocalyptic. The first-century reader, familiar with the dualistic worldview of apocalyptic, would recognize that Jesus is implicitly presenting the reader with two alternatives. One can believe in Jesus and so “do the works of God” or one can refuse to believe and so “do the works of Satan.” For the third author, the alternatives are clear. And, like the community at the time of 1 John, it is necessary to make a decision.

In addition to this call to decision regarding Jesus, the third author also shows the reader that there is a sacramental dimension to the bread from heaven, and participation in the Eucharist that the community celebrates is essential for life. Here in vv. 26-29, he only hints at the sacramental dimension in his reference to “bread that remains to eternal life” but this continues and reinforces the earlier sacramental allusion from v. 23. By so doing, the third author prepares for his major exposition of the theme in vv. 51-58.24
Additions within the Homily Itself (vv. 38-39, 40d, 44d)
Vv. 38-39

In these verses, the third author affirms once again that Jesus is preexistent, that he will not lose any of those given him by the Father and that, on the last day, he will raise up the one who has believed in him and has physically died. While none of these notions is “new,” the third author’s incorporation of them — and the worldview they imply — indicates just how important they are to a correct understanding of Jesus. Moreover, the first person statement of Jesus that “I myself will raise up …” indicates just how intimately and directly Jesus is involved in raising up the believer bodily on the last day. Thus, in the mind of the third author, it would be wrong to think that eternal life had only a present and spiritual dimension. Although the believer received eternal life in the present, in the future the body will be raised also and eternal life will involve both a spiritual and a bodily dimension. This idea is so important that the author reaffirms his view two more times — in v. 40d and v. 44d.
V. 46

In a final modification, the author responds to the text of the second edition and affirms that, while the people have had access to the word of the Father and were able to listen to him, no one except the Son has actually seen him. The “one who is from the Father” is the only one who has seen the Father. Jesus existed in heaven with the Father and so can be truly said to have seen him. But this is a prerogative limited to Jesus. No one else can make this claim.
THE ROLE OF 6:25-50 IN THE GOSPEL

At the time of the second edition, these verses comprised the discourse that was linked to the narrative complex from the first edition containing the multiplication of the loaves. The verses explained, in the theology of the second edition, how the scripture witnessed to Jesus as the true bread from heaven, given by the Father, for the life of the world. As such it constituted the first complex of narrative and discourse to articulate the individual “witnesses” to Jesus as expounded in the second edition.

At the time of the third edition, the author made changes internal to the discourse and these will be discussed following the analysis of 6:51-59.
Addendum: Which Edition Do Vv. 38-40 Come From? and the Related Question Whether the References to Jesus as “Bread from Heaven” in Chapter 6 Imply Preexistence

As was mentioned in the discussion of the Composition of 6:25-50, it is possible to read all of the references to Jesus as “bread (come down) from heaven” as implying that Jesus has descended from heaven and so also implying his pre-existence. The phrase appears seven times in 6:25-50 (vv. 32 [twice], 33, 38, 41, 42, 50); the following is an analysis of the individual references in their context.

Perhaps the most important element of the context to remember is that the phrase “bread from heaven” (artos ek tou ouranou) is not a Johannine invention. It is the phrase used in the Old Testament to refer to the manna given to the Israelites in the desert. In v. 32 (twice) and in v. 33, Jesus is portrayed as the fulfillment of what was foreshadowed in the desert; and he is described, not simply as “bread from heaven,” but “the true bread from heaven.”

Moreover, in texts speaking of the manna, it was common to refer to the eschatological manna as “coming down” or “descending” from heaven. We see this in 2 Bar 29:8 (“The treasury of manna will come down again from on high”) and in Eccl. Rabbah 1:9 (“As the first redeemer caused manna to descend, as it is stated, ‘Because I shall cause bread to rain from heaven for you’ [Exod 16:4]”). As a result it would not have been unusual for first-century Jews to speak of Jesus as “the true bread” in the sense of “the eschatological bread,” and it would not have been thought unusual to speak of this as bread “coming down from heaven.” In that context, there would be no reason to think that the author intended to imply preexistence by the phrase; it was simply the traditional expression of the idea that the eschatological bread would be given by Yahweh who was in heaven.

Of course, when such a statement was read in a context where other statements clearly spoke of preexistence, it would be very easy to read preexistence into these other texts.25 But for the interpreter it is important to determine as certainly as possible whether those earlier texts did in fact refer to preexistence. In the light of what was said above, I would conclude that unless there are features that explicitly link such statements with preexistence, they should be understood in what might be called “the traditional way,” that is, the way they would ordinarily be understood by a first-century Jew.

Applying this criterion to the instances in 6:25-50, I would conclude that, in v. 32 (twice) and in v. 33, the phrase is simply intended to echo the language and imagery of the original Scripture and so to identify Jesus as the eschatological bread.

I would judge this to be the case in vv. 41-42 also, but the case here is a bit more complicated. The author of v. 41 intends to show how “the Jews” misunderstand (2E-13) Jesus. But what are they misunderstanding? This misunderstanding is like all misunderstandings in the second edition, namely, an understanding of something on the material level when it is in fact intended to be understood on the spiritual level. Jesus is using the metaphor from Scripture to express the reality that he has been sent by the Father; he is like the “bread sent down from heaven” that Moses gave them, only better. But “the Jews” confuse the spiritual meaning of the phrase (i.e., that Jesus is the fulfillment of the manna tradition foreshadowed by the gift in the desert) with their knowledge of Jesus’ physical origins. This is the same as the misunderstanding of Nicodemus who thinks Jesus is speaking of bodily rebirth when he is not. And it is the same as the misunderstanding of the Samaritan woman who thinks Jesus is offering her real, physical “living water” when he is not. So “the Jews” of the second edition think that Jesus really is claiming to have descended “physically” from heaven. This misunderstanding is completely consistent with the theology and literary techniques of the second author.26 Thus the meaning of the statement in the second edition had nothing to do with preexistence or with “physical” descent from heaven.

I would judge the instance in v. 50 to have the same meaning. That instance occurs in the conclusion of the original homily and forms part of the repetition of the original quotation, and so it is most likely that instance also refers to the traditional meaning.

But what of the occurrence in v. 38? I am inclined to think that here the statement “I have come down from heaven not to do, etc.” does refer to Jesus’ preexistence with the Father.27 There are three features that suggest this is so. First, this is the only instance where Jesus speaks of himself in the first person as having come down from heaven. The sense of the statement seems to require a literal meaning here. This statement also seems to echo the descent/ascent schema that is characteristic of the third edition (3E-34) that presumes preexistence.

Second, when we look at the context of v. 38, we find that there are three extended statements about Jesus and his doing the will of the Father (vv. 38-40). (Note the italicized sections below.) This results in excessive repetition and is very clumsy. It is surely an indication that the verses have been edited.

Vv. 37-38: Everyone the Father gives me comes to me, and the one who comes to me I will not reject, 38 because I have come down from heaven not to do my will but the will of the one who sent me.

V. 39: This is the will of the one who sent me that I lose none of those he has given me….

V. 40: For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who sees the Son and believes in him may have eternal life.

Verse 38 explains that Jesus seeks to do the will of his Father and v. 39 explains more specifically just what the will of the Father is. But even with those verses accounted for, there is still an unusual amount of repetition. In v. 38 and in v. 40a, the clauses that are so repetitious are both causal clauses. Verse 38 begins with “because” (hoti); v. 40 has the postpositive “For” (gar). Even though the words are different, the meaning is almost identical and their function is the same. Given the presence of the statement “I have come down from heaven” in v. 38, I would propose that v. 38 is the work of the third author and that the verse incorporates his understanding of “from heaven” to refer to preexistence (3E-28; 3E-34). He then introduces v. 39 which specifies that he will not lose any of those given to him (2E?) but will raise them up on the last day (3E-16). Of these features only the second has appeared in the second edition (in 6:37; cf. also 10:29). It seems likely that the author is simply repeating this phrase from v. 37 (2E). Finally, in v. 40abc he resumes the previous context and the original statement as it appeared in the second edition. Here Jesus speaks of himself in the third person and affirms the common (and more general) conviction of the second edition that those who see the Son and believe in him will have eternal life.

The third feature that suggests vv. 38-39 come from the third edition is that when these verses are removed, it becomes apparent that v. 40abc resumes the previous context very well. The major theme of the discourse has been that Jesus is “the bread of life” (v. 35) and that this bread come down from heaven “gives life to the world” (v. 33). Verse 36 had claimed that “the Jews” had “seen” Jesus and had not believed. Then v. 40 resumes not only the general context but also the wording of v. 36. First, the verse refers to “everyone who sees the Son and believes in him.” Secondly, v. 40abc speaks of the specific benefit of seeing and believing as “having eternal life.” At the same time neither of these themes appears in vv. 38-39.

Finally, we see that the third author is concerned to address the issue of the preexistence of Jesus as a theme in v. 46. The emphasis there on preexistence is so clearly an issue that it makes it all the more likely that it was intended as an issue in vv. 38-39 also. All of this indicates that (1) v. 38 refers to the preexistence of Jesus and (2) together with vv. 38-39 comes from the third edition.
Addendum: The Pattern of Argument Common to This Discourse and to the Discourses of Chapters 8 and 10 — and Its Purpose

In addition to the patterning of this discourse according to the exegetical techniques of homiletic Midrash, the discourse also exhibits a number of features of argument that occur not only here but in the discourses of chapter 8 and chapter 10. This pattern of argument is discussed in detail in Volume 3, Appendix 3 (The Structure of Theological Argument). The results of that discussion are summarized here for the light this pattern sheds on the overall thought and intention of the discourse.

At the time of the second edition, in each of the discourses there are: (1) statements by “the Jews” demanding to know who Jesus is. (2) In response, Jesus replies that he has already told them but that they do not believe. He then (3) gives a reason for their unbelief. Jesus then speaks (4) of those who do believe, and (5) promises that he will not lose any of those who believe and (6) that they will have eternal life. These six elements are presented and developed in the discourses of chapters 6, 8 and 10 although there is some fluidity in the manner and in the images used.

Together, the discourses are intended to illustrate the conviction that the failure of “the Jews” to know and believe in Jesus took place in spite of all his attempts to make himself known to them. The second author then explains (for the benefit of the community at the time of its expulsion from the synagogue) that “the Jews” have not been given to him and are not able to hear his word and so can be said to be “not of his sheep.” But then Jesus speaks of those who do believe and gives them the assurance that they will not be lost but will gain eternal life.

When the discourses are viewed in this light, the similarities become apparent and their overall intention is also clearer. At the same time, it remains evident that the second author has, in each case, supported this common argument by appealing to specific “witnesses” to Jesus.

1. Boismard-Lamouille (Évangile 190) propose that at one stage of the tradition (Jean IIA), much of the material now in 6:22-59 existed in a different order and followed the expulsion of the sellers from the Temple (2:14-16) and the demand for a sign (2:18). It concluded with what is now 7:37-39 and took place at the feast of Tabernacles. Then the next stage of development (Jean II-B) saw the placement of the material in its present location. As a result, this second demand for a sign is simply a duplication of that original request. In the third stage of its development (Jean III), additional changes were made, some of which involved the introduction of the Son of Man and references to final eschatology. While I would agree that these latter elements (Son of Man and final eschatology) do belong to the final stage of the tradition’s development, I would see the editing as less complex than that proposed by Boismard. J. Becker (Evangelium 1:201) attributes vv. 22b-23, 27, 39, 40c, 44c, 51c-58 to the Redactor and the remainder of vv. 22-59 to the Evangelist.

2. In 4:48, the phrase “signs and wonders” had appeared (the only time in the Gospel) in material attributed to the third edition. Why that phrase is not used here is unknown. The other features of the material are so consistent that it is difficult to conclude that the verses are the work of anyone other than the author of the third edition.

9. While there are no specific indications that it is an insertion, it is possible that the last clause of v. 35 (“and the one believing in me will never thirst”) is an insertion by the third author. Since Jesus has identified himself as bread, it is appropriate that he speak of “not hungering,” but the addition of “not thirsting” appears only here in the first part of the discourse (vv. 30-50) and this clause seems out of place in a discourse solely on the theme of bread. There are two possible explanations, each attributing the statement to different editions.

It could be argued that the clause is an addition by the author of the third edition. In vv. 51-58, the discourse is given a sacramental interpretation and Jesus as bread is interpreted in terms of “eating his flesh” and “drinking his blood.” That the references to drinking in vv. 53, 54, 55, and 56 are an integral part of vv. 51-58 is clear as is the fact that they refer to drinking the blood of Jesus (in a sacramental sense). Consequently, it is possible that this unexpected mention of “thirst” here in v. 35d is an addition for the sake of completeness in the light of that later mention of flesh and blood in vv. 51-58 (3E-41).

It is also possible that the wording of the verse as a whole is meant to echo texts in the Old Testament that speak of eating and drinking and hungering and thirsting metaphorically as a reference to the blessings of the eschatological period. We will see several such references below in the Interpretation. On the basis of those texts, I am inclined to think that the entire verse is the work of the second author who chose to express himself in this way.
