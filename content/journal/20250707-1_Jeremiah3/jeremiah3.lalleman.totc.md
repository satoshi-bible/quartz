B. Turn, turn, turn! (3:1 – 4:4)
Context

These verses (3:1 – 4:4) belong to Jeremiah’s early days as a prophet, when he urged the people to repent and return to God. The Hebrew word for these two verbs is šûb, which has a variety of meanings (see below) and plays a major part in the book of Jeremiah (see Introduction, p. 37). The verb runs like a thread throughout this chapter up to 4:1–4 (see 4:1 for the same verb). This section is structured as follows:
3:1–5 	Faithlessness of God’s people (poetry)
3:6–11 	Both Israel and Judah have failed (prose)
3:12–13 	Israel invited to repent (poetry)
3:14–18 	Hope for the future (prose)
3:19 – 4:4 	Repentance and forgiveness (poetry)
i. Will God take his people back? (3:1–5)
Context

These verses continue the theme of unfaithfulness from chapter 2. Israel has left her ‘husband’ and served other ‘men’ (gods). She has acted as a prostitute (2:20).
Comment

3:1. A legal case is presented (cf. 2:9) in the style of casuistic laws found in the Torah: ‘If this happens … then …’ The issue is mentioned in Deuteronomy 24:1–4. A divorced woman cannot be taken back by her first husband, says the law. In the case of Israel, the wife here (cf. 2:2), the situation is even worse: the woman has left her husband and made love with many others (see also 2:20, 23–24, 33). Similar language is found in Hosea 1 – 3 with reference to the relationship between God and his people. As noted in the Introduction, Jeremiah may well have known those words and used them in his own way. The question, would you now return to me? at the end of verse 1, should be followed by: ‘Of course, this has become impossible!’ The earlier question (should he return to her again?) implies doubt about God’s willingness to ‘take them back’. This is an intriguing issue in the book of Jeremiah: will the people repent? Will God take them back? In verse 1, the verb šûb is used twice, translated as return. The land is completely defiled by prostitution, either literally or metaphorically (see Jer. 2), a translation of an emphatic use of the same verb in Hebrew. This linguistic device is often used in Jeremiah to emphasize the meaning of a verb. (For sin as a defilement of the land, see 2:7 and the comments there; also 3:2, 9.) The word you (have lived as a prostitute) is stressed, making the accusation clear.

2–3. On verse 2, see also 2:20. Prostitutes looked out for ‘customers’ along the roadside (see Gen. 38:14). A nomad (also translated ‘Arab’) could have been a trader waiting to sell to travellers, or a bandit waiting to rob passers-by. These metaphors indicate that God’s people were actively pursuing evil. ‘Where have you not been lain with?’ (NRSV) is a milder reading suggested by the Masoretes, who found the verb (meaning ‘been ravished’) too shocking. God had set warnings in the form of drought (v. 3; see 14:1), which was a disaster for the harvest. Apparently, the worship of Baal, the fertility god, did not bring rain. Yet this has not made the people think and repent.

4–5. The people still call God My Father and my friend from my youth (see 2:2), intimate names which nevertheless cannot disguise the people’s stubbornness towards their God. They ‘are capable of even more wickedness’ (Lundbom 1999: 303).
Meaning

Will God take his people back after their repeated adultery (literally or metaphorically)? He has the legal right to deny any return. The text does not give an answer here, but leaves the question as a challenge to its hearers: what will happen next? Will God indeed reject his people, his bride from the past (2:2)? How will the ‘love story’ end after so much betrayal?
ii. A tale of two sisters (3:6 – 4:4)
Context

Jeremiah 3:6 seems to be a new beginning. Yet verses 6–11 continue the theme of adultery and divorce from the previous passage. The message of repent/return runs throughout the chapter.

Israel, the northern kingdom, is an example for Judah, used as a kind of ‘mirror’. The result of Israel’s unfaithfulness (exile) should have been a warning to Judah and changed her behaviour, but it did not. Therefore, Judah is even guiltier than her sister Israel.

It is not always easy to distinguish whether Judah or Israel is being addressed. Verse 21 is directed to ‘Israel’, but the intention (vv. 21–25) is that Judah will hear what is said, and repent and return to God.
Comment

6–11. The passage opens with well-known words for introducing a prophecy: the Lord said to me … It is God’s word coming to the prophet, who is then to proclaim the message to the people (see Jer. 1:7, 9). The instruction to go and proclaim follows in verse 12, but we may assume that verses 6–11 were certainly meant to be heard by the people. The prophecy is dated to the early days of Jeremiah’s ministry, during the reign of King Josiah, the period assumed for most of the material in Jeremiah 2 – 6. The word šûb, with a variety of meanings, plays an important role in 3:1 – 4:4. In verse 6, a form of the verb is used as a nickname for the northern kingdom: faithless Israel. She has persistently ‘turned away’ from God by worshipping other gods. This probably also involved prostitution in the context of the fertility cult of Baal (see 2:20; 3:2). God expected her to return (šûb) to him, but she did not (‘return’, as the Hebrew reads in v. 7). The southern kingdom Judah is called her unfaithful sister. When the northern kingdom was sent into exile (here using the metaphor of an adulterous wife sent away with a certificate of divorce; see Deut. 24:1), Judah did not take it to heart, but followed ‘her sister’ in unfaithfulness. In verse 9 TNIV reads, Because Israel’s immorality mattered so little to her (= to Judah). The word ‘Israel’ is absent in Hebrew, so it could also apply to Judah: Judah has been taking her own immorality lightly and serving idols made of stone and wood (see 2:27; 10:3, 8). In doing so, the nation is ‘adulterous’, breaking the covenant with God. The land is defiled by sin (see v. 2; 2:7). Judah returned (šûb) only in pretence (v. 10): literally, in a ‘lie’, a word that often occurs in Jeremiah to indicate deceit, and is also used of prophets who speak ‘falsely’ in God’s name (see 8:10; 23:25–26). The ‘pretended return’ may refer to Josiah’s reform in 622 BC, which did not have a lasting result amongst the people. Verse 11 rounds off the previous verses and introduces the next. The conclusion to the ‘tale of the two sisters’ is that Israel is even more righteous than Judah. This must have been a shock to Judah, which had the house of David and the temple in her midst. After all, the northern kingdom had already been taken into exile by the Assyrians, but the Judeans still lived in the Promised Land.

12–13. The call towards the north: ‘Return, faithless Israel’ (two forms of the verb šûb), is directed to the exiled northern kingdom (in Assyria) or those who remained in the land. It is difficult to imagine how they may have heard these words, but, as with verses 6–11, we can interpret them as an indirect message to Judah. Judah should take on board what God is doing with Israel and draw conclusions regarding her own behaviour. The message is a hopeful one: Return (šûb), which may include a literal, physical return from exile, as well as a ‘return to God’ in terms of repentance. God is faithful, abounding in steadfast love (ḥesed). He will not be angry for ever, if the people will acknowledge their guilt and confess their sins of idolatry.

14–18. The call to Israel is repeated. Two forms of šûb are used: ‘children’ (as the Hebrew reads) who have ‘turned away’ are called to ‘return’. God refers to himself as Israel’s husband: for I (emphasized in Hebrew) am your husband, rather than Baal whom they worshipped (the Hebrew word for ‘to be master or husband’ is bā‘al; see also 31:32). However, God will bring back a remnant, not to the northern places of worship, but to Jerusalem/Zion. The following verses focus on the whole of God’s people, including Judah (and Jerusalem). The unification of Judah and Israel (v. 18) looks forward to an ideal for the future (see Isa. 11:11–13; Ezek. 37:22; Hos. 3:5; Amos 9:11). God promises shepherds (leaders, kings; see Jer. 23:1–4) after [God’s] own heart, like David (1 Sam. 13:14). In contrast with the shepherds in Jeremiah 2:8 and those mentioned in chapter 22, these shepherds will lead with knowledge and understanding. There will be the blessing of fruitfulness (v. 16; cf. Gen. 1:22, 28). The paradisiacal blessing will return, along with those blessings under the covenant that flow from obedience (Deut. 28): the relationship between God and his people will be restored. Jerusalem will be the place where God is enthroned, and there will be no further need for the ark. This vision of the future has an international character: all nations will gather in Jerusalem to honour the name of the LORD (v. 17). The Hebrew does not include the word ‘to honour’, but reads simply: ‘to the name of the LORD in Jerusalem’. He will be the centre of worship for all the earth. Sin and idolatry will belong to the past. For similar future promises, see Isaiah 2:1–5; 11:9–10; Micah 4:1–5; Zechariah 8:20–23 and, more specifically for Israel and Judah, see Jeremiah 30 – 33. For stubbornness of their evil hearts, see also 7:24; 9:14; 11:8; 13:10; 16:12; 23:17. This persistent, stubborn defiance of God’s will comes to an end, even amongst the nations (v. 17).

19–20. In a sort of lament, God utters his disappointment at Israel’s response to his love and care: I myself said – I is emphasized in Hebrew, and said can be translated as ‘thought by myself’. Commentators vary in their interpretations of what follows. The TNIV and NRSV suggest that God would have liked to treat the Israelites like children and give them a pleasant land. Lundbom (1999: 318) translates: ‘How will I treat you among the children? I will give you a fine land …’, explaining that ‘among the children’ means among the nations. Holladay (1986: 122) interprets the sentence: How (gladly) God treats them like sons, so he gave them an inheritance, the land. The argument here is that the suffixes are female: so God gave ‘her’ (you) an inheritance. This was unusual, as women did not normally inherit. We cannot say that God wished to give the land (as TNIV, NIV and NRSV seem to suggest), because the land has already been given. So taking verse 19 as a whole, we can interpret it as meaning that God treated the people like sons in giving them the land, but ‘how he wished they had responded and called him “Father”’. The relationship has been very disappointing (in accordance with Craigie, Kelley and Drinkard 1991: 64). In verse 20 the marriage metaphor is resumed (see 3:1–5). Israel has left her ‘husband’ for other gods. (For the land as inheritance, see e.g. 2:7; 16:18; 17:4.)

21–25. It is as if the prophet hears a penitential song coming from the northern kingdom in exile (v. 21), which again may have been meant to be heard by Judah. The barren heights were where the people practised idolatry (see 2:20; 3:2). God answers in verse 22a and receives a further penitential answer in verses 22b–25. God’s call to the people to return (v. 22a; return and faithless are both forms of šûb) also includes a promise to cure them of backsliding (lit. of their ‘faithlessnesses’, plural; again a form of šûb). Normally we speak of illnesses being cured and sins forgiven, but here we find a mixture (see also Hos.14:4). The people are ‘ill’, ‘wounded’ (see 8:21–22; 30:12–13, 15) and in need of healing. They need to undergo a thorough change and be healed from their inclination to turn away from God, and it is God who will bring this about. The declaration our God at the end of verse 22 is reminiscent of the covenant formula: ‘I will be your God and you will be my people’ (see Exod. 6:7; Jer. 31:33). The people confess that they were led astray by the idolatry on the mountains (cf. Jer. 2); it was a ‘lie’ (for the Hebrew word used here, see v. 10). The word idolatrous in TNIV is added as an explanation. Idols cannot save; only God can (end of v. 23). The people have trusted in other gods and in treaties with other nations (see Jer. 2), but the result has been disastrous. In verse 24 the people say that from their early beginnings (as individuals, but more probably as a covenant people, from Sinai onwards) they have lost so much which was sacrificed to ‘the Shame’ (i.e. the ‘god of shame’), another name for Baal (shameful gods, TNIV plural). Those who serve the shameful gods will themselves be put to shame (see v. 25, which echoes what has been said in v. 13).

4:1–4. The translations offered by TNIV and NRSV for the first half of verse 4 indicate a difference in interpretation, although both are possible. NRSV repeats, ‘if you return’, whereas TNIV reads, If you … will return … return to me (two forms of the verb šûb). The latter preferred translation has the sense of: ‘If, after everything that has been said before, you decide to return, let it be to me (says God) and not to the idols.’ To return should be a return to God and no-one else, and it should include the removal of ‘abominations’ (i.e. the detestable idols); otherwise, it will make no difference to their current situation. If people use God’s name in making sincere oaths in a truthful, just and righteous way (v. 2), in accordance with the covenant and the nature of God who is ‘truthful, just and righteous’, this will have an effect on the whole world. The nations will invoke blessings, ‘will be blessed’, ‘will bless themselves’ or ‘gain blessings for themselves’ (cf. a similar promise to Abraham in Gen. 12:3; 18:18; 22:18). However, in Jeremiah 4:2 the word him indicates that God is the source of blessing for all nations, and not Abraham. (See also Jer. 9:23–24 for ‘boasting in God’.) Verse 4 is a call for a radical change of life: outward circumcision should match ‘inward’ circumcision, a heart wholly committed to the Lord (see also Deut. 30:6; and Rom. 2:25–29 in the NT). There is a call for the unploughed ground of people’s lives to be broken up and the weeds removed; otherwise judgment will come like a fire (see 7:20; 15:14; 44:6). This message looks very similar to John the Baptist’s sermon in Matthew 3 (esp. vv. 8, 10, 12).
Meaning

The question in 3:1–5 as to whether God will return to his people, and whether they will be allowed to return, has its response in the call that follows in 3:6 – 4:4. The people are urged to return, but to do so thoroughly and genuinely. During this period of Jeremiah’s ministry, there is still the possibility of return and repentance: if the people will leave idolatry and adultery behind and return to their God, he promises to take them back.
