
D. Concluding Doxology (11:33–36)
V. 	God’s Righteousness to Israel and the Gentiles (9:1–11:36)
   	A. 	God’s Saving Promise to Israel (9:1–29)
   	B. 	Israel’s Rejection of God’s Saving Righteousness (9:30–11:10)
   	C. 	God’s Righteousness in His Plan for Jews and Gentiles (11:11–32)
   	➤ D. 	Concluding Doxology (11:33–36)

The depth of God’s saving plan, which reveals his saving righteousness, moves Paul to express his wonder in doxological language. The poetic cast of these verses is apparent. Wisdom and knowledge are ascribed to God. Two exclamations emerge in verse 33. Three questions are asked in verses 34–35, with the last question comprising two lines. The reason for the greatness of God is given with the three prepositional phrases in verse 36, and an acclamation of God’s glory concludes the verse.1 The structure is represented below. Verses 34–35 ground all of verse 33, and verse 36 grounds both verses 34 and 35.

The depth of the riches of both God’s wisdom and knowledge is exclaimed (33a).

His judgments are unsearchable (33b).

His ways are untraceable (33c).

For no one has known the mind of the Lord (34a),

and no one has become his counselor (34b).

And no one has ever given to the Lord in advance so that God would repay him (35).

Because all things are from him (36a)

and through him (36b)

and to him (36c).

And therefore all the glory belongs to him forever. Amen (36d).

Exegesis and Exposition

[11:33]

The language and concepts in this text reflect the OT, Hellenistic Judaism, and to some extent even Stoicism. The Stoic language is probably mediated from Hellenistic Judaism and does not indicate the acceptance of a Stoic worldview on Paul’s part (see below; cf. Cranfield 1979: 589). Despite the conceptual links with other traditions, most scholars affirm the Pauline origin of these verses.2 They reflect too many Pauline themes to be attributed to another source, and they also fit remarkably well into the context of Rom. 9–11. Some scholars have suggested that these verses function as the conclusion to 1:16–11:32 (e.g., Fitzmyer 1993c: 633). More likely they conclude Rom. 9–11, in particular 11:32, and constitute Paul’s response to the wisdom of God in arranging salvation history to maximize his glory by dispensing mercy at the appointed times to Jews and gentiles (Murray 1965: 104–5).

Most scholars understand the first καί (kai, and) in verse 33 to be continuative, with the result that the three genitives πλούτου (ploutou, riches), σοφίας (sophias, wisdom), and γνώσεως (gnōseōs, knowledge) modify the word βάθος (bathos, depth).3 According to this view Paul exclaims over the depth of God’s riches, the depth of his wisdom, and the depth of his knowledge. I think it is more likely, though one can hardly be certain, that the phrase βάθος πλούτου belongs together and that the words σοφίας and γνώσεως modify βάθος πλούτου (cf. Murray 1965: 105–6). In this construction the first καί should be rendered “both,” and the “depth of the riches, both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God” are trumpeted. This latter interpretation should be favored because the phrase “depth of the riches” is an emphatic way to communicate the immeasurable extent of the ascribed qualities to God. The two words together convey the greatness of his wisdom and knowledge. To separate the word πλούτου (riches) so that it functions in a way similar to σοφίας (wisdom) and γνώσεως (knowledge) would mean that riches, wisdom, and knowledge are parallel. It is difficult to identify, however, what the word “riches” refers to in this scheme.4 Paul never uses the term to denote God’s saving riches without a defining genitive—unless one were to argue for such a construction here.5 The depth of God’s wisdom and knowledge is clearly set forth, but what does it mean to speak of the “depth of God’s riches”? What does “riches” refer to? It hardly seems parallel to the attributes of “wisdom” and “knowledge” that follow. But to speak of the “depth of the riches, both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God” is eminently clear. Paul extols the infinite wisdom and knowledge of God.

Some scholars have sharply distinguished the terms “wisdom” and “knowledge.” Indeed, some have even argued that the words “riches,” “wisdom,” and “knowledge” are specifically elaborated in subsequent verses in reverse order.6 According to this view, God’s “knowledge” is reflected in the sentence “Who has known the mind of the Lord?” and his wisdom in “Or who has become his counselor?” and his riches in “Or who has given to him beforehand so that it will be repaid to him?” The parallels adduced here are forced (rightly Cranfield 1979: 590). I have already suggested that it is a mistake to take πλούτου (riches) as a separate attribute of God, and this point calls into question the whole schema. In addition, the alleged structure does not begin immediately after mentioning God’s riches, wisdom, and knowledge. Two lines intervene in verse 33 before the so-called parallels commence in verse 34, and the intervention of these lines renders questionable the notion that verses 34–35 unpack the first part of verse 33. Finally, the attempt to differentiate between “wisdom” and “knowledge” is itself flawed.7 The two terms overlap significantly and should not be sharply distinguished. In verse 33a Paul praises the infinite depth of God’s wisdom and knowledge. Θεοῦ is a subjective genitive rather than an objective genitive (Fitzmyer 1993c: 634; Stuhlmacher 1994: 176). The reference is not to our knowledge of God but to God’s infinite knowledge. One should also observe that God’s knowledge does not merely mean that he foresees all that will occur, although it certainly includes that idea. The notion that God ordains all that comes to pass is also entailed, so that “the knowledge of God” refers to his determining of all that happens (cf. Ps. 94:11; 1 Cor. 3:20; 13:12; Gal. 4:9; cf. Cranfield 1979: 589–90; Dunn 1988b: 699). I should emphasize, however, that the riches of God’s wisdom and knowledge focus on his wisdom and knowledge in terms of his saving plan.8 In Rom. 9–11 Paul has just explained the substance of God’s plan relative to Jews and gentiles. Now he steps back and surveys what he has said and exclaims that God’s saving plan is full of wisdom and knowledge.9

Verse 33b proceeds in the same vein, proclaiming the inaccessibility of God’s judgments and ways to human beings. This inaccessibility is communicated through the terms ἀνεξεραύνητα (anexeraunēta, unsearchable) and ἀνεξιχνίαστοι (anexichniastoi, untraceable). Significantly, Paul uses ἀνεξιχνίαστοι (untraceable) in Eph. 3:8, where God’s saving plan and its wisdom are unfolded so that the readers will comprehend the wisdom of God. The parallel to Rom. 11 is obvious, since here also Paul exclaims about the wisdom of God’s plan in salvation history. The term ἀνεξιχνίαστοι is also used in the LXX to designate God’s greatness (Job 5:9; 9:10; 34:24). The words “unsearchable” and “untraceable” are overlapping synonyms, together designating human inaccessibility to God’s ways in the world. Similarly, the terms κρίματα (krimata, judgments) and ὁδοί (hodoi, ways) overlap, referring to the activity of God in the world. The κρίματα (judgments) should not be reserved only for God’s judicial decisions, although it certainly includes them, but it should also be extended to include all the “decisions” God executes in the world (1 Clem. 60.1; Sanday and Headlam 1902: 340; Cranfield 1979: 590; Fitzmyer 1993c: 634). One should not think of God’s judgments in the transcendent realm but of his judgments and actions in history (cf. Bornkamm 1969: 106; Wilckens 1980: 270). The word ὁδοί (ways) also refers to God’s action in the world, comprehending both his saving and judging actions (in the LXX: cf. Job 26:14; Pss. 9:26; 76:20; 94:10 [10:5; 77:19; 95:10 Eng.]; Isa. 55:8–9; cf. also Rev. 15:3; see also Murray 1965: 106; Cranfield 1979: 590).

Of course, human beings perceive the course of history and events as they occur. Paul would hardly deny that we see events as they take place. The point is that the mere observation of these events does not translate into an understanding of what God is doing in history. Human beings see the bare events as they transpire, but they do not perceive the saving plan of God that is being accomplished in and through these events. To us, the events of history may simply be “sound and fury, signifying nothing,”10 since on our own we are unable to perceive God’s wise plan for history. To perceive the meaning of the events in history, we need God’s interpretive binoculars, which will enable us to see aright what he is up to. The logic of the verse progresses from God’s wisdom and knowledge to his activity in the world. God’s wisdom is infinite and immeasurable, and this wisdom is expressed in the way he guides and superintends the history of the world. Here Paul is thinking not abstractly but concretely of God’s wisdom and activity with regard to Jews and gentiles. In his wisdom and knowledge he has planned history so that his judgments and ways would be effected in the lives of both Jews and gentiles. He has imprisoned all in disobedience to lavish his mercy on all.
[11:34]

Verses 34–35 function as the ground (γάρ, gar, for) of verse 33.11 God’s wisdom and ways are inaccessible to human beings, apart from revelation, because we are limited in wisdom and strength. The first proof stems from Isa. 40:13, and here Paul cites the LXX with minor changes. With a rhetorical question Isaiah asks whether anyone knows the mind of the Lord or has become his counselor. The answer is obvious. No finite human being has enough wisdom to discern God’s mind or to give him counsel on how he should run the world. We cannot grasp the mind of the Lord, and thus it follows that we cannot instruct him on the best course in any situation. The OT context of the citation is important. In Isa. 40 the second exodus from Babylon is promised, but Israel is filled with doubts and fears because they are so weak and Babylon is so strong. Yahweh assures Israel that he can accomplish his saving plan because all the nations are as nothing before him, a mere drop in the bucket or a speck on his scales.

The thematic connection to Rom. 9–11 should not be missed. Just as Yahweh promised to save Israel when such deliverance seemed impossible and they had virtually given up, so too he has planned history in such a way that he fulfills the covenant promises made in Isaiah in an unexpected way. He has extended salvation to uncircumcised gentiles and at the end of history will again fold in unbelieving Jews. Does the inclusion of Israel again seem incredible? It is no more incredible than the pledge to rescue Israel from the dominion of Babylon. God effects salvation for the weak so that the glory of his strength is impressed upon all. Captive Israel in Babylon did not perceive the mind of the Lord, that it was his plan to rescue them from their plight; similarly no human being could anticipate the wisdom of God’s plan by which he has arranged history to bring about the salvation of both Jews and gentiles in a most improbable way.

Paul also cites Isa. 40:13 in 1 Cor. 2:16, where he claims that believers have the mind of Christ. On first glance this assertion seems to contradict Rom. 11:34, but the two notions are compatible, for the main thesis of 1 Cor. 2:6–16 is that no one can know the mind and thoughts of God’s Spirit apart from God’s free and gracious revelation. Apart from the revelation of the Holy Spirit (1 Cor. 2:9–10), no human being knows what God has prepared for those who love him, but the Spirit has granted that revelation to believers. The theme in Rom. 11 is remarkably similar. No human being has the wisdom or knowledge to discern (much less to advise) God on the course that human history should take. His wisdom and plan are inaccessible to us. Nonetheless, the point of this section is ultimately not (against Fitzmyer 1993c: 632–33) that God’s plan is a mystery to us and beyond our comprehension. That observation is true insofar as it goes. Human beings cannot understand God’s mind or plan, since our capacities are restricted. But Paul goes on to say that this inaccessible wisdom of God has been revealed to us, even though we are still unable to plumb the depths of it (Murray 1965: 104; Dahl 1977: 157). Human beings cannot discern God’s wise plan for history on their own, nor would they ever devise a scheme like God’s. Nonetheless, in Rom. 9–11 Paul has communicated the main strokes in that plan so that believers can discern the wisdom of God as it unfolds. This is not to say that comprehensive and exhaustive knowledge is granted to believers, only that the chief lineaments of his plan are made known to them. Romans 11:34 is therefore remarkably similar in theme to 1 Cor. 2:16: human beings cannot know God’s wisdom unaided, but they can access it as the Holy Spirit reveals it.
[11:35]

The second OT proof for the thesis of verse 33 is found in verse 35, which cites Job 41:11 (41:3 MT, LXX). The citation is radically different from the LXX, but it is quite close to the MT, and thus the Greek text represents Paul’s own free translation of the MT or reflects another Greek translation that was current in his day. A literal translation of the MT is “Who has anticipated me that I should repay him? For everything in the heavens belongs to me.” Paul cites only the first part of the verse, but his translation is clearly closer to the MT than the LXX, which reads, “Who shall resist me and endure?” The idea of anticipating God or giving to him in advance is communicated with the word προέδωκεν (proedōken, he gave in advance), and the repayment is conveyed with the term ἀνταποδοθήσεται (antapodothēsetai, it will be repaid). The meaning of the rhetorical question is not difficult: no one has first given to God; hence no one deserves repayment. This assertion is lodged in a context stressing that God’s wisdom and knowledge are inaccessible to humans. No one has access to the requisite wisdom to counsel God about the course of history; hence no one can expect a reward for their wise counsel. God and God alone has determined the course of history in his own wisdom, and we become aware of his plan only to the extent that he reveals it to us.

Once again the larger OT context of the citation is significant. One of Job’s major complaints during his suffering was that God is unjust. This leads Job to doubt God’s wisdom. In Job 38–41 God reveals himself to Job and rebukes him for questioning God’s justice and mode of operation in the world. Job is too limited and finite to superintend the world and all that is in it. In his soliloquy God reveals to Job that as the Creator he indeed has the capacity to superintend the world he has established. Job responds to God’s speech (Job 42:3) by admitting that he has transgressed beyond his sphere, that his knowledge is too limited, and that no one can thwart God’s plan (42:2). Just as Job doubted God’s wisdom and ability in his suffering, so too the Roman Christians might be inclined to question God’s wisdom in terms of his saving plan for world history. Job’s vision of God’s greatness was too circumscribed. God accomplished his plan with respect to Job in wisdom and justice; so too Paul declares that God’s plan to save some Jews and gentiles is wise and just. God is debtor to no one’s wisdom, strength, or goodness, and he accomplishes his purposes by his own initiative.
[11:36]

The ὅτι (hoti, because) introducing verse 36 reaches back to both verses 34 and 35. Why is it that no one can function as God’s counselor (v. 34) or give to him so that repayment is warranted (v. 35)? The reason is that “from him and through him and to him are all things” (ἐξ αὐτοῦ καὶ δι᾽ αὐτοῦ καὶ εἰς αὐτὸν τὰ πάντα, ex autou kai di’ autou kai eis auton ta panta). It has often been pointed out that the formulation here is akin to Stoicism (Seneca, Epistle 65 §8; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4 §23). But the parallels are superficial, since such formulations must be interpreted in terms of the worldview of the author, and Stoicism and Pauline thought are quite different (rightly C. Barrett 1991: 211; Bornkamm 1969: 108; Cranfield 1979: 591). For instance, Stoicism is pantheistic, which is a far cry from the personal theism of Paul. Philo also has expressions similar to Paul’s (Cherubim 35 §§125–26; Spec. Laws 1.38 §208), but once again they are integrated into his theistic framework. Philo’s use of comparable expressions may indicate that this formulation was mediated to Paul via Hellenistic Judaism (cf. also 1 Cor. 8:6; Col. 1:16–17). In any case, the theology articulated here is in accord with the OT and Jewish tradition.12 God is the source of all things, the means by which all things are accomplished, and the goal of all things.13 Since he is the source and means of all things, no one could possibly function as his counselor or expect payment for some service rendered. God is the giver of wisdom to human beings, not the recipient of wisdom from them; God is the one who gives all things to us, not the one who receives benefits from human hands. Not only is God the source of all things and the means by which all things are accomplished, but he is also the goal (εἰς) of all things. The purpose for which the world was created is God’s purpose. It is fitting, therefore, that the text ends with an acclamation of God’s glory.14 The one from whom and through whom and to whom are all things deserves all the glory.15 The goal of Romans emerges clearly at the end of the discussion on the relationship between Jews and gentiles in salvation history. The salvation of Jews and gentiles is penultimate. What is ultimate is the glory of God. As Schlatter (1995: 226) says, “Worship is the concluding word.” God has arranged redemptive history to bring the maximum glory to himself. He has arranged it so that it is clear that all things are from him, through him, and to him. The “Amen” (ἀμήν, amēn) in the text indicates Paul’s intense wish that God’s purpose to receive glory and praise will be realized.16
